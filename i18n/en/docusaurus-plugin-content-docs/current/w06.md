# A Literature Class

*1999*

Back then, "laid off" (*xiagang*) was the single most terrifying phrase in our vocabulary. The main bulletin board at the factory turned into a minefield. Every day, people would pretend to just be strolling past, only to subconsciously slow down, their eyes nervously darting across the edges of the red paper, terrified of seeing their own name printed there.

I was clinging to a dead-end desk job in the supply and marketing department, my heart constantly in my throat. It wasn't until a rumor started sweeping through the office—"a college degree is a protective amulet"—that I finally woke up and enrolled in a local night school.

The beautiful thing about night school is that nobody asks why you're there. They only care if your check clears. I majored in Chinese Language and Literature, not out of any burning artistic passion, but because literature is the most forgiving of subjects. It doesn't demand that you decipher physics or chemistry. If you can read, you're in. Literature can't put food on the table, but at the very least, it makes the struggle of trying to survive feel slightly less miserable.

Our very first course was "Modern Western Literature," taught by Professor Sun. In his younger years, he had been a die-hard specialist in the history of the proletarian revolution, devoting his life's work to tearing down capitalism. But after the Cold War ended, nearing sixty years old, he found himself with no choice but to stand at the podium of a dingy night school, lecturing about Kafka and Sartre to a room full of exhausted adults who were only there to secure their rice bowls.

"Gregor turned into a beetle," he said, lightly patting his lecture notes. "This is a profound illustration of alienation."

Right next to the podium, his travel bag sat unzipped. Poking out were the spines of a stack of brand-new books—*Existentialism and the Modern Soul*, authored by none other than Professor Sun himself. Right before class ended, he would casually lean on the desk and remind us: "The discussion of a certain issue in a specific chapter of my book will be a key focal point for the final exam."

His words were restrained, but the message was crystal clear. Understanding the unspoken rule, everyone lined up to pay for a copy.

Since knowledge could be smoothly bought with cash, the concept of attendance naturally lost all meaning. The logic of the night school was devastatingly simple: leave your money on the desk, and you can go wherever the hell you want. I quickly mastered the rules of the game and started skipping classes on a regular basis.

As the final exam approached, our class monitor began quietly collecting money, claiming it was a "token of appreciation" for the teacher. Nominally, it was framed as respecting our elders, but everyone knew it was just buying peace of mind. We all understood, and we all paid up without a fuss.

On the day of the exam, the classroom was brightly lit, but the atmosphere carried a deliberately manufactured seriousness. The proctor symbolically walked two laps up and down the aisles, then settled comfortably into a chair beside the podium, opened a magazine, and didn't look up again. The rest of the time was entirely ours.

The examinees immediately unleashed their unique survival skills. Some had photocopied the textbook, shrinking the pages down to tiny slips of paper they hid in their sleeves. Others had answers meticulously scrawled across their palms in blue ink. Like a ragtag crew of underground operatives, we used the crudest, most desperate methods to participate in an activity we believed to be elegant.

There was a young guy sitting to my left who I didn't recognize. He was wearing a sharply tailored trench coat, holding an expensive Parker pen, and looking completely relaxed. He clearly hadn't studied for a second; his test paper was entirely blank. Driven by some bottom-rung, working-class instinct for mutual survival, I subtly slid my answer sheet toward the edge of my desk so he could copy it. 

He glanced over and whispered, "No need. Thanks."

Out of politeness, he chatted with me briefly after the exam. He casually mentioned that his father had just spoken with the director of the school board a few days ago, discussing "the guiding significance of literature and art in the construction of corporate culture." The entire curriculum of these night courses had actually been planned with his father's direct involvement. He was only sitting in the classroom today so that his test paper would at least have some handwriting on it.

It was right then that I realized we had never actually been classmates. Sitting there gripping my cheap ballpoint pen, a wave of profound, ridiculous absurdity washed over me. I genuinely believed that with a little street smarts, I could game the bureaucracy of the education system. I didn't understand until that exact moment that the people who were truly qualified to game the rules never had to set foot in the classroom to begin with.
