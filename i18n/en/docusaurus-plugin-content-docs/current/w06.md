# A Literature Class

  *1999*

During those years, "laid off" (xiagang) became the most frequently mentioned word. The factory's bulletin board became a danger zone. Every day, someone would pretend to pass by, then unconsciously slow down their pace, their eyes wandering around the edges of the red paper, deeply afraid of seeing their own name.

I kept to my neglected cold bench in the supply and marketing department, my heart already hanging in mid-air. It wasn't until the rumor that "an educational degree is an amulet" spread throughout the office that I woke up as if from a dream and stepped through the gates of the night school.

The good thing about night school is that it doesn't ask why you came; it only confirms whether you have paid the tuition. I chose to major in Chinese Language and Literature, not out of love, but because literature is the most forgiving to people: it doesn't require incomprehensible math, physics, or chemistry. As long as you can read, you can be accepted. Literature certainly cannot solve the problem of making a living, but at least it can make making a living seem less wretched.

However, the first course was "Modern Western Literature," lectured by Professor Sun. In his early years, he specialized in the history of the proletarian revolution and devoted his life to criticizing capitalism. After the end of the Cold War, nearing the age of sixty, he had no choice but to stand on the podium of a night school and teach Kafka and Sartre to a group of adults who came here for their rice bowls.

"Gregor turned into a beetle," he patted his lecture notes and said, "this is a profound kind of alienation."

Next to the podium, his travel bag was open, revealing the spines of several new books—"Existentialism and the Modern Soul," whose author was none other than himself. Before the class ended, he would always casually remind everyone: the discussion of a certain issue in a certain chapter of the book is a key point for the final exam.

His words were restrained but also clear. So everyone understood tacitly and lined up to pay.

Since knowledge could be settled with cash, the attendance rate naturally lost its meaning. The logic of the night school was very simple: leave the money, and the people can go wherever they want. I quickly mastered this set of rules and started skipping classes from time to time.

As the final exam approached, the class monitor started collecting money privately, saying it was a token of appreciation for the teacher. Nominally it was to respect the elderly, but in reality, it was buying peace. Everyone understood, and everyone cooperated.

On the day of the exam, the classroom was brightly lit, but the atmosphere carried a deliberately maintained solemnity. The invigilator symbolically walked two laps in the aisle, then settled down beside the podium and opened a magazine. The remaining time was handed over to us to handle ourselves.

The examinees each showed their own special prowess. Some had textbooks reduced and printed on slips of paper; others had answers written all over their palms. Like a group of temporarily assembled underground workers, we used the crudest methods to engage in an action we thought was elegant.

Sitting to my left was an unfamiliar young man. He wore a well-tailored trench coat, held a Parker fountain pen, and looked leisurely. He clearly wasn't prepared, and his test paper was completely blank. Out of a bottom-rung mutual assistance instinct, I moved my answer sheet a little toward him.

He glanced at me and whispered: "No need, thanks."

Out of politeness, he chatted with me for a few sentences after the exam. He said his father had just talked with the director of the school office a few days ago about the topic of "the guiding significance of literature and art to the construction of corporate culture." The setup of these courses was all planned with his father's participation. He was sitting here just to make this test paper look like it had been answered by someone.

It was only at that moment that I realized we had never been classmates from the very beginning. Gripping that cheap ballpoint pen in my hand, I suddenly felt an exceptionally clear absurdity: I thought that by relying on a little bit of cleverness, I could tease the formalism of education; but I didn't understand until now that those who are truly qualified to tease the rules never had to enter the classroom at all.
