# A Literature Class

*1999*

During those years, "laid off" (*xiagang*) became the most frequently spoken word in our lives. The factory's main bulletin board morphed into a danger zone. Every single day, someone would pretend to just be passing by, only to unconsciously slow their pace, their eyes nervously scanning the edges of the red paper, terrified of seeing their own name printed there.

I clung to my neglected, cold bench in the supply and marketing department, my heart already suspended in mid-air. It wasn't until a rumor began circulating through the office—"a degree is a protective amulet"—that I woke up as if from a dream and finally stepped through the gates of the local night school.

The good thing about night school is that it never asks why you came; it only cares whether you've paid the tuition. I chose to major in Chinese Language and Literature, not out of any burning passion for the arts, but because literature is the most forgiving of subjects. It doesn't demand a mastery of incomprehensible math, physics, or chemistry. As long as you can read, you're accepted. Literature certainly cannot solve the practical problem of making a living, but at the very least, it makes the struggle of making a living feel slightly less wretched.

Our very first course was "Modern Western Literature," taught by Professor Sun. In his earlier years, he had specialized in the history of the proletarian revolution, devoting his life's work to critiquing capitalism. But after the Cold War ended, nearing the age of sixty, he found himself with no choice but to stand on the podium of a night school, lecturing about Kafka and Sartre to a room full of exhausted adults who were only there to secure their rice bowls.

"Gregor turned into a beetle," he said, lightly patting his lecture notes, "this is a profound illustration of alienation."

Right next to the podium, his travel bag sat unzipped, revealing the spines of a stack of new books—*Existentialism and the Modern Soul*, authored by none other than Professor Sun himself. Right before class ended, he would always casually remind us: "The discussion of a certain issue in a specific chapter of my book will be a key focal point for the final exam."

His words were restrained, but the message was crystal clear. So, understanding the unspoken rule, everyone lined up to pay for a copy.

Since knowledge could be smoothly settled with cash, the concept of an attendance rate naturally lost all meaning. The logic of the night school was devastatingly simple: leave your money on the desk, and you can go wherever you please. I quickly mastered this set of rules and started skipping classes on a regular basis.

As the final exam approached, our class monitor began privately collecting money, claiming it was a "token of appreciation" for the teacher. Nominally, it was framed as respecting our elders, but in reality, it was simply buying peace of mind. Everyone understood, and everyone cooperated without a fuss.

On the day of the exam, the classroom was brightly lit, but the atmosphere carried a deliberately manufactured solemnity. The invigilator symbolically walked two laps up and down the aisles, then settled comfortably into a chair beside the podium, opened a magazine, and didn't look up again. The rest of the time was entirely ours to manage.

The examinees immediately showcased their unique talents. Some had photocopied the textbook, shrinking the pages down to tiny slips of paper; others had answers meticulously scrawled across their palms. Like a temporarily assembled crew of underground operatives, we used the crudest, most desperate methods to participate in an activity we believed to be elegant.

Sitting to my left was an unfamiliar young man. He wore a sharply tailored trench coat, held an expensive Parker fountain pen, and looked completely at ease. He clearly hadn't prepared a single thing; his test paper was entirely blank. Driven by a bottom-rung instinct for mutual assistance, I subtly nudged my answer sheet toward him.

He glanced at me and whispered, "No need. Thanks."

Out of politeness, he chatted with me briefly after the exam. He casually mentioned that his father had just spoken with the director of the school office a few days prior, discussing "the guiding significance of literature and art in the construction of corporate culture." The entire curriculum of these courses had actually been planned with his father's direct participation. He was only sitting there so that his test paper would at least have some handwriting on it.

It was only at that moment that I realized we had never been classmates to begin with. Gripping my cheap ballpoint pen, I suddenly felt a wave of exceptionally clear absurdity wash over me. I had genuinely believed that by relying on a bit of street smarts, I could game the formalism of the education system. I hadn't understood until that very second that the people truly qualified to game the rules never had to set foot in the classroom at all.
