# Moth

*2006*

## Dormancy

The summer of 2000. The millennium's champagne had barely gone flat and the air still hummed with restless promise. I was twenty-two years old, clutching a diploma from Southeast University's Department of Radio Engineering, everything I owned stuffed into a backpack held together by duct tape and stubbornness. I caught the last bus heading southwest and rode it deep into the mountains until the road ran out.

The Qixi Space Center didn't appear on any civilian map—just a set of coordinates that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't been cleared to know better. But to those of us in the trade, it was Mecca. The base was buried under layers of primeval forest, cloaked year-round in mist and low-hanging cloud. On clear mornings, when the mountain wind swept the fog from the valley floor, the command tower would emerge above the canopy like a silver blade thrust into the sky. From a distance, the whole complex looked like a city floating in the clouds—something alien, something that didn't belong to the world below.

They put me in the Fourth Research Office. Quantum comms. We were chasing unbreakable encryption through photon entanglement, trying to build a communication system that no adversary could ever tap. My world shrank to the green glow of oscilloscopes, the perpetual drone of the server room, and a single classified objective: make interstellar communication unhackable.

It was a lunatic era.

The superpower across the Pacific had just unveiled its "Mars Colonization Plan" with maximum fanfare—Olympus Mons, they declared, would fly the Western Empire's flag within a decade. The announcement landed like a depth charge. Within weeks, Qixi launched its counterpunch: a classified grand initiative codenamed "Pillar of Heaven." The goal was simple and audacious—beat them there. Build the first human outpost on Mars before anyone else could plant a boot print.

The base went into overdrive. Test-fire roars shook the valley almost daily, and the launch manifest grew dense enough to rival a circuit board schematic. The frantic expansion created a critical shortage of qualified astronauts—especially the kind who could crunch data sets and troubleshoot hardware in the field. People like that were rarer than hen's teeth.

That was 2002. To plug the gap, the center took an unprecedented step: they opened the astronaut selection pipeline to internal researchers. Everyone joked it was a fire sale on standards. I knew better. This was my shot—maybe my only one. I leaned on every advantage I had: my expertise in radio and quantum systems, and the cardiovascular endurance I'd built chasing a soccer ball through four years of college. I clawed through the screening rounds like a salmon fighting upstream, and somehow came out the other side.

The evening they made my transfer official, I stood beneath the training center's great glass dome, staring up at the star chart inlaid in the skylight. Elation and dread in equal measure. Beyond the glass, the sunset was turning the western sky to molten copper, the mountains dissolving into silhouette. It felt like a door opening onto the rest of my life.

Fate, of course, has a gift for slamming doors.

I'd been in the Astronaut Brigade less than six months when a global financial tsunami hit without warning. Wall Street collapsed, dominoes fell, energy prices spiked, unemployment exploded. Desperate governments reached for the budget axe, and aerospace—that gleaming beacon of humanity's tomorrow—was the first thing on the block. Winter came early to Qixi. Every deep-space exploration program was frozen overnight. Pillar of Heaven was shelved indefinitely. The launch pads went silent. Weeds pushed up through cracks in the tarmac and wound their way around rusting gantries. Even daily training took a hit; the cafeteria menu thinned out week by week.

People started leaving. The well-connected finagled transfers to cushy administrative posts; others swallowed their pride and took jobs at telecom companies on the coast—one of the few sectors still hiring. The farewell dinners blurred together, toasts turning bitter, old dreams dissolving in cheap baijiu.

And then there was me—the shell left on the beach after the tide went out. My old R&D lab was overstaffed and underbudgeted, no room for a boomerang. But I couldn't bring myself to leave Qixi. So I became that saddest of creatures: a "left-behind astronaut," collecting a pittance of an allowance, rattling around a forgotten mountain base with nothing to do.

During those years, I developed a ritual. I'd go to the empty training center after dark, seal myself inside one of the cold simulators, and just sit there staring at the virtual star field on the screen. Mars hung below the constellations, its surface flickering like a distant ember, and I could have sworn it was calling to me. I knew what I was: a moth in the darkness, drawn to a flame the world had snuffed out. But the ember hadn't died. It was only buried. I was waiting for the ash to catch.


## Cocoon

Early 2006. The Qixi Space Center stirred from the dead.

It happened on a freezing morning after PT, our noses raw and red. The instructor clasped his hands behind his back, swept his gaze across our ragged lineup of idle men, and dropped the news: the center would urgently recruit three "special operations team members" for a new space mission—the first in four years.

He kept the details vague, but none of us were stupid. This had to be a revival of Pillar of Heaven—the Mars outpost, the holy grail. Every astronaut's ultimate dream.

The word "dismissed" had barely left his lips before the yard erupted. We jogged back to the barracks in a pack, talking over each other.

"What a load of crap." Guofeng flung his cap onto his bunk and dropped onto the squealing iron frame, arms folded. "Like they'd ever pick one of us. It's already been decided. Chen Weimin and his golden boys have probably been rehearsing their victory speeches for weeks. We're wallpaper. Extras."

Xinyu toweled off his neck and nodded. "Three slots. I heard from Fifth Squad that the bar is set sky-high—tailor-made for a very specific set of résumés."

Wenhua, quiet as always, sighed and went on folding his blanket with monastic precision. "Can't hurt. Even a participation trophy looks good on paper for guys like us."

They were right, of course. Three years on the margins will cure anyone of delusions of grandeur. But somewhere beneath the thick scar tissue of low expectations, something in me wouldn't lie still. The version of me that stared at simulated constellations every night, the kid who'd once wallpapered his bedroom with crayon rockets—that version was pounding on the walls. Even if I was only going to pace the sidelines, I wanted to be on the field.

"So," I said carefully. "Are we signing up?"

Guofeng rolled his eyes and pulled the registration form from his pocket. "Obviously. You miss every shot you don't take."

Xinyu and Wenhua grinned and nodded.


Registration was the easy part. Then came three days of hell.

The tests were designed to find every human breaking point and lean on it. Day one: the centrifuge, cranked to 8G. I remember the sensation with crystalline clarity—eyeballs crushed backward into the skull, vision collapsing to a narrow black tunnel, lungs flat as cardboard, every breath like gargling broken glass.

They'd barely peeled us out of the centrifuge—I hadn't even finished retching—when they shoved us into the hypobaric chamber.

Air pressure plunged to a third of sea level. Hypoxia crept in fast, tingling in my fingertips, turning my brain into an overheating computer running on fumes. I couldn't stop. Around me, the ex-fighter-jocks grimaced but kept hitting their marks. I had no flight training to fall back on, just raw stubbornness.

"Ruan Qizhen, heart rate elevated. Abort?" Cold voice in the earpiece.

"Negative." I bit through my lip. The pain helped.

Day two was the isolation tank. They locked each of us alone in a pitch-black simulator pod—no windows, no food, no water—for twenty-four hours straight, running us through a gauntlet of cognitive tasks while sleep deprivation and claustrophobia did their work. The darkness pressed in like something physical, probing for cracks. I held myself together through sheer clenched will, but just barely. My psych evaluation came back marked "marginally adequate." I walked out drenched in cold sweat, legs like rubber.

Day three: a comprehensive battery—written exams, spacecraft piloting sims, emergency fault-handling drills, wilderness survival, geological survey—all after more than twenty-four hours without sleep. My head swam. My limbs felt like they belonged to someone else. I stumbled through most of it and only clawed back some dignity on the technical theory paper, where code and equations were the only language that mattered.

Back in the dorm, I collapsed and didn't move. Guofeng, Xinyu, and Wenhua were still animatedly debriefing their performance—all three were former fighter pilots, and for them the whole ordeal had been a brisk workout. I couldn't even lift a finger. I knew I was done. Against their physical prowess, my only currency was abstract knowledge, and the Astronaut Brigade had never put a premium on that.


Fate, it turns out, is also fond of playing the sweetest notes on broken strings.

Dawn. The bugle had barely sounded when the intercom crackled. Director Wang's voice—flat, colorless: "Ruan Qizhen. My office. Now."

My stomach dropped. I threw on my uniform and jogged across the compound, running scenarios in my head, all of them bad.

His office was dim, the desk buried under files. He looked up when I entered, and his gaze lingered a beat too long—the look of a mechanic sizing up a part that should have been junked but was the only one left on the shelf.

"Sir!"

"Sit." No preamble. He slid a document across the desk. "Ruan Qizhen. You've been selected."

My brain whited out. "Sir, you mean—selected for Pillar of Heaven Five?"

"Correct." His tone could have been reading a weather report. "Your physical scores are a liability, but your quantum communications background is mission-critical. There's equipment aboard that only you can service. Sign here and report to boot camp immediately."

I stared at the document in my trembling hands. The cover was stamped TOP SECRET in red. Inside, my name sat beside the mission designation: *Pillar of Heaven V — Mars Exploration.* I read it three times to make sure I wasn't hallucinating.

Joy hit like voltage—a current that surged from my feet to the crown of my skull and left me buzzing. I signed before the pen could shake itself out of my grip.

"Dismissed." Wang waved me off without a second glance. "Remember: classification protocols. No one learns the mission specifics—not your teammates, not your family."

I nearly sprinted out of the room, convinced the universe would change its mind if I lingered. In the corridor I almost flattened two passing officers, but they were too absorbed in their own business to notice the grin I couldn't wipe off my face.


Back at the dorm, I was shoving everything I owned into my duffel when Guofeng and the others walked in from PT.

"Qizhen." Guofeng's eyebrows climbed. "You running from the law?"

"Director Wang called me in," I said, fighting to keep my voice neutral. "They need me at the Xinjiang launch site—some kind of accident, the techs over there can't handle it. Probably gone a while."

The teasing drained from Guofeng's face. He walked over, frowning. "Xinjiang? I heard they blew a rocket a few days ago. Bad propellant leak. Watch yourself—that stuff'll kill you."

Guilt flickered through me. But the lie had to hold. "Relax, I'll be behind a desk reading data. And hey—no more dead weight at morning PT. Try not to miss me."

Wenhua leaned in, voice low. "Word is, the explosion was sabotage. Anti-government forces. It's not safe out there."

Something sharp and unfamiliar passed through Guofeng's eyes. "Anti-government forces," he echoed, his tone suddenly bitter. "Convenient scapegoat, isn't it? These parasites in charge will bleed this country dry before they're done." He caught himself, adjusted my backpack strap, and the hardness softened. "Just watch your back. When you get home, we're getting Old Liu's braised goose. My treat."

"Deal."

I shouldered the bag and took one last look around—four years of my life in this cramped concrete box, these three men who'd become brothers. *When I come back from Mars*, I told myself, *I'll slam the medals on this table and buy every bottle in the house.*

I walked out without looking back.


## Chrysalis

The boot camp gates loomed like the entrance to another world. I stood there breathing in the cold spring air, knowing that once I stepped through, the outside world would cease to exist. No calls, no letters—not for two years. Everything I'd been was about to be overwritten. From here on, the only direction that mattered was up and out—toward a red wasteland hundreds of millions of kilometers away.

Six astronauts for the mission, split into two squads of three. Groups A and B.

Group A was the A-list. Captain Chen Weimin—special-class hero, that granite jaw of his a permanent fixture on state media. His crew, Li Wei and Zhang Hao, were pedigreed to the gills: top-tier universities, decorated flight records, uniforms that seemed to fit them better than ours fit us. They moved through the base like thoroughbreds, aware of their own superiority without needing to advertise it.

Group B was... us. Our captain, Zhao Yongjun—"Old Zhao"—was a seasoned astronaut who'd been cooling his heels on the bench for years after making the mistake of speaking truth to the wrong superior. The rest of us were Ma Xiaomei—"Xiao Ma"—and me: a pair of technicians-turned-astronauts who'd never flown a real mission in our lives.

At first I wondered if we were just understudies.

But the training schedules told a different story. Group A drilled on command protocols, scientific research procedures, and heavy machinery ops—all from air-conditioned sim pods, pushing joysticks with practiced elegance. Group B got the grunt work: EVA construction in full suit, module assembly, repair drills, the kind of labor that left your back screaming and your gloves soaked with sweat. The two groups weren't interchangeable. They needed both to build the base. We weren't backups. We were the other half of the equation.


The first time I saw Pillar of Heaven V in full holographic projection—floating above the briefing room table like a ghost made of light—I forgot to breathe.

It was a fortress built to cross the void. Three components:

The core was *Spark*, the main vessel. A palace in miniature: closed-loop life support, spacious research modules, private crew quarters. Our home for fourteen months of deep space.

Docked at the bow was *Stowage*—a massive cargo pod packed to the rivets with prefab construction materials, mining equipment, and autonomous builder bots. Everything you'd need to raise a colony from Martian dust.

Slung beneath Spark's belly was *Stardust*, the landing shuttle. Where Spark was sleek and modern, Stardust was a relic—a design locked down decades ago, cramped and clunky, its instrument panel a museum piece of analog gauges and toggle switches.

"Why are we flying an antique?" Xiao Ma had groused during a break.

"Because it works," the instructor replied. "On Mars, simple machines don't break."

The powerplant was the crown jewel. Spark's heart was a QSL3 compact fusion reactor feeding a 200-megawatt Galaxy Pulse Mark II ion drive—a blue-burning engine that would carry us between worlds. But ion drives are patient beasts, not powerful ones; they can't punch through atmosphere. For that, the mission relied on the Storm III heavy-lift vehicle, which would haul the entire stack down Qixi's legendary twenty-kilometer maglev track, accelerating to Mach 3 before igniting its chemical stages and punching skyward.

The plan was elegant in its simplicity. Spark would park in Mars orbit. Stardust would ferry crews and cargo to the surface in multiple runs, like a worker bee serving the hive. Once the outpost was built, Stardust and Stowage would be abandoned, and all six of us would ride Spark home.

Fourteen months, start to finish.


Boot camp consumed the days whole. Groups A and B trained on the same compound but lived in separate worlds, divided by sim pods and security clearances. We'd nod at each other in the cafeteria. That was about it.

On the thirtieth day—our single rest day—we drew a special assignment: a CCTV film crew had been cleared into the facility.

It was my first brush with the propaganda machine. The host was Bingbing—the darling of national television, the woman who launched a thousand engineering-school crushes—but I couldn't summon any excitement. In the makeup room, a political officer had shoved a script into my hands. "Memorize this. Verbatim."

So I performed. Under the lights, I ceased to be Ruan Qizhen and became "Inspirational Hero #4." I looked into Bingbing's polished, professionally warm eyes and recited the fiction they'd written for me: childhood dreams of rocket ships, a homemade experiment that burned down the family kitchen, a beating from my father that only "fueled the fire of my aerospace ambitions." The beating, at least, was real.

They'd scripted our personalities, too. Chen Weimin: the Pillar. Me: the Eccentric Genius. Zhang Hao: the Fearless Warrior. We were action figures, freshly painted and posed on the shelf for public consumption.

When the cameras stopped, Bingbing shook my hand with a practiced smile. "Safe travels. By the time this airs, you'll be halfway to the Moon. The whole nation will be watching."

Her hand was very soft and very cold. I thought it suited her name.

I imagined watching the broadcast from orbit—pictured my parents pointing at the screen, my old neighbors calling each other over, everyone crowding around to see little Qizhen from down the street heading off to save the species.


## Emergence

Two months poured away like sand from a cracked hourglass. Launch day.

Dawn mist clung to the Qixi valley. The first sunlight cut through the cloud cover and hit the maglev track—twenty kilometers of silver rail climbing the spine of Xuanlong Ridge and disappearing into the sky like a stairway with no visible end.

Pillar of Heaven V sat at the base of the track, gleaming. Its alloy skin caught the early light and threw it back as something cold and holy—a temple from the future, waiting to receive its congregation. I stood at the boarding bridge in my pressure suit, heart hammering against my ribs. Up close, every rivet and weld seam was a love letter to precision engineering. I could have stared for hours.

I took a breath and walked toward Spark's airlock. A white-gloved hand stopped me.

"Ruan Qizhen. Hold."

Ground crew supervisor. Face like a blank wall. He gestured toward a narrow side passage beneath the gantry. "Change of plan. Group B rides in Stardust for the ascent."

"What? Since when? Why wasn't this—"

"Ballast rebalancing." Four syllables, no eye contact. "Orders."

I swallowed my questions and ducked into the passage. Stardust's interior was exactly as advertised: a sardine can with instrument panels. The air smelled of old sealant and recycled oxygen.


I was the last of our crew to board. Old Zhao sat by the porthole, buckling his harness, his brow furrowed deep enough to plant seeds in.

Xiao Ma was muttering over the control panel. She looked up when I squeezed through the hatch. "Can you believe this? Group A gets the penthouse and we get stuffed in the luggage compartment. Ballast rebalancing, my ass."

"Stow it," Old Zhao said quietly. "We're here. That's what matters." But his eyes stayed fixed on the porthole, watching the umbilical cables detach from Spark's hull, and his expression gave nothing away. "What we do on Mars is what counts. That's where we prove ourselves."

I wedged into the corner seat and pulled the harness tight. The walls pressed in from every side. Something unnamed was growing in my gut—not fear, exactly, but a slow, creeping wrongness, like weeds pushing through concrete.


0900 hours. The countdown hit zero.

No thunderous rocket ignition—just a deep, thrumming electrical whine and the rising hiss of air friction.

"Track charged. Electromagnetic catapult—engage!"

The acceleration hit like a wall. Not a shove—a compression, as if an invisible hand were trying to fold me into the seatback. Outside the window, the launch gantry blurred past. I caught a flash of support struts, then everything smeared into streaks of color.

Mach 1. Mach 2.

The vibrations climbed my spine and tried to exit through the top of my skull. Organs compressed. Breathing became a conscious, muscular act.

Under a minute in, a sharp metallic report—and suddenly the weight vanished. The craft had left the track, flung into the sky like a stone from a giant's sling. One heartbeat of stomach-dropping weightlessness.

Then the Storm III's main stage lit.

The sound wasn't sound anymore. It was a physical force, a detonation that swallowed every other sensation whole. G-forces piled on like a mountain falling sideways onto my chest. My vision tunneled to a dark pinpoint. We were astride something primordial and furious, a dragon of fire and thrust, hammering us into the black.

Ten minutes of sustained violence. Each second an epoch.

Then—a gentle lurch, and silence. Blessed, floating silence. The harness pressure evaporated. My body drifted upward against the restraints, weightless, as though my bones had turned to smoke.


Earth hung in the viewport, blue and white and heartbreakingly fragile against the void. Below us, the spent Storm III booster tumbled away, a dying ember spiraling back toward the planet's edge. Xiao Ma stared at it, eyes glistening. "Worth it," she whispered. "Whatever happens. This was worth it."

Spark's ion drive ignited—a spectral blue lance cutting the darkness. We were accelerating again, bound for Mars. The cabin tension eased by degrees. Cramped or not, we were in space.

Chen's voice crackled over the intercom, ordering Group B to remain in Stardust and await further instructions.

I ran a diagnostic on the comm systems and noticed the blinking red light beside the console—the redundant comms module interface. I'd helped design it. I knew about the maintenance backdoor no one had bothered to remove.

"Hey," I said, breaking the silence. "Anyone want to see what the press is saying about us? I can patch into the geostationary broadcast feed."

The rules were clear: Pillar of Heaven V's comms were locked to a quantum-encrypted military channel. No public protocols allowed—paranoia about cyberattacks and leaks. But the designers had hedged their bets. Buried in Stardust's systems was a one-way receiver for TV and radio signals, meant to activate only in crash-landing scenarios. It was supposed to be dormant. I knew how to wake it up.

A violation? Technically. But under the circumstances—shoved into a tin can, snubbed on live television—it felt less like insubordination and more like petty, justified revenge.

"Do it," Xiao Ma said, rubbing her palms together. "I want to see if my mom's crying."

Old Zhao studied the ceiling and said nothing. Permission granted.

I unbuckled, floated to the console, and got to work. Bypassed the security handshake, slaved the backup antenna to the Hongyin broadcasting satellite. Static resolved into a sharp picture: CCTV's live special, *Voyage: Humanity Returns to Mars.*

We pressed our faces to the screen.

The coverage was wall-to-wall. Every major network, every anchor desk, every crawling banner—Pillar of Heaven V, Pillar of Heaven V, Pillar of Heaven V. Once we'd achieved stable orbit, the news cycle shifted to human interest: profiles of the brave heroes aboard. Chen Weimin's portrait filled the screen, followed by Li Wei, then Zhang Hao. Biographies. Hobbies. Elementary school awards. A live feed from inside Spark, the crew saluting the camera, waving at the nation.

Stardust was never mentioned. Not once.

The broadcast cut to the studio anchor: "Let us salute these three extraordinary individuals! For the next year, they will represent all of humanity as they explore and build upon the red frontier—"

Three. The host said *three.* They even namedropped the construction robots. But not us. We didn't exist. We were ghosts—less real than machines, already erased from the story before it had begun.

"What—" I started. "Broadcast delay? Some kind of classification issue?"

"No." Xiao Ma's voice was tight. "Nobody ever told us we had different clearance levels. If Group A is public, why are we classified?"


Old Zhao went very still. Then his head snapped around, his tone suddenly steel: "Forget the broadcast."

He was already issuing orders. "Ruan—try pulling international feeds. See how the foreign press is covering this. Xiao Ma, you and I are going through every byte of mission data on this ship and cross-referencing it against our training specs. Everything. Now."

He was the most seasoned astronaut among us, the load-bearing wall of our small crew. Near retirement, still too stubborn to quit.

Xiao Ma jolted into motion, fingers flying across the terminal. "Stardust's mainframe mirrors all core mission files. I'll pull the public-facing propaganda docs first—whatever the official line is, that's where the cracks will show."

Minutes later, she'd found them: a set of unencrypted press materials, now glowing on the main display. The header read: **PILLAR OF HEAVEN V EXPLORATION MISSION — THREE-PERSON TEAM.** Below it, three headshots, three résumés, three carefully worded biographies. Group A. Complete.

Group B was absent. Not redacted, not classified—simply absent, as if we had never existed. As if we'd been deleted from history before we'd had a chance to make any.

The cabin went cold. The only sound was the life-support system's metronomic hum, and even that seemed to have taken on a sinister quality—less mechanical rhythm, more ticking clock.

"Captain." Xiao Ma's voice was barely a whisper. Beads of sweat had lifted from her forehead and were drifting slowly in the microgravity, tiny crystal spheres catching the panel light. "I ran the numbers three times. The consumables... they don't add up."

"Report."

"Total payload matches the simulation baseline. But Spark's actual carrying capacity, food stores, water recycling load capacity, oxygen regeneration panels—they're all significantly below training specs." She hesitated, as if saying it aloud would make it irreversible. "Real provisions max out at five crew for twelve months."

Nobody asked the obvious question.

The silence that followed had weight and texture. It pressed on the chest like deep water. The life-support hum filled the void, and it no longer sounded like machinery. It sounded like something counting down.


## Wings

The comm panel lit up without warning—indicator light strobing like a distress beacon—and shattered the silence. Xiao Ma glanced at the call code, and the color drained from her face. "Ground center. It's... it's Director Cai."

Cai had been Xiao Ma's old team leader. They were close—close enough that Xiao Ma's hands were trembling before she even opened the channel. But the voice that came through the speaker bore no resemblance to the composed, commanding woman we knew. It was ragged, breathless, barely held together.

"Xiao Ma—listen to me. As soon as I finish, kill the main comm link. I'm switching to Channel 23. There's a recording. Watch it. *Now.*"

"Channel 23?" Xiao Ma's hand stopped mid-reach.

Channel 23 was the nuclear option—a one-way hardened line so deeply embedded in the system architecture that not even Qixi's own IT division could tap it. Standing orders forbade its use outside of existential emergencies. Cai had helped design the original system; she was one of the only people alive with root-level physical access. It was, perhaps, the one loose thread the bureaucrats had forgotten to snip.

"This violates about fifteen protocols," Xiao Ma murmured. "Should we clear it with Captain Chen first?"

"Just open it," I snapped. "We might be flying to our own execution and you're worried about paperwork?"

She set her jaw and punched in the codes. A burst of static, then Director Cai's hologram materialized in the narrow space between our seats.

She looked destroyed. Her normally immaculate hair was in disarray, her eyes raw and red—whether from crying or from days without sleep was impossible to tell. But it was the look behind her eyes that turned my blood cold. Naked terror. The kind you see in someone who has glimpsed something they can never un-see.

"I'll be brief." Her voice cracked on every other word. "There is a top-level classified program. Codename: *Moth.* I only learned of it myself hours ago, and I—God, I don't know how to say this—"

"Two months before your launch, intelligence reached the top brass: the Western Empire intended to use the upcoming transfer window to accelerate their Mars colonization timeline. To seize the initiative, leadership ordered an immediate restart of the stalled Pillar of Heaven V mission."

"But there wasn't time." Cai pressed her palms against her face. "The outpost was designed for a six-person crew, but the ship was never upgraded. Development froze during the budget cuts. Spark's life support, fuel margins, return-trip mass budget—everything is specced for three. There is no physical way to bring six people home from Mars."

"They couldn't redesign the outpost. Couldn't refit Spark. Couldn't build a second ship. So they devised what they called an 'elegant solution.' Internally, they codenamed it *Moth.*"

She looked up, and her eyes held nothing but grief. "The plan was to recruit a second crew—people with low profiles, expendable credentials—and designate them Group B. Your group. You would handle the most dangerous phase of base construction. And then... there would be an accident."

"To ensure Group A had sufficient supplies for the return trip, Group B would need to quietly... cease to exist." Each word came out like it was being pulled from her with pliers. "As far as the world would ever know, this was always a three-person mission. A perfect, unblemished triumph."

Silence. Not the hum-of-machinery kind. The airless, absolute kind—the silence of a coffin lid closing.

Rage? Terror? No. What flooded my skull in that moment was something worse: a vast, absurd emptiness. We'd believed we were soldiers of the future, bearing humanity's torch into the dark. Turns out we were kindling. Disposable fuel, selected for our obscurity, meant to burn so that better men could wear the laurels.

Our names weren't even supposed to make it onto the monument.


"*Screw* your heroes!" The words ripped out of me. My fist hit the bulkhead hard enough to split skin. "What gives them the right? Since when are some lives worth less than others?!"

Despair is contagious. Xiao Ma was sobbing, curled in on herself, shoulders heaving.

"*Enough!*" Old Zhao was on his feet. This man—past fifty, built like gnarled oak—had a look in his eyes I'd never seen before: the cold, absolute focus of someone who has stared down death and is no longer impressed by it.

"Tears won't save us." His voice wasn't loud, but it cut like a blade. "They wrote us off as dead men? Fine. Then we live to spite them. We don't just survive—we rip their masks off and hold them up for the whole world to see."

He turned to me, and the weight of his gaze was physical. "Ruan. You've been our liaison with Group A. Get over to Spark. Tell Chen Weimin everything. I'll compile the data. Then we meet."

To Xiao Ma: "Back up every file and recording Cai just sent. Every byte. Then we organize what we have."

"But—" I hesitated. "Group A benefits from this. The broadcast made it pretty clear where they stand. What if they—"

"Chen Weimin is a soldier." Old Zhao's voice left zero room for doubt. "Arrogant, maybe. But not a coward, and not a murderer. I'd stake my life on it."


I requested hatch clearance, squeezed through the connecting tunnel, and floated into Spark's bright interior. It was smaller than the simulator had suggested—barely room for three operator stations—but compared to our sardine can, it felt like a cathedral.

Li Wei and Zhang Hao were working at their consoles, relaxed, easy, oblivious. They had no idea what had just happened twenty meters away on the other side of a metal wall.

Chen Weimin was waiting at the hatch. "Ruan. I got an alert—you activated Channel 23 without authorization. Explain."

I steadied my voice. Mostly. "Captain, I need to report something. It concerns the survival of every person on this ship."

I walked to the main display and played Director Cai's recording.

Li Wei and Zhang Hao drifted over, curious at first, mildly annoyed. As Cai's voice filled the cabin, as classified documents stamped TOP SECRET unfolded across the screen, their expressions underwent a slow, terrible transformation—amusement to confusion, confusion to disbelief, disbelief to white-faced horror.

"We should verify the source," Li Wei said, but her voice had lost its steadiness. "This could be a cyberattack. Enemy disinformation."

I met her eyes. "The supply manifest is right there in the ship's database. Run the numbers yourself."

Zhang Hao volunteered to physically inspect the cargo hold. He came back twenty minutes later, ashen. The inventory matched the files. Provisions for three. Return trip for three.


Chen Weimin hadn't moved. He stood rigid before the screen, chest heaving, a man watching the foundations of his world dissolve.

He'd spent a career in service to something he believed was greater than himself. He'd thought he was a sword in the hand of justice. He'd just learned he was a butcher's knife.

He closed his eyes. For two full minutes he stood like that, breathing hard, the sound filling the cabin. On one side: the nation he'd sworn to serve, the chain of command he'd never once questioned. On the other: three living, breathing human beings in the next compartment who'd been condemned to die in his name.

Li Wei and Zhang Hao watched him in frozen silence. Those two minutes stretched into something geological.

*Bang.*

His fist hit the titanium desk so hard the entire cabin rang.

"Sons of bitches."

When his eyes opened, they were red—and in them was something more devastating than rage. It was shame. The specific, corrosive shame of a man who discovers he's been made complicit in something unforgivable.

"What do they think we are? Accomplices? Tools?" He turned on his crew, and his voice carried the force of a verdict. "To hell with the 'bigger picture.' If we follow this order—if we go home carrying their bones and collect medals for it—then we aren't human. Then there's nothing inside these suits but empty air."

"I'd rather die out here," Li Wei said quietly, "than walk over their graves to get home."

"Same," said Zhang Hao.


Standing there, watching these golden children of the establishment choose conscience over career, I felt something warm crack open in my chest. The universe was cold and the politics were filthy, but this—this small, stubborn flame of human decency—it was still burning.

"Go tell Old Zhao," Chen said, and his voice was a commander's again—level, certain. "We're one crew now. All six of us. I have a briefing to handle. Half an hour, main cabin, full assembly. Nobody gets left behind."



## The Flame

Thirty minutes later, Spark's control room felt like the inside of a pressure cooker. Six people ringed the holographic star chart, tension radiating off them like heat from a reactor core. Six gamblers. All in.

Chen stood at the center, grave and measured. "Full transparency from here on. Here's what I know. I reached Commander Gao on a secure line. Once I briefed him, he ordered us to alter course and hold in Earth orbit. Then I received a countermanding directive from Secretary Yan: continue to Mars as planned. Immediately after that, ground command severed our comm link. As of now, we have no contact with anyone—nothing but broadcast TV."

He let that sink in.

"One thing is beyond doubt: Mars is a death sentence. I know none of you are afraid to die. But I will not allow anyone under my command to die in the dark, as a pawn in someone else's game. The orders from above are contradictory and we have no means of clarification. My recommendation: we turn around. We go back. We blow this thing open ourselves."

"Agreed." Old Zhao didn't hesitate. "We're not chess pieces. We're not going to float out here and wait for someone to decide whether we live or die."

The others nodded, one after another. Unanimous.

"Then here's how we divide it. Li Wei—keep trying to raise command on any frequency. Ruan, Xiao Ma—you two are our navigation brain trust. Plot us a return trajectory. Old Zhao, Zhang Hao—with me on contingency planning."

The cabin erupted into activity.

The problem was brutally simple: Stardust could land anywhere—desert, tundra, ocean—but Spark could not. Spark was built for deep space, not atmospheric entry. No chemical engines, no parachutes, no landing gear. Its only option was an agonizingly slow ion-brake deceleration in orbit, followed by a series of careful aerobraking skips along the upper atmosphere, before finally being caught by Qixi's maglev capture track. That process, under normal circumstances, took the better part of three days.

Three days was too long. Three days meant the people who'd tried to kill us would have time to regroup, cover tracks, prepare a different kind of welcome.

What we needed was speed. What we came up with was insanity.

The plan: jettison the cargo pod. Fire both Spark's ion drive and Stardust's chemical rockets simultaneously. The combined thrust wasn't enough for a major orbital change, but it could nudge us onto a new vector—one aimed at the Moon. We'd use lunar gravity as a slingshot, whipping around the far side and hurling ourselves back toward Earth at maximum speed.

The catch was separation. Once we reached low Earth orbit, Stardust and Spark would have to part ways. Stardust could punch straight through the atmosphere. Spark would need to bleed speed in orbit first, skim the atmosphere down to Mach 8, then ride the maglev track home.

Two ships. Two landings. No margin for error.

Xiao Ma ran the numbers until sweat dripped off her chin. "That fusion engine pushed our initial velocity way higher than standard—we'll reach lunar closest approach in two hours. One shot. If we burn everything Stardust has left on the far side, we can use the Moon's gravity well to sling us back into an Earth-return trajectory." She paused. "If we get the angle wrong by more than a fraction of a degree, we get flung out of the Earth-Moon system entirely. Permanently."

"Still better than sitting here waiting to be erased," I said. "We go."

Chen and Old Zhao signed off on the plan. Everyone strapped in. The cargo pod separated with a muffled thud and fell away into the black. Spark rotated, aimed its nose at the pale disc of the Moon, and burned.


"Five minutes to closest approach!"

The Moon swelled outside the viewport—first a coin, then a wall, then something that filled the entire visual field with dead gray immensity. Craters resolved into sharp relief, ancient impact scars staring back at us like empty eyes.

"Entering far-side shadow in thirty seconds!"

The instant we swung behind the Moon, Earth's light vanished. Total darkness. Not the darkness of a closed room—the darkness of the void itself, so deep it seemed to have substance, to press against the hull. Gravity seized us. The ship's skeleton groaned, metal flexing under tidal stress.

"*Now!* Full reverse burn!"

*BOOM—*

Stardust's chemical engine—a modest thing, built for short hops between orbit and surface—unleashed everything it had in a single, screaming, suicidal burn. Through the porthole I could see Spark's massive hull shuddering under the force, hear the tortured shriek of structural members pushed past their design limits. The ion drive's blue whisper was nothing against the chemical rocket's white fury. We were an ant trying to wrestle an elephant into a U-turn.

Physics cooperated—barely. Under the combined pull of lunar gravity and the rocket's thrust, our trajectory bent into a knife-edge acute angle. We skimmed the surface so closely I could have counted the boulders on those frozen ridges.

"Shadow exit! Course correction confirmed! Target: *Earth!*"

When the blue planet swung back into view, we screamed. All of us. A raw, ragged cheer that had nothing elegant about it. We'd pulled it off—stolen ourselves back from the abyss with a stunt that belonged in a physics textbook's appendix of "things you should never attempt."

The long coast home was haunted. Not just the silence from ground control—we'd expected that. But even the civilian broadcast satellites had gone dark. Every frequency was dead air. Something had happened down there, and the silence told us more than any transmission could have.



The moment came.

"Stardust, prepare for separation!" Chen's voice over the intercom, steady as a metronome. "See you on the ground."

A mechanical clunk. Stardust drifted free. Through the porthole I watched Spark recede—that great silver hull growing small against the black, solitary, resolute. It would face the longer, harder road: hours of orbital braking, alone, while we took the express route straight down.

Stardust's engine fired one last time for attitude adjustment. "Fuel consumption's running five percent over projections," Xiao Ma reported, tension leaking into her voice. "If we don't cut thrust now, we won't have enough for a controlled landing."

"Kill the main engine," Old Zhao commanded, already calculating. "Steepen the entry angle. Let the atmosphere do the braking. We'll arrive early, but we'll arrive."

I monitored cabin pressure and temperature—nominal, both—while Earth grew in the viewport, vast and vivid, oceans and cloud swirls resolving into heartbreaking detail.

"Final check," Old Zhao said. "Suits sealed, life support green, harnesses locked. Brace for entry."


"Reentry velocity eleven klicks per second—near escape speed!" Xiao Ma's face was gray. "This heat shield was specced for the Martian atmosphere. Whether it can handle *Earth*—your guess is as good as mine."

"Then it'll just have to exceed its specs," I said through clenched teeth. "Let's go."

*WHAM.*

No transition. No warning curve. The craft hit atmosphere like a hammer hitting a bell.

The viewport became a furnace.

Plasma engulfed the hull, turning the black of space into a blinding orange inferno. Temperature spiked. The bulkheads radiated heat. The air inside the cabin tasted of scorched insulation.

Vibrations so violent my teeth cracked against each other and my vision strobed. The shriek of superheated air tearing across the hull filled the world—a thousand banshees clawing at the skin of the ship.

"Heat flux one-twenty percent of max design load!" Xiao Ma was screaming her readouts, tears streaming sideways across her face in the G-forces. "Ablation detected! Port wing thermal anomaly!"

"Hold attitude! *Nobody touch anything!*" Old Zhao's voice cut through the chaos like a foghorn. "As long as we don't tumble, we survive!"

We blazed across the upper stratosphere like a meteor—a streak of white fire trailing a comet's tail of vaporized heat shield.


Time dissolved. It might have been minutes. It felt like eons.


"Blackout over! Comms restored!" Xiao Ma sagged in her harness as if her bones had liquefied. "Fuel at seven percent. Enough for precision landing."

I heard her voice and knew we'd made it through the worst. The shaking faded to a manageable tremor; the fire outside died to darkness. We were subsonic now, falling through the stratosphere. Xiao Ma deployed the emergency glide surfaces—a pair of stubby delta wings that unfolded from the hull like a butterfly testing its wings after metamorphosis.

Then the comm channel erupted in static.

"Ksshhh... ksshhh... This is Ground Command."

The voice was synthetic in its flatness—cold, mechanical, stripped of every human quality. It raised the hair on the back of my neck.

"Calling Pillar of Heaven Five. Respond."

Old Zhao opened the channel. "Ground, this is Stardust. We have suffered critical mechanical failure. Requesting emergency clearance for immediate landing. Repeat—emergency landing."

By rights, mission control should have been pandemonium—a lost spacecraft returning unannounced from deep space. Instead: silence. A long, knowing silence. As if they'd been expecting us.

Then the dead voice again, flat as a coroner reading a chart:

"Acknowledged. Landing authorized. Proceed on designated vector to Zone Three."


## Phosphorescence

The Qixi control tower swelled in the viewport, and for one naïve moment, I thought: *feet on the ground and we're safe.*

"Captain—*look!*" Xiao Ma's voice cracked. "Thermal scanner! The ground is—something is very wrong!"

I pulled up the thermal feed and my stomach dropped through the floor. The runway apron—which should have been empty—was crawling with heat signatures. Dozens of them, arrayed in tactical formation. Fully armed special forces, deployed in a perfect kill box. Wolves in position, waiting for the lamb to land.

"They're here to clean up." Old Zhao's voice went flat and arctic. No surprise in it—just grim confirmation of something he'd already calculated. "This is a sanitization op. Listen up. Activate suit shields *now.* Xiao Ma—put us down on the far edge of the field, as far from their positions as possible. We hold until Chen and Group A reach the surface. That's our only play."

We moved fast. The shield systems hummed to life—layers of electromagnetic field energy rippling across our suit surfaces in pale blue arcs. These suits were built for the grinding hazards of space construction. They were never meant for combat. But they were all we had.

Retro-rockets kicked up a sandstorm as Stardust slammed into the wasteland at the launch site's periphery. The hatch blew and we were out, boots hitting dirt for the first time in—how long had it been? Days? A lifetime?

Soldiers swarmed us before the dust settled—a dozen of them, weapons up, shouting. They expected scared scientists.

They miscalculated.

Old Zhao met the first two at a walk. No flourish, no wasted motion. The exoskeleton's hydraulic arms hissed once, then discharged—and a soldier in full combat gear went airborne, spinning, rifle and all, landing in a heap fifteen meters away. I felt something snap inside me: rage, animal and absolute. My leg servos screamed as I launched myself upward, catching three meters of air, and drove a powered kick into a trooper lining up an RPG. I felt his body armor buckle under the impact.

"Get to a vehicle! Commandeer cover! *Move!*" Old Zhao roared.

But these were elite soldiers. The surprise lasted seconds. They fell back with disciplined speed and brought up the heavy guns. Armored vehicles opened up with turret-mounted autocannons and heavy machine guns, tracer rounds stitching the air in luminous threads.

The fusillade hit our shields and the world turned blue—rippling, crackling, blinding. Each impact sent a subsonic pulse through the suit frame that rattled my skeleton. The shields held the rounds out, but the kinetic transfer was brutal, pounding my organs against my ribs. On my HUD, the shield integrity bar was dropping in visible increments—a shrinking blue line counting down to nothing.

*"Warning: shield reserves declining—seventy-two percent... sixty-five..."* The synthetic voice in my helmet was relentless.

"They've switched to AP incendiary! Shields won't take much more!" Xiao Ma's suit was already scorched black from near-misses, her voice shredding at the edges.

We fell back behind Stardust, pressing ourselves against the massive titanium landing struts—our last piece of hard cover. The air stank of cordite and ozone. I crouched there, listening to the labored pneumatic gasps of my exoskeleton joints, watching red warning icons blossom across my visor like digital flowers. The slow, certain knowledge that our protection was being chipped away, layer by layer—that was a cold beyond anything space had offered.

"Captain... we can't hold." I kept my voice low, suit-to-suit. "Maybe we should—surrender? Buy time?"

Old Zhao's laugh was short and ugly. "The *entire premise* of Project Moth is that we're dead. To these people, a good hero is a dead hero. Surrender just saves them ammunition."

We were out of options and almost out of shield when the sound hit—a deep, furious roar of diesel engines from the western perimeter.

Two troop carriers barreled in from the west, wheels churning up rooster tails of dust, cutting a pair of smoke trails across the open field. The vehicles were near-identical to the special forces' own APCs, but someone had hastily slapped bright red paint across their flanks—garish, unmistakable. Crude loudspeakers were bolted to their roofs, and the voice booming from them punched through the gunfire with startling clarity:

"We are the Red Mountain Free Army! Brothers at the Space Center—do not be afraid! We are here to get you out!"

I knew that voice.

*Guofeng?*

Guofeng. Free Army. The two concepts collided in my skull and produced only static. My old bunkmate—the bitter cynic who'd washed out of selection and vanished—was leading the insurgent force that haunted government propaganda broadcasts? The Red Mountain Free Army had always been painted as fringe terrorists, a minor nuisance in the border highlands, probably foreign-backed. The idea that they could field armored vehicles and assault a *space center*—

The thought that their arrival might brand us as traitors flashed through my mind, but there wasn't time to process it.

The special forces hadn't expected a second front. Their formation stuttered. Some guns swiveled toward the newcomers; others kept firing at us. The Free Army gave as good as they got—tongues of muzzle flash stabbed from the transports' firing ports, trading volleys with the government troops. Between bursts, the loudspeakers roared again:

"Soldiers! Stand down! Your government has already been exposed! They sacrificed astronauts to cover their own failures, and the evidence is public! The world is watching this field *right now!* Journalists are inbound! Your commanders' days are numbered!"

The battlefield splintered into pandemonium. Armor-piercing rounds hammered our shields in staccato bursts, each impact a flash of blinding blue. Smoke and dust choked the air. Through the haze I caught glimpses of vehicles wheeling and firing, muzzle flashes winking like angry stars—a three-way melee with no clear lines.

Then—a light in the sky. A brilliant point above Xuanlong Ridge, growing fast, trailing a faint luminous wake.

"*Spark!*" I shouted.

Chen and Group A were coming in on the maglev approach, completely unaware that they were descending into a war zone. They were following the original landing protocol, nose aimed at the track entrance like nothing had changed.

"No—*no!* They don't know!" Old Zhao's voice broke over the comm. "The track is destroyed! If they come in at speed—"

We screamed into our suit radios, trying to warn them. Nothing came back. No acknowledgment, no response. Spark entered the maglev rail at the crest of Xuanlong Ridge—but the track was dead, its power severed. Without electromagnetic braking, the ship became a runaway train, an unguided projectile carrying catastrophic momentum, hurtling off the end of the rail at three hundred kilometers per hour.

"*EVERYONE DOWN!!!*"

Old Zhao's roar. My body moved before my brain caught up—I threw myself sideways into a drainage culvert and covered my head.

The impact was geological.

A wall of sound and force—the ground bucking like a living thing, a shockwave of debris and shredded metal shrieking inches above my helmet. Spark's colossal hull clipped the control tower, carved through half the launch complex like a plow through wet earth, and slammed into the perimeter wall at the base's edge. Reinforced concrete disintegrated on contact. A mushroom cloud of dust and rubble boiled skyward, blotting out the sun.

What happened next was chaos compounding chaos. The wall's collapse ripped a gaping hole in the base's perimeter—and waiting on the other side was a mob of journalists. They'd been gathering for hours, drawn by fragmentary reports and rumor, held back by armed guards and razor wire. Now the barrier was gone. They poured through the breach like water through a broken dam, heedless of the gunfire still crackling inside. Drones launched in swarms—hundreds of them, camera-equipped quadcopters rising into the smoke-filled sky like metallic locusts. Recording lights blinked red. Every lens, every sensor, every satellite uplink locked onto the scene below:

Burning wreckage. Soldiers firing. Three battered figures in scorched spacesuits, huddled behind a shattered landing craft.

"Is that the ship? That's Spark!" "Are those—are those *gunshots?* Who is the military shooting at?" "Oh my God—those are the astronauts. The *missing* astronauts!"

The world was watching.

The soldiers knew it. You could see the hesitation ripple through their ranks like a physical thing. Under the gaze of a billion screens, fingers eased off triggers. The gunfire thinned, stuttered, and died to an uneasy silence—the loaded, brittle silence of a standoff, every weapon still aimed, every nerve still raw.


I lay in the drainage ditch, clutching my cracked helmet, staring through the haze at the smoking ruin of Spark.

Then Old Zhao's voice, ragged on the comm—carrying something I'd never heard from him before. Not anger. Not determination. Fear.

"The tower. *Look at the tower.*"

I dragged myself upright, squinting through the pall of dust and smoke, and turned toward the control tower.

What I saw there stopped every thought in my head.
