# A Boat Ticket to America

*1995* 

All over the chaotic streets of Shenzhen, cheap, square speakers were haphazardly wired outside barbershops, midnight food stalls, and the corrugated iron sheds of motorcycle repair shops. The sound quality was absolutely terrible, heavily distorted by static, but the songs were always blasted at maximum volume. Sometimes it was Su Rui singing "Holding Hands," sometimes it was Sun Yue singing "Wishing You Peace," and sometimes they were just incredibly sad, syrupy love songs I didn't even know the names of. The melodies drifted endlessly from one end of the dusty street to the other, violently clashing with the relentless, rhythmic pounding of pile drivers at the construction sites and the endless blaring of car horns.

Shenzhen had simply changed too fast in recent years.

When I first arrived, huge chunks of the Luohu district were still just empty, overgrown wasteland. Now, Shennan Avenue had been aggressively paved into a massive, spacious boulevard, though both sides were still flanked by endless construction sites. Giant tower cranes spun non-stop from the crack of dawn until the dead of night. Fleets of heavy dump trucks rumbled back and forth, and the humid air was permanently choked with the harsh, metallic smell of wet cement and dry red dust.

Absolutely everyone was incredibly busy. The people selling clothes were busy, the people opening factories were busy, and the people running transport were busy. The unspoken rule hung heavily in the air: whoever stopped moving would instantly be left behind by this ruthless city.

I was already over thirty years old and had spent the last several years carving out a living doing wholesale clothing business in the Dongmen market. I forced myself out of bed past three o'clock every single morning. I would splash cold water on my face, gnaw on two cold, tasteless steamed buns, and hurry off to the railway station, dragging massive, overstuffed woven sacks behind me.

The earliest morning train heading to Guangzhou was always packed to the absolute brim. The train cars were completely overrun with small-time business people from all over the country. Some were hauling clothes, some were moving counterfeit shoes, and others were pushing cheap digital watches. Woven bags were piled dangerously high in the narrow aisles, and heavy cardboard boxes were brutally stuffed beneath every available seat. The stale, uncirculated air was a suffocating mixture of cheap instant noodles, soy-sauce tea eggs, and heavy, unwashed sweat.

Sometimes the train was so crowded you couldn't find a place to sit even after standing for two grueling hours. But absolutely no one complained. Because everyone on that train knew one thing with absolute, crystal clarity: the moment you stepped off that train, you were making money.

Relying on running back and forth on that brutal route all year round, I eventually managed to squirrel away a decent nest egg. Hidden securely under my bed was a locked suitcase containing every single cent of my life savings. Thick bundles of hundred-yuan bills were stacked meticulously, tied off tightly with thick rubber bands.

On the nights when I couldn't sleep, I would occasionally drag the heavy suitcase out from under the bed, sit quietly in the dark, and count the money. After counting it once, I'd count it all over again. It wasn't that I was paranoid the money was magically disappearing; it was simply the only thing that made my anxious heart feel completely grounded. Counting those bills was the only way I knew for sure that I had finally gained a solid foothold in Shenzhen.

Whenever I actually had a moment of free time, I loved dragging my best friend, Ah Fang, out to go window shopping. But no matter where we went, I always kept my heavy black pager clipped firmly to my belt. The second that pager let out its shrill beep, I had to drop whatever I was doing, frantically hunt down a public payphone, and call the number back. Sometimes it was an angry customer rushing me for a delayed shipment. Sometimes it was a tip that hot new styles had just dropped at the wholesale stalls in Guangzhou. In this city, business people were terrified of missing out on a single scrap of news.

At the beginning of the year, I met Richard Liang. 

That afternoon, the luxurious Sunshine Hotel was hosting a massive economic and trade networking reception. Rumor had it that a slew of wealthy Hong Kong businessmen and deep-pocketed overseas Chinese bosses were attending. I begged a well-connected friend for a spare admission ticket. My plan was simple: slip in, look around, and see if I could somehow find a channel to start exporting my clothes directly to Hong Kong.

It was pouring rain that day, and the main entrance to the hotel was undergoing heavy renovations, turning the entire driveway into a disastrous, muddy swamp. The exact second I stepped out of my taxi, my foot sank deep into a hidden mud puddle. Filthy, brown water splashed everywhere, completely ruining my expensive high heels.

As I was squatting miserably by the side of the road, furiously trying to scrub my shoes clean with a flimsy tissue, a sleek, black Toyota Crown slowly pulled up right next to me. The tinted window rolled down smoothly, revealing a man in his early thirties sitting in the back seat. He wore delicate, gold-rimmed glasses and had the smooth, extremely pale skin of someone who rarely worked in the sun.

He reached out and handed me a beautiful, long-handled black umbrella. He spoke in flawless Mandarin, tinged with a soft, polite Taiwanese accent. "The rain is incredibly heavy today. Please, don't get wet and catch a cold."

Gripping the handle of the umbrella, I was completely stunned. By the time I finally recovered my wits, his luxury car had already glided smoothly away.

I never in a million years expected to bump into him again inside the massive banquet hall.

He actually took the initiative to walk over through the crowd and greet me. "I didn't completely ruin your shoes out there, did I?" That was our first official conversation.

He introduced himself as Richard Liang. He explained that he lived long-term in the United States and was currently employed as a senior executive for a massive international investment firm. The entire purpose of his return trip to China was to scout out highly lucrative cooperation projects.

The networking reception was absolute chaos; desperate people were shoving business cards into each other's hands everywhere you looked. The local bosses spoke incredibly loudly, boasting aggressively, and drank their liquor fiercely. But Richard was entirely different. He always spoke in an unhurried, incredibly soothing tone. Whenever the boisterous men toasted him, he only took a polite, measured sip of his wine. Whenever others loudly bragged about the millions they had made, he simply listened with a quiet, knowing smile. That kind of profound composure was exceedingly rare among the aggressive, frantic business people I dealt with every day.

Over the next few weeks, we met up a few more times. Sometimes we drank expensive coffee in the grand lobby of his hotel; sometimes we enjoyed leisurely morning tea at a high-end teahouse in Luohu. His perspective on the world was incredibly broad. He would effortlessly transition from discussing the political climate in the US to the booming economy in Hong Kong, from the volatility of stocks to the explosion of local real estate, and from clothing export quotas to the intricacies of international trade. He seemed to deeply understand a thousand different things that I knew absolutely nothing about.

Once, while we were cutting steaks at an expensive Western restaurant in Luohu, he suddenly set his knife down and looked at me intensely. "You know," he murmured, "for a woman as smart and capable as you, it would be an absolute tragedy if you just kept yourself chained to that tiny, dusty stall in Dongmen for the rest of your life."

I blinked, caught off guard. "Then... what exactly do you think I should do?"

He leaned in closer. "You need to create your own brand. We can export your clothes directly to the United States."

At that exact moment, I fully admit that my heart leaped in my chest. I had worked myself to the absolute bone trying to make money these past few years, but a deep, gnawing regret had always lingered in the back of my mind. It didn't matter how much cash I stuffed under my bed; in the eyes of the world, I was still just a terribly rustic, uneducated individual business owner. But the brilliant, glittering future he was painting for me looked like an entirely different universe.

After that, our relationship rapidly became much closer. He frequently took me up to the exclusive executive lounge on the top floor of the Sunshine Hotel for late-night coffee. Staring out through the massive floor-to-ceiling windows, the sprawling city of Shenzhen was brilliantly lit. In the far distance, you could just make out the glittering, mesmerizing lights of Hong Kong.

He wrapped his arm around my shoulder. "Come with me to San Francisco," he whispered softly. "The weather there is absolutely beautiful. It's never as muggy and suffocating as Shenzhen. I promise you, you'll love it."

Whenever he spoke about our future, he was always incredibly, fiercely serious. He was so utterly convincing that he left me with absolutely no room for doubt.

I had never once traveled abroad in my entire life. I had never even set foot in Hong Kong, which was right next door. In my limited eyes, the United States was as impossibly distant as another planet. And Richard was the magical bridge connecting me to that glittering new world.

Ah Fang had tried to warn me. She constantly told me to be wary of him. Once, she pulled me aggressively into the back kitchen of her noodle shop and quietly demanded, "Have you actually investigated this guy?"

I crossed my arms, feeling instantly defensive and annoyed. "Investigate what exactly?"

"That massive investment company he keeps bragging about. How come I've literally never heard a single person mention it?"

I laughed out loud, rolling my eyes. "Ah Fang, there are tens of thousands of massive companies in the US! How many could you possibly have heard of sitting here in Luohu?"

Ah Fang frowned deeply, her eyes full of genuine worry. "Whatever you say. I just... I don't think he's reliable. Something feels wrong."

I didn't take her warnings to heart at all. Looking back on it now, it's a bitter truth that bystanders standing on the shore can often see the approaching storm far more clearly than the fools currently drowning in the sea.

In mid-September, Richard suddenly became incredibly anxious. He paced his hotel room endlessly and didn't sleep well for several days in a row. Sometimes, I would wake up in the middle of the night to find him frantically making international long-distance calls in hushed, urgent tones.

I finally grabbed his arm and demanded to know what was wrong.

He sighed heavily, rubbing his pale face. "There is a massive export project that's ready to be approved immediately. It's guaranteed money. But we are agonizingly short of completing the very last step."

A few days later, he rushed into the room, excitedly waving a thick stack of documents entirely in English. He triumphantly announced that the US partners had officially agreed to the cooperation. As soon as a security deposit was wired over, the project would officially launch.

"It's purely a bureaucratic procedural issue," he explained smoothly, kissing my forehead. "The funds are only going to be temporarily frozen in escrow. Every single cent will be returned to us the moment the final procedures are cleared."

I asked him exactly how much money was still needed to clear the hurdle. 

"Eight hundred thousand," he said without blinking.

When I heard that massive number, my heart violently skipped a beat. Every single cent of my life savings, scraped together over years of blood and sweat, only amounted to a little over five hundred thousand.

But he immediately pulled me into a tight hug, comforting me in that soothing voice. He promised I didn't need to worry about the money at all; he would figure out a way to handle the shortfall himself. And precisely because he didn't demand I pay the whole thing, I believed him even more deeply.

I rushed home and dragged the heavy suitcase out from under my bed. I pulled out every single bundle of cash I owned. I then spent the next three days desperately borrowing the rest from every relative and friend I had in the city, ultimately helping him scrape together the full eight hundred thousand yuan.

The afternoon I handed the massive duffel bag of cash over to him, Richard hugged me so tightly I could barely breathe. "Trust me," he whispered fiercely into my ear. "This is the very first step toward our brand new life."

I believed him. I believed him completely, with every fiber of my being.

In early October, Richard announced he had to rush to Guangzhou for a few days to meet a critically important government contact. If the meeting went smoothly, he promised that as soon as he returned, we would immediately start processing the marriage paperwork and our visas to go abroad.

Before leaving for the train station, he stood in the doorway of his hotel room, wearing that elegant beige trench coat. He turned back, flashed me his signature, brilliant smile, and said, "Wait right here for me to come back. We'll go take our wedding photos this weekend."

That exact second was the happiest moment of my entire life. 

It was also the last time I ever saw him.

On the first day he was gone, he didn't call. On the second day, there was absolutely no reply on his pager. By the third day, a cold, creeping panic began to set in.

I ran frantically to the Sunshine Hotel, straight to the front desk of the luxury suite he had occupied for months. The polite receptionist checked the computer, frowned, and informed me that Mr. Liang had officially checked out and cleared his bill days ago.

I agonized in a state of sheer, suffocating terror for several more days. Finally, entirely broken, I mustered up the courage to walk into the local police station.

The tired police officer in charge of the reception desk didn't look the slightest bit surprised after hearing my hysterical, tear-soaked story. He calmly flipped through a thick binder of reports, made two quick phone calls, and finally looked up at me with dead, exhausted eyes. 

"This is an incredibly common scam," he said flatly. "We've had dozens of nearly identical cases reported in this district recently."

A deafening, high-pitched buzzing sound suddenly erupted in my ears. I couldn't hear a single word the officer said after that. 

When I finally stumbled blindly out of the police station, the midday sun was massive and blindingly bright. Traffic on Shennan Avenue flowed in a continuous, deafening roar, and car horns blared incessantly. Clouds of thick, yellow dust still flew aggressively into the air above the endless construction sites.

Shenzhen was still the exact same Shenzhen. It seemed as if absolutely nothing in the world had changed. And yet, for me, it felt as if absolutely everything had been destroyed.

I sat numbly on the dusty curb by the side of the road until the sun finally went down and the streetlights flickered on. I watched the endless tide of busy, frantic people rushing past me, and I suddenly felt overwhelmingly, bone-crushingly tired. 

I had endured so much bitter hardship over the years. I had hauled so much heavy cargo on my own back, and I had survived so many freezing, sleepless nights on those trains—and I had never once allowed myself to cry. But sitting on that curb, I finally felt that I couldn't hold on for a single second longer.

I dragged myself to Ah Fang's noodle shop. The moment she saw the dead, hollow look on my face, she didn't ask a single question. She just silently went to the kitchen, cooked a massive bowl of steaming beef offal noodles, and placed it gently on the table in front of me. The beef offal was scalding hot, with a thick layer of bright red chili oil floating on the surface of the rich broth. 

"Eat something first," she said softly.

I slowly lowered my head and took a small bite. Heavy, hot tears finally broke loose, falling silently, drop by drop, into the dark soup. 

Suddenly, the noodles tasted exceptionally, agonizingly spicy. And they tasted incredibly, unbearably bitter.
