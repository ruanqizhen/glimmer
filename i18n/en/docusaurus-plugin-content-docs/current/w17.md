# A Boat Ticket to America

   *1995* 

All over the streets, square speakers were hung in barbershops, food stalls, and corrugated iron sheds for repairing motorcycles. The sound quality of the speakers was very poor, but the songs played were particularly loud. Sometimes it was "Holding Hands," sometimes it was "Wishing You Peace," and sometimes they were love songs I didn't know the names of. The singing drifted from one end of the street to the other, mixing with the sounds of pile driving at construction sites and car horns.

Shenzhen had changed too fast in recent years.

When I first arrived, many places in Luohu were still wastelands. Now Shennan Avenue had been built wide and spacious, but both sides were still full of construction sites. Tower cranes turned non-stop from morning till night, mud trucks came and went, and the air was always filled with the smell of cement and red soil.

Everyone was very busy. Those selling clothes were busy, those opening factories were busy, and those in transport were busy. It seemed like whoever stopped would be left behind by this city.

I was already over thirty and had been doing wholesale clothing business in Dongmen these years. I got up at past three in the morning every day. I washed my face, gnawed on two steamed buns, and hurried to the railway station carrying snakeskin bags.

The earliest train to Guangzhou was always packed to the brim. The train cars were full of business people everywhere. Some sold clothes, some sold shoes, some sold digital watches. Woven bags were piled up in the aisles, and cardboard boxes were stuffed under the seats. The air was mixed with the smells of instant noodles, tea eggs, and sweat.

Sometimes you couldn't find a place to sit even after standing for two hours. But no one complained. Because everyone knew clearly in their hearts that once they got off the train, it was money.

Relying on running back and forth like this all year round, I finally managed to save up a bit of a nest egg. Under my bed was a suitcase containing my savings from these years. Bundles of hundred-yuan bills were tied neatly with rubber bands.

When I couldn't sleep at night, I would occasionally drag the suitcase out and sit on the edge of the bed counting the money. After counting it once, I'd count it again. It wasn't that I was afraid there would be less, but it just made my heart feel grounded. I knew I had gained a foothold in Shenzhen.

When I had free time, I liked to drag my good friend Ah Fang out to go shopping. But I always carried a pager (BP machine) with me. When the pager beeped, I had to quickly find a phone and call back. Sometimes it was a customer rushing for goods. Sometimes new styles had arrived at the Guangzhou stalls. Business people are most afraid of missing news.

At the beginning of the year, I met Richard Liang. That day, Sunshine Hotel held an economic and trade networking reception. I heard many Hong Kong businessmen and overseas Chinese bosses had come. I asked a friend for an admission ticket. I wanted to go there and see if there was a chance to sell clothes to Hong Kong.

It was raining that day, and the hotel entrance was under construction, leaving the ground a muddy mess. As soon as I got out of the taxi, I stepped into a mud puddle. Muddy water splashed everywhere, and my high heels got dirty too.

While I was squatting by the roadside wiping my shoes with a tissue, a black Toyota Crown slowly pulled up next to me. The window rolled down, and sitting inside was a man in his thirties, wearing gold-rimmed glasses, with very pale skin.

He handed me a long-handled black umbrella and said in Mandarin with a slight Taiwanese accent, "The rain is so heavy, don't get wet and catch a cold."

Holding the umbrella, I was stunned for a moment. By the time I recovered, his car had already driven away.

But I never expected to meet him again inside the venue.

He took the initiative to come over and greet me: "Didn't ruin your shoes just now, did you?" That was our first official conversation.

He said his name was Richard Liang, he lived in the United States long-term, and was currently working for an investment company. His return to China this time was to look for cooperation projects.

At the networking reception, people were exchanging business cards everywhere. The bosses spoke very loudly and drank fiercely. But Richard was different; he always spoke unhurriedly. When others toasted him, he only took a sip. When others boasted about how much money they had made, he just listened with a smile. That kind of composure was not common among business people.

Later we met a few more times, sometimes drinking coffee at the hotel, sometimes having morning tea at a teahouse in Luohu. He had a very broad perspective, chatting from the US to Hong Kong, from stocks to real estate, from clothing exports to international trade. He understood many things that I didn't.

Once at a Western restaurant in Luohu, he said, "Man, you know? For a woman like you, it would be a pity if you just stick to that stall in Dongmen all the time."

I asked, "Then what do you think I should do?"

He said, "You should create your own brand. Sell clothes to the United States."

At that moment, I admit my heart was moved. I had worked desperately to make money these years, but I always had a regret in my heart. No matter how much money I had, I was just a terribly rustic individual business owner. But the future he described looked like another kind of life.

Later, our relationship got closer and closer. He often took me to the top floor of the Sunshine Hotel for coffee. Looking out the window at night, Shenzhen was brightly lit. The lights in the direction of Hong Kong could be seen in the distance.

He said, "Come with me to San Francisco. The weather there is very good, not as muggy as Shenzhen. You'll like it!"

When saying these things, he was always particularly serious. So serious that it left me with no room for doubt.

I had never been abroad, and had never even been to Hong Kong, which was close at hand. In my eyes, the United States was as far away as another world. And Richard was the person connecting to that world.

Ah Fang had warned me, telling me to be wary of this person. Once she pulled me aside and quietly asked, "Have you investigated him?"

I was a bit unhappy: "Investigate what?"

"That company he mentioned, how come I've never heard of it?"

I laughed: "There are so many companies in the US, how many could you have heard of?"

Ah Fang frowned: "Anyway, I just don't think he's very reliable."

I didn't take these words to heart at all at the time. Thinking back later, there are some things that bystanders can often see more clearly than those involved.

In mid-September, Richard suddenly became anxious and didn't sleep well for several days in a row. Sometimes he was still making international long-distance calls in his room in the middle of the night.

I asked him what had happened.

He said, "There is a project that can be approved right away. We are only short of the last step."

A few days later, he brought a thick English document and told me that the US side had agreed to the cooperation. As long as a deposit was paid, the project could officially launch.

He said, "This is just a procedural issue. The funds are only temporarily frozen and will all be returned after the procedures are completed."

I asked him how much money was still needed, and he said eight hundred thousand.

When I heard the number, my heart skipped a beat. All my savings over the years were only a little over five hundred thousand.

But he comforted me, saying I didn't need to worry about the money; he could handle it. Precisely because of this, I believed him even more.

I pulled out all my savings from under the bed, borrowed some more from relatives and friends, and helped him scrape together the eight hundred thousand.

The day the money was handed over, Richard hugged me. He said, "Trust me. This is the first step to a new life."

I believed it, believed it completely.

In early October, Richard said he was going to Guangzhou to meet an important person. If things went smoothly, we could start going through the procedures for marriage and going abroad when he came back.

Before leaving, he stood at the door, wearing that beige trench coat, turned back and smiled at me, and said, "Wait for me to come back. We'll go take wedding photos."

That was my happiest moment, and also the last time I saw him.

On the first day, there were no phone calls at all. On the second day, there was no reply on the pager. On the third day, I started to panic.

I ran to the Sunshine Hotel, the hotel where he usually stayed long-term. The front desk told me that Mr. Liang had checked out long ago.

I agonized for many days, and finally, I mustered up the courage to go to the police station.

The police officer in charge of reception wasn't surprised at all after hearing the whole story. He flipped through some materials, made a few phone calls, and finally looked up at me and said, "This is a very common scam. There have been many similar cases recently."

I just felt a buzzing sound in my ears, and I couldn't hear clearly what was said after that. When I walked out of the police station, the sun was huge. Traffic on Shennan Avenue flowed continuously, and horns blared incessantly. Dust still flew in the air at the construction sites.

Shenzhen was still the same Shenzhen. It seemed as if nothing had changed, yet it seemed as if everything had changed.

I sat by the roadside until dark, watching the people coming and going on the street, and suddenly felt particularly tired. Having endured so much hardship these years, carried so much cargo, stayed up so many late nights, I had never cried. But at this moment, I felt I couldn't hold on any longer.

I went to Ah Fang's noodle shop. When she saw my state, she asked nothing and just made a bowl of beef offal noodles and placed it in front of me. The beef offal was steaming hot, with chili oil floating on the soup's surface. She said, "Eat something first."

I lowered my head and took a bite. Tears fell into the bowl. Suddenly, I felt it was particularly spicy, and also particularly bitter.
