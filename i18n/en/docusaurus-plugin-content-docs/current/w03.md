# The Tricycle at Night

*1998*

The factory gates closed with harsh finality. Without much explanation—just a flimsy paper notice pasted on the wall—people like us were abruptly evicted from the lives we had always known. After that, I started pedaling a manual tricycle to make a living.

The tricycle was a rental, fitted with a dark purple cloth canopy and a heavy, cumbersome iron frame. Every day at four or five in the morning, I had to wheel it out of the alleyway. I had to move as quietly as possible, terrified of waking the neighbors. At the time, we still lived in the old tube-shaped apartment buildings (*tongzilou*), where the walls were paper-thin, the quarters were crowded, and absolutely no one was well-off.

Pedaling a tricycle came with strict rules. The local Transportation Bureau decreed that odd-numbered tricycles could only operate on odd days, and even-numbered tricycles on even days. The official line was that this ensured "fairness," giving everyone a chance to earn a meal. But the reality is that an empty stomach doesn't recognize odd and even days. When your wife needs to buy groceries and your kids need to pay school fees, those rules won't help you survive an extra day off.

On the days I wasn't permitted to take the tricycle out, I had to wait until dark. Only after the Transportation Bureau officials had gone home for the night could I quietly push the tricycle out onto the streets. Business was never good at night; sometimes I couldn't even manage two fares, and I had to stay constantly alert for patrols. Getting caught meant a hundred-yuan fine—a devastating blow.

I remember one night very clearly. I was waiting for a fare by the side of the road when a family of five approached. Two adults, three kids. They took one look at the tricycle, glanced at me, and without a word, began squeezing themselves in. The kids squatted on the footboard, the adults sat with their legs spread wide, and the tricycle was packed to the absolute brim. I knew perfectly well that this violated safety rules and was incredibly dangerous, but I didn't utter a single word of protest.

In moments like that, it is nearly impossible to refuse. You know it's overloaded, but you also know this might be the only fare you get all night.

When I tried to start moving, I could barely push the pedals down. I practically had to stand up off the seat, leaning all my weight forward in desperation. My arms gripped the handlebars like a vice, every single muscle in my body trembling with tension. The wheels wobbled dangerously a few times before they finally began to roll forward. By the side of the road, some pedestrians stared openly, while others quickly averted their eyes. At that moment, I could only focus on one thought: *Don't stop. If you stop, you'll never be able to get it moving again.*

Later on, I picked up two young men. They were very polite, chatting amiably, and eventually asked if I could earn five hundred yuan a month doing this. I didn't give them a straight answer. Yes, five hundred yuan was technically achievable—provided you didn't get sick, didn't get fined, it didn't rain or snow, and you found customers every single day. I also didn't mention that I used to work in a state factory. I was too afraid to even say the words "laid off."

When they arrived at their destination, they handed me the money and walked away without asking for change. I called out "thank you" after them several times. I knew they didn't leave the tip because I was an exceptional driver; they did it because they pitied me.

But pity isn't always a blessing for people like us. If you refuse to take my tricycle because you feel "bad for me" or think it's "too degrading," it actually hurts me far more. We are selling our physical labor, not our emotional dignity. If you don't sit in the back, I don't get the fare.

Tomorrow is my turn to legally take the tricycle out again. I'll need to wake up even earlier, pushing it out into the quiet streets long before the break of dawn.
