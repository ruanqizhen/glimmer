# The Tricycle at Night

*1998*

The factory gates slammed shut for good. There was no grand explanation, no apology—just a flimsy piece of paper glued to a brick wall. Just like that, thousands of us were evicted from the only lives we had ever known. To keep food on the table, I started pedaling a rusty, manual tricycle through the streets.

It was a rental. It had a faded purple cloth canopy and a heavy, brutal iron frame that fought you on every hill. Every morning at four or five o'clock, I had to wheel the massive thing out of the alleyway. I crept along in total silence, terrified of waking the neighbors. Back then, we all still lived in those cramped, tube-shaped apartment buildings (*tongzilou*). The walls were paper-thin, you could hear a pin drop three doors down, and absolutely no one had a dime to their name.

Running a tricycle came with a maddening set of rules. The local Transportation Bureau declared that tricycles with odd-numbered license plates could only hit the streets on odd days, and even-numbered plates on even days. They spun it as a way to ensure "fairness," giving every driver a fighting chance to earn a meal. But the harsh reality is that an empty stomach doesn't care what day of the week it is. When your wife needs to buy groceries and your kid's tuition is due, an arbitrary day off doesn't help you survive. 

On the days I was legally banned from the roads, I had to wait for the sun to go down. Only after the Transportation Bureau officers had clocked out and gone home could I quietly push the heavy iron frame out into the dark. Business was always awful at night. Sometimes I couldn't even scrounge up two fares, and my head was always on a swivel, watching for police patrols. Getting caught meant a hundred-yuan fine. That wasn't just a penalty; it was a death sentence for my month's budget.

One night is permanently burned into my memory. I was killing time by the curb, waiting for a fare, when a family of five walked up. Two parents, three little kids. They took one look at my tricycle, glanced at me, and without saying a single word, started piling in. The kids squatted on the cramped footboard. The adults sat on the bench with their legs splayed wide. The cart was packed to the absolute bursting point. I knew damn well that the load was way over the limit. It was incredibly dangerous. But I didn't say a word. I just got on the bike.

When you're backed into a corner, you don't refuse money. You know it's overloaded, but you also know they might be the only people who need a ride all night.

When I tried to take off, my legs felt like lead. I couldn't push the pedals down. I had to stand up completely off the leather seat, throwing my entire body weight forward over the handlebars in sheer desperation. My knuckles went white. Every muscle in my back was screaming. The back wheels wobbled violently, groaning under the weight, before the tires finally caught and began to roll. Pedestrians on the sidewalk stopped and stared openly. Others quickly looked away, uncomfortable with the sight. My brain blocked it all out. I only had one thought pulsing in my head: *Do not stop. If you stop, you'll never get this thing moving again.*

Later that night, I picked up two young guys. They were friendly, chatting with me over the roar of the street, and eventually asked if I could pull in five hundred yuan a month doing this kind of work. I dodged the question. Yes, five hundred was technically possible—if you never took a sick day, if you never got slapped with a fine, if it never rained or snowed, and if you managed to hustle fares every single day of the month. I didn't tell them that I used to be a proud worker in a state-run factory. I couldn't bring myself to say the words "laid off."

When I pulled up to their stop, they handed me the cash and walked away without waiting for their change. I shouted "Thank you!" after them a few times, but they didn't look back. I knew they didn't leave a tip because I was a great driver. They left it because they felt sorry for me.

But pity is a double-edged sword for guys like me. If you refuse to get in my tricycle because you feel "bad" for me, or because you think having a grown man pedal you around is "degrading," you're not doing me a favor. You're starving me. We are selling our sweat and our muscle, not our emotional dignity. If you don't sit in the back, I don't get paid.

Tomorrow is my turn to legally hit the streets again. I'll have to wake up even earlier, pushing this heavy iron frame out into the dark, silent alleys long before the sun even thinks about coming up.
