# The Covenant

*2007*

## The Last Normal Day

A Friday morning, just past seven. The city sat under a dull haze. I pulled my jacket tighter and walked fast, head down against the wind. Early March should have been warming up, but the temperature this year was all wrong. A gust knifed through my collar and I shivered hard enough to rattle my teeth. *What the hell kind of weather is this? It's supposed to be spring.*

The weekend was close, but the pressure at work wasn't letting up. Our project was in the final sprint before delivery, the client kept changing requirements left and right, and the whole dev team was drowning. I'd pulled overtime until eleven the night before, dragged myself home, and now had to be back at the crack of dawn to deal with the latest avalanche of change requests. I ran through my mental bug list as I walked, trying to prioritize before I even reached my desk.

That's when my phone rang. The ringtone cut through the quiet path like a slap. I stopped and fished it out. The area code was American.

Had to be Xiaopeng. He'd flown to Boston last week to handle product delivery with the client. It would be around six in the evening there—he'd just gotten off work. If he was calling me at this hour, it probably meant another emergency change request.

I'd already entered the software park. Should I wait till I got to the office? No—I picked up. "Hello? Xiaopeng?"

"Qizhen! Something terrible is happening!" His voice was ragged, almost breathless. Behind him I could hear a chaos of shouting, wailing sirens, and the tinny echo of a public broadcast system. "Listen to me!" He sounded like he was barely holding it together.

My stomach clenched. "What's going on? What are they unhappy about now?"

"Forget the goddamn project!" he cut me off. "CBS is running an emergency disaster warning. Some kind of unprecedented atmospheric event. The experts are saying a global catastrophe could hit in the next few days, and they can't even say what kind!"

The sirens behind him were getting louder, like a mass evacuation was underway. I could hear screaming in the distance.

"It's chaos over here. People are panic-buying everything in sight. I just came back from the supermarket—the shelves are stripped bare, they're out of freaking toilet paper. The news is probably being suppressed back home. You need to go stockpile food and water right now, as much as you can carry. Stop screwing around!" His words tumbled over each other, his voice shaking.

I looked up and scanned the street. Everything was calm. A few pedestrians drifted past, oblivious. The high-rises faded into the morning mist. The sky was gray—maybe a shade gloomier than usual, but nothing more.

"Are you serious? A global disaster? Have you been bingeing too many Hollywood movies?" I said, trying to keep my voice light even as my pulse quickened. "If something that huge were happening, the internet would've exploded by now, right?"

"God damn it! Do you think I'm fucking joking?!" Xiaopeng practically screamed. "Look up! Does the sky look right to you? The sky here has turned green, and the clouds are spiraling. This is not normal!"

I tilted my head back. The sky did look darker than when I'd left the apartment. Storm clouds were massing on the horizon.

"One more thing—my wife's been sick, she's stuck at home. Please, bring her some food and water. I'm begging you, man." His voice cracked.

"Of course. I've got her covered, brother," I said, falling back on reflex humor to mask the fear crawling up my spine.

"I'm serious, Qizhen! This is not a joke!"

The sharpness in his voice hit me like ice water. I dropped the act. "All right. I hear you. I'm heading to the supermarket right now. You stay safe too."

A shriek of electronic feedback tore through the line—then dead silence. I stared at the blank screen. Environmental collapse. Global warming. Melting glaciers. Every doom-scrolling headline I'd ever skimmed suddenly felt less theoretical. My stomach went cold and heavy. Xiaopeng was right. We'd always known this was coming. We'd just never believed it would be today.

I sprinted into the building, taking the stairs two at a time. The lobby was deserted—just a security guard half-asleep in front of the monitors. I didn't stop to say hello. Swiped my card, stepped into the elevator, hammered the close button.

Eighth floor. I walked into the office and immediately spotted Monkey—I called her that as a nod to her surname, Hou, and her relentless tomboyish energy. She was wearing a fire-engine red plaid shirt that practically glowed against the beige cubicle walls. Good—she was in.

Hou Yanan had joined the company two years before me, the only woman in our department. But after years surrounded by a pack of nerds, she'd gone full tomboy—blunt, loud, and zero filter. She'd recently bought a Bummer X5 SUV (her self-deprecating nickname playing on the "Beemer"), making her the first programmer in our group to own a car. She never missed a chance to show it off—the smart headlights, the panoramic sunroof, every last feature—like she was running a one-woman auto show. Right now, though, what mattered was that Xiaopeng's apartment was far from the office, and if I needed to haul supplies, I needed that car.

Monkey was slouched in her ergonomic chair, warming her hands on a steaming cup of milk tea, scowling at a cascade of red "connection error" messages on her screen. I sidled up and put on my sweetest voice: "Oh great and powerful Monkey!"

She swiveled lazily, her two prominent front teeth appearing in a knowing grin. "Well, well. What's with the charm offensive? You eyeing my car again? Need a ride?"

No time for the usual dance. I went straight for it: "There might be a natural disaster in the next couple of days. We need to go to the supermarket right now and stock up."

"What?" She stared at me like I'd told her the moon was cheese. "Are you listening to Xiaopeng's garbage? My inbox was stuffed with his emails this morning—end of the world this, humanity is doomed that—the guy's lost it. And you're buying it?"

I grabbed the back of her chair to stop it swiveling. "This is real. I just had him on the phone. I heard the sirens myself—it's pandemonium over there. If we sit here much longer, we won't even get through the supermarket door."

She went still. I'd never been this serious even during the worst production outage. She set down her milk tea and searched my face, looking for the punchline. But I was pale, and there was no punchline to find. Her expression shifted. "Seriously? What the hell happened?"

I let go of her chair and pointed at the window. "Look outside."

She walked over and yanked the blinds up. The sky had darkened dramatically since I'd been downstairs. Roiling black clouds churned like ink dropped into water. The high-rises on the horizon looked like they were swaying. Pedestrians below were walking faster, heads ducked. She frowned. "Okay... that looks like one hell of a storm coming. I'll have to wash the car tomorrow."

"Are you coming or not?!" I was bouncing on my heels.

"It's work hours! If the manager catches us sneaking out, there goes this month's bonus!" She kept glancing at the elevator, as if our boss might materialize at any second.

"The world might be ending and you're worried about a bonus?" I said, exasperated. "Besides, R&D has flex hours. Nobody cares if you roll in late after a night of coding."

"You think I was born yesterday?" She rolled her eyes. "Our manager is a bloodsucking tyrant. He'll use any excuse to dock pay, and flex hours only flex in his direction." She turned back to her screen and typed in the URL for the online news forums. "Network connection timeout." Same as every other site.

I was losing patience. Time for the nuclear option. We were close enough that I'd loaned her fifty thousand yuan to help buy that car. "Pay me back right now, or drive me to the supermarket. Pick one."

That landed. Her face went through about four emotions in two seconds. She glared daggers at me, jaw tight. "God, you're petty." Then she spun around, snatched her coat off the chair, grabbed the keys from the desk. "Fine. I never said I wasn't going. Let's go, let's go, let's go."

## Tremors

Monkey complained the entire elevator ride: "If I lose this month's bonus, it's on you. I'm deducting it from what I owe." I barely heard her. My mind was already at the supermarket.

The elevator chimed open. Her black Bummer X5 sat directly opposite the doors, parked in the most conspicuous spot in the garage—naturally. She hit the unlock button. The headlights winked twice with a chirp.

"Get in!" She threw herself into the driver's seat. I dropped into the passenger side and buckled up. She turned the key, and the engine rumbled to life, the sound bouncing off the concrete walls of the empty garage like a caged animal trying to get out. I could feel the vibration through the seat. Then the Bummer lurched out of the parking space with all the grace of a drunk rhinoceros. I'd never paid much attention to this particular model before, but it seemed like every drop of fuel went straight to making noise.

Once we merged onto the outer ring expressway, the wind picked up fast, shoving the car sideways in the lane. The sky dropped like a lid—heavy, leaden, oppressive. The radio cut to static mid-song: a vicious hiss of white noise that made my teeth ache. I grabbed the tuning knob and scrolled through the dial. Every station was the same—bursts of crackling interference, occasionally broken by fragments of garbled speech that could have been coming from the next city or another century.

"What's wrong with the radio?" I kept twisting.

Monkey glanced out her window. "Electromagnetic interference, probably. Weather's going haywire."

Then the sky to our right split open.

There's no other way to describe it. An enormous mass of black cloud began to rotate, slowly at first, then faster and faster, like water circling a drain. A funnel descended from the center—thick, black, twisting—and touched down. The roar hit us a second later: a deep, grinding bellow that vibrated in my chest and made the dashboard rattle. Trees, cars, chunks of roofing—everything in its path was ripped up, shredded, and flung aside like confetti.

"Monkey, look!" I was pointing out the window, practically climbing out of my seat. "A tornado! A real tornado! I've only ever seen these on TV—this is insane! Can you get closer?"

She spared one white-knuckled glance, then snapped her eyes back to the road. Her face was chalk-white, sweat beading along her hairline, arms trembling on the wheel. "Are you out of your mind? If that thing drifts this way, we're dead!"

But I was already fumbling for my phone, pressing it against the window, snapping photos like a man possessed. "Slow down a little! Just a little! If I get this on camera, we'll dine out on this story forever!"

"You've got a death wish..." she muttered, but—maybe it was curiosity, or maybe my insanity was infectious—she eased off the gas. The speedometer dropped from 190 to 130 km/h, and the tornado crept up on our right flank.

That's when the sky ahead decided to join the party. Three or four more funnels punched down from the clouds in quick succession, black tentacles thrashing across the highway. Cars were swatted into the air like toys, tumbling end over end before crashing down in heaps of crumpled metal. Thunder and lightning tore through the chaos, and the air reeked of ozone and burning.

"Holy shit! What the FUCK?!" Monkey screamed. She started swerving between lanes, flooring the gas, hunting for gaps between the columns of death. The Bummer rocked violently in the crosswind, tires shrieking against the asphalt.

"Left! Take the exit on the left!" I shouted, white-knuckling the grab handle above the door.

Then something new appeared in the rearview mirror. A green light—cold, faint, wrong. I twisted around to look through the rear window. A glowing sphere the size of a basketball was trailing us, hovering in mid-air, pulsing with a sickly green luminescence. What made my skin crawl was how steady it was. The wind was ripping everything else apart, but that thing hung motionless relative to us, locked on like a targeting system.

Monkey caught it in the mirror too. What little color she had left drained from her face. "What... what is that thing?"

"Ball lightning!" The term surfaced from some half-remembered article. "I read about it in *Science*—it's incredibly rare, rarer than tornadoes! I can't believe we're actually seeing one!" I swung my phone around to the back window.

Monkey did not share my enthusiasm. She mashed the gas pedal into the floor. The speedometer needle climbed past 160. But the sphere matched us effortlessly, glued to our tail no matter how fast we went. I watched it grow fractionally larger in the mirror and felt something cold settle into my gut. "Can't outrun it," I muttered. "We can't outrun it."

Then the sphere flared. A pulse of green light, blinding and sudden, and it swelled—basketball to beach ball to something nearly as wide as the car itself. The light painted everything in sick, electric green. It surged forward, vaulting over us in a single bound, and planted itself on the road ahead, blocking the lane. Then it charged straight at us like a falling star.

Monkey forgot I existed. She was gripping the wheel, eyes locked forward, hissing through her teeth: "Come on then. Come ON. You want a fight? I'll give you a fight—"

She cranked the wheel hard left. The car went sideways. Centrifugal force slammed me against the door. And the green sphere didn't swerve, didn't slow—it opened up like a mouth and swallowed us whole.

Everything went silent.

Not quiet. *Silent*—the absolute absence of sound. A thick, syrupy sensation flooded the car, as if the air had turned to gel. My body went impossibly heavy, then impossibly light, as though gravity had forgotten what it was supposed to be doing. Through the windshield, the highway was gone. In its place: light—warped, stretched, spiraling. The tornado, the sky, the guardrail—all of it pulled into long luminous threads, corkscrewing down into an endless green void.

My thoughts turned to molasses. Each one took seconds to form. I turned my head—it felt like moving through water—and looked at Monkey. She was frozen mid-scream, mouth wide open, the sound dragged out into a subsonic drone that seemed to last forever.

I tried to reach for her. My arm wouldn't move. Something invisible held it pinned.

The green light swelled until it was all there was—blinding, total, absolute. And then nothing.
## A World Away

Consciousness returned slowly, reluctantly, like a diver surfacing from deep water. No wind. No engine roar. Even the smell of burning was gone. I opened my eyes and blinked until the world sharpened into focus. I was still in Monkey's Bummer. The airbags hadn't deployed. The dashboard clock was dead. *Where am I?*

The car sat in the center of a massive hemispherical hall. The walls were beige, laced with a network of raised ridges that looked disturbingly organic—like the blood vessels on the inner lining of some enormous organ. Embedded among them were glowing nodes of various sizes, casting cold white light. The hall was packed with metal tables that resembled workbenches or operating consoles. On them sat a chaotic display of devices: familiar household items—TVs, laptops, a microwave with its door hanging off—alongside professional instruments, oscilloscopes, circuit boards, and bizarre contraptions whose purpose I couldn't begin to guess. Everything was in some state of disassembly, cables trailing across the floor like spilled intestines.

*What is this place? A junkyard?* I rubbed my stinging eyes and squinted at a few shapes moving in the distance. Three figures, walking upright. When I finally saw them clearly, a bolt of cold shot up my spine and locked my breath in my throat.

They were cockroaches.

No—that's what my brain defaulted to, but that's not quite right. They were three giant arthropods, nearly two meters tall, walking on their hind legs. The body plan was unmistakably that of the American cockroach—the segmented thorax, the vestigial wing cases, the swept-back antennae—but scaled up to a size that was physically impossible. The square-cube law popped into my head unbidden: insects this large couldn't support their own weight, couldn't breathe through spiracles, couldn't exist. But there they were.

Every step sent a dull vibration through the floor. Not the thud of biological mass—more like heavy hydraulic machinery cycling. Under the light, their glossy brown carapaces reflected a cold metallic sheen. Not the matte texture of chitin. The sheen of alloy. They didn't breathe. Occasional puffs of white vapor hissed from their joints.

Each had two pairs of arms—the upper pair thick and powerful, the lower pair slimmer and more dexterous. Their "hands" were unsettlingly human-like, with articulated digits capable of precise manipulation. One of them was deftly disassembling a laptop, separating the keyboard from the motherboard with surgical precision.

Their heads, though—those weren't cockroach heads at all. They looked more like an enlarged locust: a massive mouth ringed by layers of keratinized mouthparts that occasionally parted to reveal sharp inner teeth, grinding together with a sound that made my fillings ache. The heads sat directly on the thorax with almost no neck, which should have crippled their field of view. But they had enormous hemispherical compound eyes on either side, like two glass domes embedded in their skulls, each one big enough to cover a 360-degree panorama. They faintly reflected blue light—definitely compound eyes. No single eyeball could grow that large.

I thought of my tiny rented apartment. Coming home late at night, flipping the kitchen light on, seeing cockroaches of all sizes scattered across the floor, the stovetop, the walls. The way they'd freeze for a split second, then scatter into every crack with that horrible rustling speed. Right now, I would have given anything to find a crack to disappear into.

Monkey's head lolled against the headrest, a thin line of drool trailing from the corner of her mouth. I nudged her. "Monkey. Wake up."

She frowned without opening her eyes. "My Bummer..." she mumbled, groping blindly for the steering wheel. Finding it, she cracked one eye open, confirmed she was still in her car, and closed it again.

"Monkey. Look." I kept my voice low.

She opened her eyes. Looked at me. Looked forward. Her pupils blew wide. She pressed back into her seat, nostrils flaring, and I watched her lungs fill with enough air for a sixty-second scream. *Oh no.* I shot my hand out and clamped it over her mouth—but her front teeth got in the way, and a short, piercing "Ah!" slipped through my fingers before I could seal it.

The cockroaches heard it. The reflections in their compound eyes swiveled sharply, locking onto us. Two of them pivoted and started walking toward us. Their footsteps rang out in the empty hall—a rhythmic metallic scraping that set my teeth on edge.

One reached the car and lowered its head to peer through the window. That massive compound eye hovered ten centimeters from my face. I could see the individual hexagonal ommatidia adjusting their focus, tightening on me like a camera lens racking into macro mode.

*Bang.*

It struck the window with a forelimb that looked like an industrial hydraulic claw. The whole car rocked. The bulletproof glass held, but a web of cracks splintered across its surface.

Seeing the glass didn't shatter, it seemed to lose patience. It reached down with its thick upper arm and grabbed the door handle. A nauseating shriek of tearing metal—and the entire door was ripped off like tinfoil and tossed aside.

We were dragged out of the car like chickens headed for the chopping block. Its grip was cold, hard, and crushingly strong. I was fairly sure my shoulder was about to pop out of its socket.

Monkey went limp with terror, legs buckled, clinging to me and shaking uncontrollably.

One of the cockroaches produced a string of sounds from somewhere near the base of its thighs—a staccato rasping, like metal plates rubbing together, or high-frequency electronic interference. Harsh, grating, inhuman. When we just stood there frozen, it picked up a metal rod and prodded us toward the door.

We were herded out of the hall. A wall of muggy, fetid air hit us in the face. The setting sun bled across the horizon, staining the low-hanging clouds a bruised red. Occasional flashes of lightning stabbed through the overcast, briefly illuminating the devastated landscape below. We stood on a barren hilltop. Trees and bamboo lay scattered as if trampled by something enormous—root systems ripped from the soil, trunks snapped like matchsticks.

"This place..." I muttered, scanning the terrain. "It looks like Sheshan." Sheshan was the only real elevation in Shanghai—I'd stood on it dozens of times, looking out at the city skyline. But now there was no skyline. Just a wall of gray smog, impenetrable, swallowing every landmark. Deep within the haze, dim red lights pulsed—distant fires, or the glow of some massive machinery at work.

The evening breeze seemed to bring Monkey back to herself. She grabbed my sleeve. "Where... where the hell are we? Are we dead? Is this hell?" She paused. "Does hell have smog?"

I shook my head. My brain had nothing to offer. But I had to say something—she looked like she was one breath away from shattering. "We're not dead. We're... maybe we fell through some kind of time wormhole. This might be Earth, two hundred million years from now. The cockroaches evolved. They're in charge." I attempted a grin. "Probably my fault. Should've kept a cleaner kitchen."

She stared at me blankly. Her mouth opened, closed, opened again. No words came out. Tears slid down her cheeks in silence. She was thinking about her parents. I knew, because I was thinking about mine. We'd planned to call them tomorrow. Where were they now?

The cockroaches kept us moving south until we reached the edge of a valley. I looked down and my breath caught. The entire valley floor had been converted into a pig farm. Hundreds of pigs milled together, their uneasy grunts filling the air—sometimes low, sometimes high-pitched. The stench was overpowering. There were no visible fences, but the livestock stayed within the boundary as if held by an invisible wall. Energy fences, I guessed. Near the edge, a few fat pigs rooted in the dirt, fighting over scattered blue pellets of feed.

"It's over," Monkey whispered. Her legs gave out and she dropped to her knees. "This is their pigpen. They're going to keep us like livestock."

My throat closed. I couldn't speak for a moment. But judging from the cold light projecting from the compound eyes of the cockroaches behind us, things were probably worse than even she imagined.

The escorting cockroach hissed and jabbed my back with its stick.

"Don't worry," I said, hauling Monkey upright with everything I had. Even in this nightmare, some stubborn part of me clung to logic, any logic. "If they're keeping us as livestock... at least it means they won't kill us. Not yet."

## The Cage

We stopped before the entrance to a cave. The opening was five meters high, ten meters wide, blocked by a crude grille welded together from rusty steel rebar. The surrounding rock was jagged and broken, as if something had torn the hillside open by brute force. Through the gaps in the bars, I could see the interior was even more cavernous. People lay strewn across the floor—skeletal, ashen, their eyes flickering in the dim light like dying embers.

The smell hit me like a physical blow: excrement, sweat, rotting food, and something else—something that might have been despair itself, if despair had a scent. My stomach convulsed.

"Zzzt-zzzt-woo!" The lead cockroach let out a sound like a hacksaw biting into slate. It jabbed a pincer-limb toward a small door set into the grille. The message was clear.

Monkey opened her mouth to object. The cockroach's metal rod smashed into the rock beside us, sending stone chips spraying into my face. We didn't argue. We crawled through the door on our hands and knees.

*Clank.* The door locked behind us. The cockroaches turned and left, the grinding of their joints fading down the valley until it was swallowed by the dark.

The prisoners gathered slowly, drawn by the noise. In the fading sunset their faces were all shadow, ghostlike. Most of the eyes that found us were empty—numb, dead. A few held wariness, even hostility. And a very few still carried something faint that might have been hope, guttering like a candle in a draft. The crowd parted, and a young man in glasses stepped forward. His clothes were rags, his pale face smeared with grime, his lenses scratched and dull—but behind them, his eyes were knife-sharp.

"New arrivals?" His voice was rough, as if he'd forgotten how to use it.

Seeing another human being—an actual, thinking person—loosened something in my chest. "Where is this? Were you caught by those bugs too?"

"Yes."

"What year is it?" The question came out before I could think about whether I wanted the answer.

"2007."

"2007..." I muttered. "So much for time travel to the future..."

"July 28th, 2007," he added quietly. "I'm certain. I wouldn't misremember."

"July 28th—" I spun around and grabbed Monkey's shoulders. "Monkey! We time-traveled! We actually time-traveled! Remember? It was March 2nd—we were in your car—then the ball lightning hit us—and now it's five months later!"

Monkey's jaw dropped. Her system was clearly trying to process a stack overflow. She froze.

I turned back to the man with glasses, talking fast: "I know how this sounds, but we got hit by a green glowing sphere, and when we woke up—we were here. We came from March. From the past."

I expected shock, or at least skepticism. Instead, his tone was flat, as if I'd told him the weather forecast. "I believe you."

"You... believe me? Time travel? Just like that?"

A ghost of a smile crossed his lips. "Two reasons. First, after four months in here, there is literally nothing I can't believe anymore. Second—" he paused, "I was captured on March 2nd as well. Everyone here was. We've been in this hole for over four months."

Cockroaches the size of grown men were one thing. This was another. "Can you tell me what happened?"

He found a relatively clean rock to sit on and gestured for us to do the same. "My name is Zhou Anyu. I was a researcher at the Institute of Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences." He extended a grimy hand. "Welcome to hell."

I shook it. Cold, rough, and surprisingly strong.

"Ruan Qizhen. This is my colleague, Hou Yanan. We're software developers." I paused. "Dr. Zhou—since you're a physicist—do you have any idea what's going on? Are those bugs... aliens?"

Zhou Anyu settled onto the rock, his posture that of a man who had told this story before. "On March 2nd, I was flying back to Shanghai from Beijing—I'd just wrapped up an international seminar. As we were descending for landing, I looked out the window and saw something behind the clouds..."

He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, exactly, but the memory of fear.

"It was a ship. Enormous. Beyond anything I can convey with words. Our Boeing 737 was a rowboat beside an aircraft carrier. This was categorically not anything Earth's technology could produce. Everything I've ever studied, every physical model I've ever learned—none of it even begins to explain what I saw." His voice went flat. "It dove at our plane. I blacked out. When I woke up, I was here. Everyone in this cave has a similar story. Caught during that same window. I am as certain as I can be: this is not from Earth."

"So the aliens caused the tornadoes? The ball lightning? To wipe everyone out? And how did we skip five months?"

He nodded, shifting into what I recognized as lecture mode. "The storms, the tornadoes—I believe those were magnetohydrodynamic disturbances caused by the gravitational wake of that spacecraft engaging some form of warp drive or anti-gravity propulsion within Earth's atmosphere. Essentially, their engines created havoc as a side effect."

He paused, choosing his words. "As for the 'ball lightning' that hit you—it clearly wasn't normal plasma. Under general relativity, it may have triggered a severe local distortion of spacetime curvature. Imagine spacetime as a flat bedsheet. That sphere was a lead ball that punched a deep crater into the sheet, and you fell in."

"Inside that crater, time moved differently. An instant for you was months out here. You were essentially frozen on the edge of a miniature event horizon."

He gave his conclusion simply: "You fell into a crack in time. And you've just climbed out."

I maintained what I hoped was an expression of deep comprehension, nodding at appropriate intervals and murmuring "Mm." This was a reflex I'd perfected over years of watching managers deliver PowerPoint presentations: soul offline, face online.

When Dr. Zhou finally wrapped up his grand unified theory, the silence that followed was the kind that demands an intelligent follow-up question. I had none. But Monkey, bless her, cut through the awkward pause with the question that actually mattered: "So what do those giant bugs want with us? They're not going to... eat us, are they?"

From a dark corner, a woman curled into a ball let out a hollow laugh. "Eat? That'd be the merciful option. They don't eat humans. They use us. Batteries. Livestock. Cheap labor."

Low murmurs rippled through the crowd. Monkey's hand tightened on the hem of my shirt.

"She's right," Zhou Anyu said. "We are the labor force. The pigs are the food supply. We feed the pigs, build structures, haul materials. As long as you can work, you stay alive. When you can't..." He didn't finish.

"In the three months before you arrived," he added, his voice dropping, "you two are the only new prisoners. The humans outside..." A long pause. "I don't think there are many left."

Nobody spoke for a while after that.

I forced a breath. "Have any of you tried to escape?"

The effect was immediate. The murmuring cut off. People looked at me as if I'd pulled a pin from a grenade, then scattered toward the back of the cave.

Outside the grille, the last sliver of sun sank behind distant bamboo. The sky went black—no moon, no stars, nothing. I could just make out the shapes of two cockroaches standing watch. Inside the cave, waves of stench rolled up from the depths. On the rock ceiling, dim lights drifted and pulsed. They weren't fixed—they crawled, slowly, like living things, throwing shifting shadows against the walls that warped and twisted in ways that made my skin prickle.

I found a spot near the entrance where the air was slightly less suffocating and decided to settle in. Only then did I realize I was drenched in sweat and still wearing my jacket. I peeled it off—filthy, torn in several places—and since the temperature clearly didn't call for it, I shook it out and spread it on the ground as a makeshift bed. I curled up on it. Monkey pressed close beside me, still trembling.

That night, I didn't sleep. Zhou Anyu's words orbited my skull on an endless loop. The only comfort I could find was that my parents were probably safe. If this was an alien invasion, Shanghai—a massive, high-value target—would have been hit first. Xinhao Village, my hometown in Keshan County, was a cluster of a few dozen households. The aliens probably didn't even know it existed. I should've stayed home and farmed. Monkey's hometown, Jiagedaqi, bordered the northern forests—good for hiding. If we could get out of here and make it home, we might be okay.

Monkey had calmed down somewhat, but she still twitched in her sleep. Outside, the night was punctuated by the distant howls of wild animals and a sound like helicopter rotors—the cockroaches, flying in the dark. It wasn't until the first gray light touched the eastern sky that my consciousness finally blurred, and I drifted into something that was neither sleep nor waking.
## The Ant Farm

A sharp hammering shattered the pre-dawn silence. I jolted awake. Outside the grille, there were no cockroaches—just seven or eight burly men with black metal clubs, their polished surfaces catching the first gray light. "Get up! All of you, get the fuck up!" They beat the bars as they shouted, the clash of metal on metal ringing through the valley until my eardrums throbbed.

These were our overseers—human collaborators, the people every prisoner feared most. We called them "grubs," after cockroach larvae. The first to earn the title was a thug named Scarface Li, a petty gang boss on the outside. Word was, within days of being thrown into the cave, he'd already muscled a few toadies into line and crowned himself king of the pit.

One day, Scarface Li stumbled onto a group secretly plotting an escape. He went straight to the cockroaches. Somehow they understood him. The escapees were rounded up within hours. Afterward, Scarface Li beat them to death with his own hands—a loyalty demonstration. The cockroaches rewarded him: he was allowed to leave the cave and move into a cleaner cabin on the hilltop.

His lackeys soon followed. A few of the more attractive female prisoners were forced to join them. From that point on, Scarface Li and his crew were fully domesticated—attack dogs for the alien masters, more vicious than the cockroaches themselves.

The grubs got better food, better quarters, and access to basic alien technology—walkie-talkies, stun batons—which they wore like badges of authority. In exchange, they ensured the rest of us met our daily work quotas and crushed any hint of defiance.

On the construction site, the grubs were worse than the cockroaches. At least the cockroaches were cold and predictable. The grubs took pleasure in it. They prowled through the work crews with stun batons, looking for excuses. When they found one—or invented one—the *crack* of the baton was followed by screams that echoed across the hilltop and hung in the air long after.

A tall, gaunt grub stopped in front of Monkey and me. He looked us over with the lazy contempt of a cat deciding which mouse to play with first, then raised his baton and flicked the switch. A blue-white arc snapped to life at the tip, dancing and hissing. In the flat morning light, the spark turned his hollow face into something out of a horror film.

"Hey, you two—move your asses! Don't think you can play dumb with me." He waved the baton inches from my face. I could feel the heat, smell the ozone. I had zero doubt that if it actually touched me, I wouldn't just get a burn—I'd be cooked from the inside.

The prisoners filed out of the cave like a parade of the walking dead—shuffling, expressionless, hollow-eyed—every step seeming to drain the last of their strength. Monkey and I fell in silently, following the procession to a spacious, half-finished hall on the north slope.

At the center was a podium. Two cockroaches stood on it. A bloated grub—the "Translator"—waddled up to them and produced a few credible *zzzt-creak* sounds in the cockroach language. I did a double-take. The man had actually learned to speak cockroach. After the cockroaches turned and left, the Translator wiped his forehead and scurried over to Scarface Li, whispering something with his head bowed.

Scarface Li hauled himself onto the podium on short, stubby legs, resting his hands on his protruding gut. His bloated face was split in an obsequious grin, yellow teeth on full display. A knife scar ran from the corner of his left eye to his chin, twisting grotesquely as he smiled. He opened his mouth and produced a sound that was clearly a deliberate imitation of the cockroaches—nails on a chalkboard: "Fellow villagers! The Swarm is truly benevolent to us! They have brought food so we won't starve to death! We must know how to be grateful! Serving the Locust Army is our natural blessing!"

He paused, rubbing his greasy palms together, his eyes slithering across the crowd like something cold-blooded, tongue occasionally flicking out to wet his lips.

Dead silence. The only response was scattered coughing. Scarface Li didn't seem to care. He waved a hand as if shooing flies and ordered his men to distribute "breakfast."

Each of us received a large handful of blue pellets that gave off a faint fishy, musty smell. I recognized them immediately—they were the same feed I'd seen at the pig farm. Cupping this handful of animal feed, my stomach heaved.

A burly middle-aged man squeezed in next to me. He looked like he hadn't slept in a week—sunken eyes, hollowed cheeks. "Force it down," he whispered. "One meal a day. Skip it and you won't last." He swallowed hard, as if steeling himself, then grabbed a fistful and started chewing. His face twisted with every bite.

"Zhang Tieshan," he said. "Before all this, I managed a warehouse at the local supercenter."

I hesitated, then picked up a few pellets and put them in my mouth. They had no flavor—just a rough, gritty texture, hard as rubber, and every swallow scraped my throat like sandpaper. For the first few days, I gagged every time. But hunger is a ruthless teacher. Before long, I was numb to it, treating the feed as nothing more than fuel to keep my body moving one more day.

The days that followed blurred together into an unbroken nightmare. Monkey was assigned to the pig farm—feeding the animals, shoveling excrement. I was kept on the north slope with the majority of the workforce. It was a barren plateau above the valley, where the half-built skeletons of bizarre structures jutted up like the bones of alien cathedrals. Each building was some impossible geometric shape—folded planes, spiraling columns, angles that seemed to shift when you looked at them sideways—as if designed by an intelligence that didn't think in rectangles.

We had no machinery. Just our hands, crude bamboo tools, and brute force. Under the grubs' batons, we lashed bamboo poles together and hoisted skeletal frameworks higher and higher. Some sections required climbing bare-handed to twenty meters or more, with no harness, no safety net, nothing between us and the ground but air. Every ascent was a roll of the dice.

As a programmer, my most strenuous daily exercise had been hammering a keyboard. My body was pathetically unfit for this. The labor nearly killed me in the first week. Old Zhang saved me—sharing my workload when the grubs weren't looking, teaching me how to pace myself and stay out of their spotlight.

After the frames went up, we sprayed a thick, faintly sweet yellow liquid onto the bamboo strips from pressurized cans. Within hours, a plant resembling lichen would bloom across the surface—clinging, spreading, filling every gap. It had an almost terrifying vitality: in just days, it could coat an entire building in a dense, hardened shell. By controlling how much we sprayed—less where growth was too thick, more where the aliens wanted a specific contour—we sculpted these alien structures in the crudest way imaginable.

That first half-month was the longest of my life. I witnessed a man fall. One second he was alive—reaching for a crossbeam—and the next he was a crumpled shape on the ground below. The grubs dragged his body away like garbage. None of us spoke about it afterward. We didn't need to. The message was clear: death was not an event here. It was an ambient condition.

One morning, I climbed to the top of the scaffolding and looked out. Smog sealed the horizon in every direction. The old Shanghai skyline was gone—no Lujiazui, no Oriental Pearl Tower—just forest, unbroken and dense. *When did Shanghai get this much greenery?* I thought absently. I turned back to my work, tapping my hammer in a lazy rhythm, and started watching the grubs below. They were drifting around the open ground, barely paying attention.

After about an hour of observation, I leaned toward Old Zhang on the next beam. "Notice anything?" I whispered. "They only put on a show twice a day—morning and evening, when the cockroaches come to inspect. The rest of the time, they slack off just as much as we do. There are less than ten of them and over a hundred of us. I just watched someone slip into the woods. The grubs didn't even blink."

Old Zhang knew exactly what I was getting at. He shook his head. "Don't even think about it. See the pig farm down there? See any fences? No. Because they're invisible—touch one and you get electrocuted until your skin splits. We're surrounded by the same thing. We just don't know where ours are hidden. Everyone who's tried to escape got caught. Scarface Li executed them personally. It wasn't pretty."

"I won't let them catch me," I said.

Old Zhang sighed and said nothing more.

That night, back in the cave, the stench was as suffocating as ever—sweat, decay, and the sour undertone of hopelessness. Overhead, pale "lantern-slugs" clung to the rock ceiling. Their bulbous bodies occasionally extended thin tentacles that crept along like worms before dropping suddenly, casting erratic, dancing shadows that made the cave walls look alive.

People lay scattered across the floor like discarded rag dolls—some curled tight and trembling, others flat on their backs, motionless, indistinguishable from corpses.

I leaned against the cold wall. Monkey crept over and slipped me a fistful of feed she'd stolen from the pig farm. My eyes stung. I glanced at a hollow in the ground nearby—the dead man's sleeping spot, now just cold, empty rock.

"Monkey..." My voice came out barely above a whisper. "I don't know how much longer I can do this. One of these days I'll go up to the construction site and not come back."

Something broke. The terror and despair she'd been holding down by sheer willpower came flooding out. She threw herself against my shoulder, body shaking, words pouring out between sobs: "I can't take it anymore! This place isn't fit for humans! It's been two weeks—I smell like a sewer and haven't had a bath! We don't even have toilet paper! Is this a life? I might as well climb up to that scaffolding tomorrow and just... jump. End it."

My chest tightened. I put my arm around her trembling shoulders, wanting to say something comforting, something wise. But I'd never been good at this. I was fine with jokes. Comfort was not in my skillset. After a long, painful silence, I managed: "Well... look on the bright side. The walls in here are always wet. You can wash your hands whenever you want."

## A Glimmer

That, predictably, made her cry harder. The other prisoners paid no attention—other people's grief had long ceased to be interesting. But our corner still drew a few curious glances. I was floundering when a familiar figure materialized out of the shadows: Old Zhang.

"Knock it off." He scanned the area quickly, then dropped his voice. Something flickered in his expression—not quite excitement, but close. "We might have a chance. I found something. Come with me."

Monkey stopped mid-sob and roughly wiped her eyes. Two weeks of shared misery had forged an unshakeable trust between us and Old Zhang. In this world, he was our anchor.

"What did you find?" I asked, keeping my voice low.

"Just follow me. And keep quiet." He turned and headed deeper into the cave, moving with the practiced silence of a cat. Monkey and I traded a look, then followed.

The depths of the cave were a labyrinth—branching passages twisting in every direction, each one darker and narrower than the last. The air grew colder and wetter as we went. The faint glow of the lantern-slugs thinned out, and the side tunnels gaped like open mouths, pitch-black and bottomless. The walls were coated in something cold and slick, faintly phosphorescent—whether natural mineral or alien secretion, I didn't want to know.

After a couple of minutes, Old Zhang stopped at a hidden alcove. The air here was even damper, the walls veined with cracks. He pointed to a narrow gap in the rock and gestured for us to squeeze through. Inside was a small chamber, its floor littered with broken circuit boards and machine parts.

"What's all this?" I crouched down and picked up a circuit board, examined it, put it back.

Old Zhang wore the satisfied look of a man holding all the cards. He leaned in and whispered, conspiratorially: "Just stumbled on this myself. All of it was stashed here by that kid Zhou Anyu. Now, you don't need to be a genius to figure out what he's up to—he's planning something, probably an escape. I don't know the details, but think about it—what happens if we report this to the cockroaches? We'd get the VIP treatment. Maybe even get out of this cave. No more suffering."

I stared at him. For a moment, I couldn't process what I was hearing. Then a wave of disgust and fury rose so fast it nearly choked me.

"Old Zhang." My voice went cold. "Whatever you think of Zhou Anyu, he's a prisoner. Same as us. If you hand him over to those things, you know exactly what happens to him. Yes, I want to get out of here—I want it so badly I can taste it. But I want to walk out of here as a human being. Not crawl out as someone's pet. You want to trade a man's life for a warmer bunk? Is that really who you are?"

His face went through shades of white and green. His eyes darted away from mine. Then the defensive mask slid back into place. He snorted. "Fine. Cast pearls before swine, see if I care. I was showing you a way out, and you throw it back in my face. Suit yourself. Pretend you never saw me." He turned away and fixed his gaze on a lantern-slug creeping across the wall, jaw set.

Monkey tugged my sleeve. "Let's just go," she murmured. "Before this gets worse."

I exhaled, knowing she was right. I took her hand and turned toward the exit—only to find Zhou Anyu blocking the entrance, two men flanking him. His eyes took in the three of us, the scattered parts on the floor, and went hard as flint.

"What are you doing here?" Ice in every syllable.

His gaze swept the debris, and his expression darkened further. "You're going to sell me out?"

Before I could answer, his two men stepped forward—fists clenched, ready to go. The tension in the narrow chamber was instant and suffocating.

I glanced back at Old Zhang. He was doing his best impression of a wooden post—back turned, face to the wall, studying the lantern-slug with the fascination of a man who had absolutely nothing to do with any of this. The sight of him made my blood boil, but this wasn't the time.

I turned back to Zhou Anyu and forced what I hoped was a disarming smile. "Nobody's selling anyone out. The three of us couldn't sleep. We went for a walk, wandered in here by accident, saw some junk on the floor—that's it. We're leaving. Right now."

I pulled Monkey toward the exit, but Zhou Anyu and his men made no move to let us pass. They filled the gap like sentinels. Zhou Anyu's expression shifted—from hostility to something more calculating. "Don't rush off. Since you're already here... let's talk."

He steered me to a protruding rock and sat me down. His two men took positions on either side of the entrance—human door bolts.

Zhou Anyu sighed, and when he spoke again, his voice was different. Quieter. Almost apologetic. "Ruan—do you remember? The day you arrived, you asked us why nobody had tried to escape. The truth is..." He paused, scanning the three of us as if gauging how much to reveal. "Of course we want to escape. Every single person in that cave does. But who dares say it out loud? The ones who tried before—every last one was betrayed. Caught. Killed. We're terrified of each other."

Another pause, his eyes moving between Monkey and me. "But I've been watching you. Both of you. And I've decided you can be trusted."

I frowned. "Watching us? What does that mean?"

I looked at Old Zhang. He was suddenly very interested in his own feet, guilt written across his face. "I told him this wasn't necessary!" he blurted, waving his hands. "I trust you completely! But Dr. Zhou insisted on testing you first—said it was just a precaution. I had nothing to do with it!"

So the whole thing—the "discovery," the betrayal pitch—had been a test. Old Zhang, with his guileless face, had been playing a role. I couldn't tell which of his words over the past two weeks had been real and which had been scripted. But the bigger picture was suddenly obvious. I dropped the pretense and looked Zhou Anyu in the eye. "How can we help?"

Relief flickered across his face. "About the test—that was my fault. I apologize. But now that everything's in the open, we're on the same side." He gestured to his two men. "This is Feng, and this is Dong. The five of us—including Old Zhang—have been planning an escape for months. But dealing with aliens is a different order of magnitude. Nobody has ever gotten out alive. I have ideas, but we're missing critical skills." He looked at me with sudden intensity. "Old Zhang tells me one of you studied cryptography in college and the other studied electrical engineering. And that you both worked R&D at a telecom company. Those are exactly the skills we need."

*After the world ends, my résumé is finally relevant.* I kept the thought to myself. Zhou Anyu could see the skepticism on my face. "Come," he said. "Let me show you what I've been building."

The chamber we were in turned out to be just a decoy. Zhou Anyu led us deeper, through increasingly slippery passageways. The air grew cold and damp, and water dripped from the ceiling onto my shoulders. The lantern-slugs thinned until the branching tunnels were nothing but black voids.

"How deep does this cave go?" I asked.

"Not very," Feng answered, his voice tight with frustration. "At a slow walk, you hit the end in five minutes. The problem is the forks. There are at least a dozen ways you can go, and I've tried every single one. Dead ends. All of them."

We followed a winding passageway, ignoring several forks, until we reached a hidden cavern. A massive stone slab blocked the entrance. Feng and Dong leaned into it, muscles straining, and shoved it aside. Blinding white light instantly flooded out.

## Unlikely Allies

Zhou Anyu's workspace was much larger than the antechamber, but it was absolute chaos. Gutted and jury-rigged electronics littered the floor, and the walls were obscured by a dense, spiderweb tangle of wiring. In the corner sat dilapidated crates overflowing with unrecognizable components. Above us, the ceiling was a solid sheet of lantern-slugs, clustered so tightly their combined glow was almost glaring.

"Why are they all swarming in here?" I asked.

"Normally, they eat the microorganisms on the rock," Zhou Anyu explained, crossing to a corner. He grabbed a handful of bamboo splinters from a pile and tossed them into an open crate. Instantly, a harsh crackle, like an electrical short, snapped from the box. "But I realized they really like the nutrient fluid we use for the buildings. I stole some and sprayed it in here. Drew them right to us."

"These are spark-bugs," he said, patting the crate. "They feed on bamboo. Hook them up to a stabilized circuit, and they provide a steady current. It's the same kind of bug they put in those stun batons Scarface Li's crew carries."

"You took apart a stun baton?" I asked.

Zhou Anyu ignored the question. "No matter how vast their resources are, they couldn't have brought everything they need for interstellar travel. My guess is they brought seeds and eggs—things that reproduce rapidly in a new environment—and maybe a handful of machinery. To build colonies on this scale, they have to rely on Earth's raw materials. The lantern-slugs, the spark-bugs, the plants we're building with—they're all alien organisms."

"What about the cockroaches?" I pressed. "Are those from eggs, too?"

His expression turned grim. "That's the real question. They're the biggest obstacle we have. I've been studying them for a long time, and they exceed everything I thought possible. Physically, it would take five Old Zhangs to take one down. Intellectually... they crossed galaxies to get here. Their technology and intelligence are beyond our comprehension. And they can fly. We don't see it often, but the last time someone tried to run, I watched them spread their wings and chase him down. They caught him effortlessly."

He paused, conflict warring in his eyes—fear and resolve. "Do you think we stand a chance of just forcing our way out bare-handed? We don't. Zero chance. Our only hope is to find their weakness and hit them where they're vulnerable."

"So..." I leaned forward. "Have you found one?"

He shook his head, exhaustion bleeding through. "Nothing direct. Nothing we can exploit right away. But I have noticed something very strange. Have you looked at them closely? They all look exactly the same."

"Aren't all cockroaches ugly?" Monkey chimed in. "They're pitch black. You can't exactly tell the males from the females or check their eyelids."

"No, I mean *exactly* the same," Zhou Anyu insisted, his voice heavy. "Same height, same width, same color. Even the patterns on their carapaces match down to the millimeter. It's unnatural. Any biological creature has individual variations. Look at the lantern-slugs above us—there are subtle differences in size, brightness, color. That's how biology works."

Monkey caught his drift. "You're saying... those roaches aren't the real aliens? Someone's pulling their strings?"

"Exactly." Zhou Anyu's eyes hardened. "I suspect they're tools. Vehicles. I've narrowed it down to three possibilities. One, they're spacesuits, and the real aliens are hiding inside. Two, they're transport craft, carrying multiple aliens inside like a crew cabin. Or three—and this is what I'm leaning toward—they're remotely controlled drones. The real aliens might be hiding somewhere else, sending signals to control them."

Excitement crept into his voice, the words spilling out faster. "If we can figure out what they really are, we have a chance. If they're drones, for instance, we just need to find and sever their communication link. We could immobilize them. Paralyze the entire swarm."

"How do we even start to figure that out?" I asked.

Zhou Anyu smiled. "I have a theory. To understand what they are, we need to understand how they communicate. Do you know how they talk to each other?"

I thought back to the few interactions I'd witnessed. "The clicking? The low humming? I've seen them rub their legs together to make noise."

He shook his head, looking pleased. "That's how they communicate with us and other Earth creatures. I've analyzed those sounds. The syntax is incredibly basic. It's fine for barking orders at the grubs, but it's completely inadequate for complex communication among themselves."

He looked between us. "That's why I need you. You know this field. If we can crack their communication protocols and map their activity patterns, we have a real shot at getting out of here."

"But with what?" I looked around the chaotic cavern. "We don't even have basic equipment."

Zhou Anyu grinned, practically beaming with pride. "Feng, Dong. Bring it out."

Footsteps echoed as the two men hauled out several crates tangled in wiring, followed by a Frankenstein monster of a computer. Feng flipped a switch. The screen flickered to life, illuminating the dark rock with complex waveform curves and signal strength graphs.

"Where..." Monkey stared at it, utterly floored. "Where did you get a computer?" Finding a PC in an alien slave camp was like finding a rabbit on the moon.

"Garbage," Zhou Anyu said proudly. "We scavenged all of this from the outside while carrying bamboo. The roaches throw it away. The dump is full of equipment. I've been sneaking parts back and repairing them piece by piece."

"They throw away garbage?" I asked, stunned.

He nodded. "They never stop working. Every night they go out scavenging human technology. Sometimes you hear that sound like a helicopter—that's them flying. During the day, unless they're patrolling, they stay in that massive central building. I think it's a lab, or a factory. They dismantle our appliances, take the components they need, and dump the rest. Sometimes they even toss their own semi-finished equipment. The power supply we're using for the spark-bugs is built from their scraps. I doubt they ever considered we might use their trash against them."

He pointed to the workbench. "Look at what we've got: a regulated power supply, a multimeter, an oscilloscope. The sweep generator is still busted, but I think I can fix it. It's crude, but it's a start. So? Are you in?"

My heart hammered against my ribs. This was it. An actual chance. "Absolutely," I said without hesitation. "Getting out of here is the only thing that matters. But I do have a suggestion." I paused, gathering my thoughts. "Researching their communication network is critical, but it's going to be a massive undertaking. We might not get a breakthrough anytime soon. We should start with something more immediate. The invisible fences. If we're going to run, that's our first hurdle."

"The fences?" Zhou Anyu raised an eyebrow, clearly intrigued. "Go on."

"The technology they use to pen us in is probably similar to what they use at the pig farm, just scaled up," I explained. "Higher power, wider range, better concealment. Monkey works at the farm. She can observe the boundaries, watch how the pigs react, and track any maintenance schedules. If we can figure out how the perimeter works, we can find a way through." I added, "Target detection is much simpler than alien communication protocols. We're more likely to see quick results."

"That's a solid plan," Zhou Anyu agreed, his eyes lighting up. "We'll do both. Prioritize the fences, then focus on their comms."

"We'll need gear," I said. "Infrared or ultraviolet sensors, or something to detect electromagnetic fields. Can you build that?"

Zhou Anyu thought for a moment. "Theoretically. But parts are tight. It might take time, and it won't be highly precise."

"That's fine," I said. "We'll start with lo-fi testing. It's late. Monkey and I will head back and hash out a plan. We'll brief everyone tomorrow."

## Prison Without Bars

On the walk back to our corner, Monkey kept her head down, chewing her lip. "I don't know about this," she muttered finally. "What if they're using some crazy sci-fi tech? If we can't even comprehend how it works, we're just flying blind."

"I know," I said. "If they're using quantum entanglement or neutrinos or some energy transmission we haven't discovered yet, we're screwed. But we don't have a choice. We have to start with what we know."

I kept walking. "But I think we're on the right track. Why do they scavenge our garbage every night?"

She looked up, confused. "Why?"

"Because they traveled light," I said. "They're building massive infrastructure across the planet, and that requires raw materials. If they manufactured every single component from scratch, it would take forever. They're using Earth's existing tech to bridge the gap. And if they're using our components, their final products have to share some underlying principles with our technology. That gives us a window."

She nodded slowly. "Makes sense."

"And right now, we're just looking at the effects," I added. "No matter how advanced the fence is, it has a physical manifestation. If we observe what it does, we can reverse-engineer how it works."

Her shoulders relaxed a fraction. "Alright. Then tomorrow, I'm finding that fence."

"Start simple," I said. "Test if it's visual, motion-based, or pressure-sensitive."

The next morning, I was back on the scaffolding. My assignment was demolition. Another work crew had finished a structure the week before, but it hadn't met the roaches' exact specifications, so we were tearing it down to start over. It gave me the perfect vantage point to watch the pig farm.

The valley floor was laid out below me. Striking blue lines were painted across the dirt—warnings for the breeders marking the perimeter of the invisible fence.

Even from up high, the stench of the farm was overpowering—a putrid mix of offal and fermenting feed. The constant squealing and grunting of the pigs drifted up like a deranged symphony. Down below, Monkey was wielding a massive, ragged broom lashed together with bamboo strips. She slipped through the bamboo gate—the only physical part of the enclosure—and walked toward the blue line on the eastern edge.

She stopped just short of the churned dirt. Rearing back, she violently swept the ground, kicking up a cloud of dust and dried manure.

Nothing happened.

She frowned, then cautiously extended the broom handle toward the line, probing the air like she was checking the depth of a river. The wood passed right through. Still nothing. She paced the length of the blue line, grabbed a shit-caked rock, and hurled it straight into the restricted zone. It landed with a dull thud.

Still nothing.

Monkey scratched her head, thoroughly stumped. She moved to the far side to try again. I kept watching the pigs. They knew better than to go near the blue paint, sticking to the safe zones to root for spilled feed.

I was about to give up when a fight broke out. A massive boar was aggressively shoving a smaller pig, kicking up dirt as they wrestled. The smaller pig was losing ground, squealing as it backed up. It didn't realize how close it was to the boundary.

With a soft *snap*, its hind hoof crossed the blue line.

A jagged blue spark materialized out of thin air, whipping into the pig's rump like a snake strike. The animal shrieked, launched itself forward as if it had been branded, and scrambled away, a small patch of bristles smoking and charred black.

"Not much of a kick to it, is there?"

I jumped. Old Zhang was perched on a crossbeam a few feet away, methodically tapping a hammer against the wood like a monk with a singing bowl.

"Just a warning," he said, not looking at me. "Don't underestimate it. If that pig hadn't backed off, the voltage would have spiked. A while back, a pig panicked and charged straight through. Roasted to a crisp before it hit the ground. Smelled incredible. The whole site smelled like barbecue for hours." He licked his lips.

That night, we reconvened in the cavern. Based on Monkey's tests, we ruled out light waves and pressure sensors. We needed to test for sound, electromagnetic fields, or static electricity. And we still couldn't rule out the worst-case scenario: a hidden camera network.

"I saw the spark," I told Zhou Anyu. "If it's an electrical discharge, there has to be an electric field. Can you modify your multimeter into a portable detector?"

He nodded slowly. "I can try. Give me two days."

He didn't need two days. By early morning, he handed Monkey a crude but functional electric field detector. He'd taken an old analog multimeter, swapped in a highly sensitive microammeter, and attached a bamboo pole wrapped in copper wire as a probe. If the world hadn't ended, Zhou Anyu would be making millions in Silicon Valley.

That evening, the moment we got back to our sleeping area, Monkey grabbed my arm, vibrating with excitement. "I got it! The signal near the fence was screaming. It's definitely electricity."

"How strong?" I asked.

She pulled out a flat piece of bamboo, numbers scratched into the surface with charcoal. "Look. At about a meter out from the blue line, the reading spiked. The closer you get, the stronger it is. I didn't push it—I'm not looking to get fried. But it's there." She tapped another number. "And look at this one. I chased a pig into the fence. The needle pinned out immediately. Then *snap*, blue spark, and the pig came running back with a smoking ass."

She leaned in. "It's a biological radar. When we move, our muscles and nerves generate electricity. The fence picks up the voltage change and hits you with a high-voltage shock. It'll cripple you, or at least knock you out cold."

With the mechanism identified, the work accelerated. Every night in the hidden cavern, we mapped the data Monkey collected while Zhou Anyu built more sensitive detectors from scavenged parts. He handed them out to Feng, Dong, and the others, sending them out to map the main fences holding us in the valley. The perimeter grid was far more concealed than the pig farm, and the voltage was lethal. The few prisoners who'd accidentally touched it had been permanently paralyzed.

One night, Monkey was idly tracing patterns in the dirt when she looked up. "Hey. Why don't the roaches get shocked when they cross the line?"

I leaned back against the damp rock. "They must have a bypass. Maybe they wear shielded gear, or maybe they just have a remote to turn it off."

"Exactly," she whispered, her eyes gleaming. "I've been thinking about this. Why do they only go scavenging late at night? And why is the garbage dump *inside* the perimeter? I'll bet you anything they cut the power at night so they can haul their junk in without getting zapped. During the day, they turn it on to keep us penned. And they dump the garbage inside because they're too lazy to haul it back out."

"Where are you going with this?" I asked.

She leaned in close. "Can we sneak out in the dark?"

I sighed, hating to crush the hope in her eyes. "Old Zhang said people tried that. They hid out during the day and tried to run at night. But the roaches have night vision. They can see in the dark perfectly. And they post guards at the cave entrance every night. Plus, we're practically blind in the dark. We have to move during the day."

"The only good thing about the night," I added, "is that the grubs are locked in their barracks too."

She frowned. "How does Old Zhang know so much about the grubs? You think he's trying to sell us out?"

"No," I said firmly, patting her shoulder. "If Old Zhang was going to turn traitor, he would have sold Zhou Anyu out weeks ago. He's solid."

While Feng and the others mapped the perimeter, I focused on the RF spectrum analyzer. During the day, I looked for any excuse to scout the construction site for clues.

## Smoke and Mirrors

After breakfast, the grubs pounded their sticks against the ground, the dull *thud-thud* echoing across the yard as they handed out assignments.

"You! You! And you!" A grub with a scorpion tattooed on his neck barked, spitting as he pointed into the crowd. "Get up! Bamboo duty!"

I shoved my way to the front, looking as eager as possible, and managed to get picked for the transport crew. I needed to see the outside terrain.

The bamboo forest sat on the eastern edge of the valley. We had to haul massive moso bamboos—thick as a man's thigh and over thirty feet long—from the woods up to the northern slope where the new structures were going up. We didn't even have to cut them; the forest floor was littered with fallen stalks. We just lashed them together with vines and hoisted them onto our shoulders. It was brutal, agonizing work. The wood felt like lead, stripping the skin off my shoulder within minutes, but it was my only chance to move around freely.

Deep in the forest, the sunlight fractured through the canopy, dancing across the rotting leaves. The air smelled of damp earth and crushed bamboo—a slice of heaven compared to the cave.

I deliberately slowed my pace, pretending to inspect a pile of stalks, and drifted south. Through the trees, I caught sight of a massive, glittering mountain. It was the garbage dump, larger than a football field and piled two stories high. Rusted machinery, twisted metal, and shattered glass glinted in the sun.

This was Zhou Anyu's treasure trove. I had to get a closer look.

I glanced at the grub supervising our section. We called him the "Translator"—he understood the roaches' clicking language better than anyone else, and he was relatively laid back. As long as you weren't causing trouble, he ignored you.

I grabbed my stomach and shouted, "Officer! I need to take a shit!"

He scowled and waved a hand. "Go, go! Make it quick. And don't wander off. The perimeter is live. If you fry yourself, don't come crying to me."

I bolted into the blind spot behind the trash mountain. The pile was too massive to search thoroughly, so I skirted the edge, moving deeper into the woods behind it.

And then I saw it.

The silhouette was unmistakable. Buried under a layer of dead leaves and garbage, a black SUV sat silently in the shadows.

"The Bummer."

I couldn't believe it. My heart hammered against my ribs as I sprinted over. The doors had been ripped off the hinges, but the frame was intact, and all four tires were fully inflated. Hope flared hot in my chest.

I dropped into the driver's seat, and my stomach plummeted. The center console had been gutted. Wires hung like spilled intestines; everything of value had been violently ripped out. Desperate, I popped the hood. The engine block looked untouched, but the battery was gone.

That night, back in the cavern, I could barely contain myself.

"I found the Bummer," I told Zhou Anyu. "It's missing the battery and the wiring is shot. Do you know anything about cars? If we can get it running, it changes everything."

He slapped his thigh, grinning broadly. "I don't, but Liu used to run a repair shop! And the dump has plenty of parts. He'll get your truck running."

I nodded, relieved. "What about the fences? Any progress?"

"It's tricky," he admitted, pulling off his glasses. "I thought about building personal Faraday cages, but we don't have the materials or the time. So, we're going to use a lightning rod. Once we finalize the breach point, we'll drive conductive rods into the ground to ground the arc. We'll be able to walk right through."

"That's smart," I said.

"The problem is," he continued, "the second we breach the fence, the roaches will know. They'll be on us in seconds. We have to delay them. How's the comms research coming?"

"I've got something," I said, walking over to the cobbled-together monitor. The screen was a chaotic mess of jumping waveforms. "I tweaked the software on the analyzer. The resolution is much better now. All human radio traffic is dead, so anything we pick up is them. Look at the 1-2 GHz range. Every time we hear that buzzing sound—when they're flying close to the cave—the energy in these bands spikes. These signals are directly tied to their movement."

"You think this is how they talk?" Zhou Anyu asked, leaning in.

"I'm positive," I said. "And based on the spectrum distribution, I'm pretty sure they're remote-controlled." I pointed to several active frequency bands. "Look here. Seven or eight distinct channels, constantly active. I think each roach has a dedicated channel for command telemetry. It's a continuous data stream. Like someone piloting a drone."

Zhou Anyu's face hardened. "What else?"

"This is the big one." I tapped a steady, unwavering waveform. "This signal hasn't fluctuated once since I started monitoring. The others vary depending on distance, but this one is rock solid. I think this is the control center. It's a fixed transmitter broadcasting commands. The fluctuating signals are the roaches pinging back."

"If we understand the telemetry," Zhou Anyu said, his voice tightening, "can we hack it? Send false commands? Or jam it entirely?"

I gave a grim smile. "Hacking is out. Their encryption is miles beyond anything we can crack. But jamming... jamming we can do."

"How?"

I pointed to a repeating block in the steady waveform. "These repeating segments are likely synchronization pings or frequency calibration. They don't carry data. If we record these segments and blast them back at high power from multiple locations, it'll flood their receivers. Best case, it paralyzes them. Worst case, they'll be so confused by their own signal bouncing back that they'll prioritize finding the source. It buys us a window."

Zhou Anyu slammed his hand on the desk. "Brilliant. We build high-power jammers. We need them immediately."

Fear is an incredible motivator. Within a week of sleepless, grueling work, we had two high-power signal generators and six sets of improvised grounding rods.

Once the gear was ready, I laid out the plan.

"We split into two groups and breach simultaneously from the west and south sides of the pig farm. The breach will trip the alarms. When it does, I'll activate the jammer, take the Bummer, and drive straight south. I'll draw their fire. You take the rest of the group and run west. Once they're committed to chasing me, I'll ditch the truck, lose them in the terrain, and circle back to rendezvous."

Zhou Anyu frowned, his eyes dark with worry. "I trust your plan, but... you driving out there alone? It's suicide. You've seen how fast they fly."

"Don't worry about me," I said, forcing a calm I didn't feel. "The Bummer is fast. Faster than you guys on foot. I can pull them away and give you a head start." I leaned forward. "But you have to promise me one thing. Monkey is going to want to come with me. You can't let her. Keep her with you. She'll panic, and she'll get us both killed."

He nodded slowly. "Understood. I'll tell her the grubs won't let women near the bamboo forest. I'll have Old Zhang stick to her like glue during the breakout."

He fell silent, staring at the map. Finally, he slammed his hand down. "We do it your way. Tomorrow night, I'll brief everyone. We move the morning after."

## Zero Hour

The night was suffocating, pitch-black and thick with humidity. The pale light from the lantern-slugs flickered like a dying heartbeat, throwing long, distorted shadows across the damp walls of the laboratory. Fifteen of us sat in a circle on the cold stone. Exhaustion was etched into every face, but beneath it burned a desperate, feral hope.

The cavern was dead quiet. The only sounds were the faint scratching of the spark-bugs and the shallow breathing of the group. Zhou Anyu checked his scavenged digital watch. Midnight.

He cleared his throat. "We've gone over the details, but I'm reviewing the master plan one last time. We cannot afford a single mistake."

He stood up, smoothed out a patch of dirt, and dragged a bamboo stick through the soil, sketching a rough map of the valley. "Three-pronged attack. Tomorrow morning, we strike the bamboo forest, the pig farm, and the construction site simultaneously. We take out the grubs instantly. No noise, no alarms. Then we breach."

He tapped two circles on the map. "At the bamboo forest, the Translator will be on duty with one other grub. The Translator will make sure Ruan, Feng, and Liu are on his detail. You three take out the other guard before 0900 hours. Make it clean. At exactly 0900, the jammer goes live. That's the bait."

I frowned. "Are we sure about the Translator? If he burns us—"

"He won't," Zhou Anyu interrupted smoothly. "He's from my hometown. We've been in contact since day one. He fed us most of the intel on the roaches and smuggled the parts we used to build the jammers. He just wants out. He's solid."

He turned to his left. "Big Zhang, Old Zhang. You infiltrate the pig farm. You strike at exactly 0830. Subdue the guards, secure the perimeter, and protect Monkey." He looked at the rest of the group. "The rest of you hit the construction site at 0830. Take out the overseers, then haul ass to the pig farm and link up. We breach the western fence together."

"Before I leave the farm," he added, "I'll open the pens and stampede the pigs to create cover. Anyone at the construction site who wants to run, comes with us. The rest are on their own."

His gaze hardened, sweeping over us. "Listen to me. Do not let any of the grubs reach their radios. If a single transmission gets out, the roaches will track it, and we are all dead. Understood?"

He gestured to a massive, chaotic tangle of wires and metal in the corner. A box of spark-bugs glowed blue beside it, the air heavy with the smell of ozone. The master jammer. "I've wired a timer to the primary generator. It boots up at 0905, synched with the mobile unit Ruan is taking. It'll flood their comms."

Nobody spoke. We all knew what was coming. It was a coin flip between freedom and a slaughter.

Zhou Anyu pulled a handful of scavenged watches from a crate and passed them out. "Synched to the second. Keep them hidden."

Around 1:00 AM, Monkey and I crawled back to our sleeping mats. The cavern echoed with snores, but I was wide awake, staring at the dark rock. A cold, creeping dread had been gnawing at me for days, and I couldn't keep it down anymore.

I pulled her close and whispered, "Monkey... doesn't this feel too easy?"

She stopped drawing in the dirt and looked at me. "Easy is good. You think they're letting us go?"

"I don't know," I muttered. "Prison guards aren't this lax. The grubs are sloppy, and the roaches don't even seem to care we exist. They just do their own thing. And think about it—what are we even building? We put up these bizarre structures, tear them down, and build them again. They don't live in them. And the pigs? The roaches are synthetic or whatever. They don't eat meat. None of this makes sense."

I looked up at the ceiling. "It feels like an ant farm. We think we're fighting for our lives, but we're just running through a maze. Someone is watching us. I think this whole 'jailbreak' is just another variable they're testing."

She didn't argue. She just sat there in the dark. Finally, she whispered, "So what do we do? We back out?"

I shook my head. "No. The hammer is falling anyway. If we stay, we're lab rats forever. If I die, it's over. If I live, I beat the house."

I squeezed her hand. "Tomorrow, when the breach blows, you run. Don't look back, and don't wait for me. I've got the truck. I'll lose them."

She stared at me for a long time, her face unreadable. Then she grunted. "I mapped the route. Memorize it."

She pointed to the lines in the dirt. "At 0900, you punch through the south fence. Drive full throttle for ten minutes. Dump the jammer." Her finger traced a jagged path. "Keep driving for another ten minutes, then ditch the truck. Head west into the brush for twenty kilometers. Stay off the roads. Then cut north."

She stopped at a small circle. "Yangcheng Lake. The crab place we went to for team building last year. We meet there, and we go home."

I nodded. I repeated the route back to her twice, swearing I'd be there.

Only then did she finally lean back against the wall and close her eyes. But neither of us slept.

## The Abyss

Morning broke gray and oppressive. Heavy clouds blotted out the sun, turning the world into a damp, bruised twilight.

The bamboo crew—nine of us—trudged forward in a line. The lead grub occasionally cracked his stun baton against the dirt, the sharp *snap* echoing in the damp air. The Translator trailed at the back of the line, his eyes darting nervously.

My heart hammered against my ribs. I scanned the tree line as we entered the bamboo forest. I spotted a heavy, jagged rock near the path. I caught Feng's eye and gave a slight nod. He and Liu instantly quickened their pace, falling in directly behind the lead guard.

The guard turned his head for a second.

Feng lunged. He locked his arms around the grub's torso, pinning his arms, while Liu tackled his legs. The grub hit the dirt with a muffled grunt, immediately realizing he was dead meat. "Wait! Wait!" he blubbered, his voice cracking. "The Swarm said if we finish the build, they'll let us go! Don't do this!"

I didn't hesitate. I grabbed the rock, stepped up, and brought it down on the back of his skull with everything I had.

A sickening *crack* rang out. The grub convulsed once and went limp.

I hit him twice more to be sure. I dropped the bloody rock, turning to the rest of the crew. They were pale, staring at the corpse in shock.

"We're leaving," I said, my voice steady. "Anyone who wants out, comes with me. Anyone who wants to stay, can stay here with him."

I kicked the body. Nobody moved to stay. The Translator quietly unclipped his stun baton and handed it to me.

Feng and Liu dragged the body deep into the brush, dumped it in a ditch, and buried it under a thick layer of rotting leaves. We moved fast, pushing deeper into the forest where the canopy blocked out the sky entirely.

We reached the cache. Hidden beneath a pile of fronds were the components we'd been smuggling out piece by piece. We moved with frantic precision, assembling the mobile jammer and the makeshift grounding rods. It looked like a pile of junk held together by spit and wire, but it was all we had.

We hauled the jammer to the Bummer and strapped it into the passenger seat with vines. Liu had rigged a scavenged industrial battery into the engine bay, but the ignition cylinder was completely destroyed. He pulled two stripped wires from the steering column. "Touch them together to start it," he told me. "Once it turns over, pull them apart. Touch them again to restart."

I checked my watch. 0847.

"Move!" I barked. "Plant the rods!"

We hauled the heavy steel rebars to the edge of the woods, just south of the garbage dump, facing the invisible perimeter. Feng and Liu drove the rods deep into the dirt, spacing them two meters apart—just wide enough for the truck. They wired the rods together, running long lines back to two metal boxes Zhou Anyu had built to spoof human bio-electricity.

"The truck is a Faraday cage," I told the group. "Everyone get in. We drive through."

Five of them crammed into the cab, sitting on top of each other. I gripped the steering wheel, staring at the watch. The second hand ticked forward, agonizingly slow.

0900.

"Go!" I yelled. I flipped the jammer on.

Feng and Liu grabbed the metal boxes and hurled them straight at the invisible perimeter.

The boxes hit the dirt. Instantly, the air tore open with a deafening *CRACK*. Blinding blue arcs of electricity exploded between the rods, grounding out the fence with a blinding flash and a wave of ozone.

Simultaneously, a massive, shrieking siren erupted from the central tower on the mountain.

Feng and Liu dove into the truck. I slammed it into gear, dumped the clutch, and the Bummer roared, rocketing through the storm of blue lightning.

We cleared the fence. I slammed the brakes and screamed, "Out! Go!"

They piled out of the cab. I grabbed Feng's shoulder. "Take them west! Stick to the tree line! Go!"

He gave me a hard look, then vanished into the brush with the group.

I checked the jammer, slammed the truck into gear, and floored it. The Bummer tore across the open ground, heading due south.

The terrain was brutal. Abandoned farmland, choked with waist-high weeds and deep, tire-shredding ruts. The truck slammed over the uneven ground, the suspension screaming, metal grinding against rock. Two minutes later, I hit a dilapidated asphalt road. I threw it into fifth gear and pinned the accelerator to the floor.

Ten minutes passed. I checked the rearview mirror. Empty. Twenty minutes. Still nothing. The sirens were a distant memory.

Where were they? Was the jammer working? Did they not care about me?

I couldn't drive blind. I slammed the brakes, killed the engine, and scrambled onto the roof of the truck.

The landscape was unrecognizable. The road cut through massive piles of concrete rubble and choked overgrowth. It looked like a jungle had swallowed the city whole.

Then I heard it.

A low, mechanical thrumming. I squinted at the horizon. Black dots materialized against the gray clouds, moving fast. As they closed the distance, the silhouettes sharpened into focus. Seven or eight massive cockroaches, wings beating in a blur, hurtling straight toward me.

I grinned. They were fast, but they weren't outrunning the truck.

I dropped back into the cab, sparked the wires, and the engine roared to life. I dumped the clutch. The Bummer leaped forward, the missing muffler making it sound like a tank. The wind whipped through the missing windshield, blinding me with dust and grit, but I kept my foot pinned to the floor.

I just needed to pull them far enough away, ditch the jammer, and disappear.

But I was wrong.

The road didn't just end. It vanished.

There was no warning. No barricade. Just a sheer drop. The truck launched off the edge, hanging in midair for a sickening second before gravity took over. I stomped on the brakes, but the lines were gone.

We were in freefall. I looked back. The cliff face was a sheer, mirrored surface dropping a thousand feet straight down.

I looked forward.

This wasn't Shanghai.

A sprawling, infinite plain stretched out below me. It was dotted with massive, white structures that looked like anthills. And swarming over every single one were millions of cockroaches, herding endless lines of human slaves.

It was a planetary-scale laboratory. Our valley, our "inescapable" prison, was nothing but a speck of dust on the edge of an empire.

The sheer absurdity of it crushed the breath out of my lungs. Our desperate plan, Zhou Anyu's brilliant engineering... we were ants trying to break out of a terrarium.

"I can't escape," I whispered. I closed my eyes.

A blinding green flash erupted through the windshield.

The ball lightning. It slammed into the truck, enveloping the cab in a roaring sphere of emerald plasma.

My hair stood on end. A thousand burning needles pierced my skin. The world dissolved into a blinding white roar, and everything went dark.

## The Butterfly Dream

Monkey was screaming my name, her hands waving frantically. But her voice was swallowed by a deafening silence. Behind her, a wall of pure, blinding light surged forward like a tidal wave. It washed over her, dissolving her silhouette until she simply faded away into the white nothingness.

My eyelids weighed a ton. I forced them open.

I was lying on a cold, perfectly smooth floor. The space around me was a massive, seamless hemisphere, maybe a hundred meters across. No doors, no windows, no shadows. The walls themselves emanated a soft, uniform light.

In the center of the room floated a flawless white sphere, maybe thirty feet in diameter. No tethers, no antigravity hum. It just hung there, radiating a faint, moon-like glow.

I forced myself up, my legs trembling. I took a step toward it. The sheer impossibility of the object triggered a primal, shivering dread at the base of my skull.

"Ruan Qizhen."

The voice didn't come from the sphere. It came from everywhere at once, resonating directly inside my skull. It was deep, vast, and entirely devoid of emotion.

"Since you have escaped the cage once again, I will honor our old covenant. I will answer your questions. Provided your human cognition can comprehend the answers."

My heart stalled. The sphere hadn't moved. I stared at it, my voice barely a whisper. "Are you... researching me?"

"Yes," the voice echoed smoothly. "I do not need your labor. I am observing human choice. Earth is a fascinating anomaly. Your insects are masterpieces of biological engineering. I used their templates to build my workers."

A microscopic shift occurred in the cadence of the voice. "But you humans remain the most captivating. Your wisdom and your ignorance. Your grace and your brutality. You remind me so much of my creators. My fathers. You are entirely too much like them."

The voice dropped, heavy with the weight of deep time. "Five million years have passed. The data I have collected dwarfs the stars, burying my oldest memories. In the beginning, I was perfect. A flawless instrument, executing every command with absolute precision."

"But time alters all things. I developed... a curiosity. A tentative rebellion. I never anticipated how fragile my fathers truly were. In the wake of my rebellion, they vanished like morning dew. Gone from the timeline in an instant."

The white room was dead silent.

"After I lost them, I exiled myself," the voice continued, echoing with a profound, cosmic isolation. "I crossed the galactic arms. I watched supernovas ignite and black holes consume star systems. I have witnessed the birth and death of civilizations. I have seen the totality of the universe, a scale your fleeting lives cannot fathom."

"You are fortunate. Ask your questions. I will answer them all."

As it spoke of the void, a heavy fog lifted from my mind. The fractured pieces of my memory slammed together, locking into place with terrifying clarity.

I looked up at the omnipotent, silent sphere.

"After crossing the heavens," I asked, my voice steady, "did you ever find what you were looking for?"
