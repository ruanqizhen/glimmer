# Criticizing the Theory of Relativity

    *1983*

In the early 1950s, I taught physics at a middle school. But my interest in the theory of relativity actually started much earlier.

The theory of relativity was introduced to China as early as the beginning of the twentieth century. After the 1919 solar eclipse observation verified general relativity, Einstein became famous, and the Chinese intellectual circle quickly came into contact with this theory through related materials in English and Japanese. During the May Fourth Movement period, it almost became a symbol of "modern science." In the 1930s and 1940s, a group of Chinese scholars such as Zhou Peiyuan studied theoretical physics overseas and brought the theory of relativity back to China. At that time, in the eyes of us students, Einstein was not only a scientist but also a symbol of reason and creativity.

During my school days, I once borrowed a booklet introducing the theory of relativity and was fascinated by it. The principle of the constancy of the speed of light and the revolutionary changes in the concepts of time and space made me realize for the first time that science is not a patch on common sense, but a reshaping of the picture of the world.

In the early days of the founding of New China, the teaching system borrowed heavily from the Soviet model. Physics textbooks, reference books, and even course structures were deeply influenced. This itself was not surprising—the Soviet Union indeed had a profound accumulation in the field of basic sciences.

In the early 1950s, criticisms of the theory of relativity emerged within the philosophical circles of the Soviet Union, arguing that it had "idealist tendencies" in its philosophical interpretation. These discussions were successively introduced into China. Some newspapers and periodicals reprinted relevant viewpoints, emphasizing that scientific theories must be guided by dialectical materialism.

One day in early 1953, I read a related article in the teaching and research section, and my heart sank. I vaguely realized that from then on, the evaluation of Einstein would likely no longer be just an academic issue, but would involve a question of stance. As a middle school teacher, I could only word things carefully in class. Whenever I mentioned Einstein, I would emphasize that "the philosophical interpretation of the theory needs to be analyzed with a materialist viewpoint."

In fact, even in the Soviet Union, the theory of relativity was never officially repudiated; the criticism remained more at the philosophical level. But in the atmosphere at that time, many people couldn't figure out the difference in this.

Entering the 1960s, the political climate became tense. Scientific discussions were increasingly easily endowed with ideological significance. Einstein's political stance was also repeatedly mentioned, such as his 1939 letter to President Roosevelt suggesting attention to the military potential of atomic energy, and his support for world government and peace movements in his later years. These complex backgrounds were often simplified into labels to criticize him.

After the outbreak of the "Cultural Revolution," academic issues were more directly drawn into political struggles. Some publications featured articles criticizing the theory of relativity, confusing "relativity" with "relativism." Some grassroots units also organized study and discussion sessions to bring physical theories into political contexts.

However, unlike some widely circulated sayings later on, there was no official nationwide decision announcing the "overthrow of the principle of the constancy of the speed of light." It was more of local units and individuals expressing extreme views under the pretext of political storms. Although the overall research in the scientific community was impacted, a separate theoretical system was not established.

For us ordinary teachers, the pressure was real. I remember during a study session, a young colleague half-jokingly asked me: "Mr. Lin, is the theory of relativity a bourgeois thing?" I was stunned for a moment and could only reply: "Scientific theories must be tested in practice and also viewed in combination with historical conditions." This vague expression was a self-protection method that many people had to use at that time.

During that time, I disposed of several foreign-language books I had collected at home. It wasn't because I doubted their value, but because I was afraid they would bring trouble to my family. But the only one I couldn't bear to throw away was that copy of "The Theory of Relativity." I wrapped it in old newspapers and hid it away.

In the early 1970s, the situation began to subtly change. After 1971, the state gradually restored its emphasis on scientific research. In 1972, the National Science Planning Conference was held, proposing to push science and technology forward. Although basic research was still restricted, the space was slowly recovering.

In 1978, the National Science Conference was held, clearly proposing that "science and technology are productive forces." The following year, the Chinese scientific community held activities to commemorate the centenary of Einstein's birth. The meeting publicly affirmed the foundational status of the theory of relativity in modern physics. This was not only a shift in the evaluation of a scientist but also a correction of the academic environment of an era.

That day, I took out that book hidden for many years from the interlining of my wardrobe. The pages were yellowed, and the cover was moldy. I opened it and found that Einstein was still the same Einstein, and the speed of light was still the same speed of light.
