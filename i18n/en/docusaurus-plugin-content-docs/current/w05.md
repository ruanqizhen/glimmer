# Criticizing the Theory of Relativity

*1983*

In the early 1950s, I was teaching physics at a local middle school. But my fascination with the theory of relativity actually began much earlier.

The theory of relativity was introduced to China as early as the beginning of the twentieth century. Following the 1919 solar eclipse observation that famously verified general relativity, Albert Einstein became a global icon, and Chinese intellectual circles quickly encountered his theories through English and Japanese materials. During the May Fourth Movement, the theory almost became synonymous with the concept of "modern science." In the 1930s and 1940s, a cohort of brilliant Chinese scholars, such as Zhou Peiyuan, studied theoretical physics overseas and brought their deep understanding of relativity back to China. To us students back then, Einstein wasn't merely a scientist; he was the ultimate symbol of reason and boundless creativity.

During my school days, I borrowed a small booklet introducing the theory of relativity and found myself completely captivated. The principle of the constancy of the speed of light, coupled with the revolutionary shifts in how we understand time and space, made me realize for the very first time that science isn't just a patch applied to common sense—it is a complete reshaping of our picture of the universe.

In the early days following the founding of New China, our educational system borrowed heavily from the Soviet model. Physics textbooks, reference materials, and even the fundamental structure of our courses were deeply influenced by Soviet pedagogy. This wasn't surprising in itself—the Soviet Union undeniably possessed profound expertise and accumulation in the realm of basic sciences.

However, in the early 1950s, criticisms of the theory of relativity began to surface within Soviet philosophical circles. The core argument was that its philosophical interpretation harbored dangerous "idealist tendencies." These debates inevitably made their way into China. Several newspapers and academic periodicals began reprinting these Soviet viewpoints, loudly emphasizing that all scientific theories must be guided by dialectical materialism.

One day in early 1953, I sat in our teaching and research office reading one of these articles, and my heart sank. I vaguely realized that, moving forward, the evaluation of Einstein would likely cease to be a purely academic matter; it was destined to become a question of political stance. As a middle school teacher, I had to begin wording my lessons incredibly carefully. Whenever I mentioned Einstein in class, I made sure to emphatically state that "the philosophical interpretation of this theory must be analyzed strictly through a materialist viewpoint."

In reality, even within the Soviet Union, the theory of relativity was never officially repudiated; the criticism remained largely confined to philosophical debates. But in the tense atmosphere of that era, many ordinary people couldn't distinguish the nuance.

As we entered the 1960s, the political climate tightened drastically. Scientific discussions were increasingly, and easily, hijacked and imbued with ideological significance. Einstein's personal political stances were repeatedly dragged into the spotlight. For instance, his famous 1939 letter to President Roosevelt—suggesting the government pay attention to the military potential of atomic energy—and his later support for a world government and global peace movements were frequently cited. These complex historical contexts were stripped of nuance and simplified into convenient labels used to denounce him.

After the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution, academic debates were violently dragged into the arena of direct political struggle. Radical publications ran fiery articles criticizing relativity, often deliberately conflating "relativity" with the philosophical concept of "relativism." Some grassroots units even organized mandatory study and discussion sessions specifically designed to force complex physical theories into rigid political frameworks.

However, contrary to some exaggerated rumors that circulated later on, there was never an official, nationwide decree announcing the "overthrow of the principle of the constancy of the speed of light." Instead, it was largely local units and radical individuals weaponizing extreme views under the cover of the era's political storms. While the broader scientific community's research was undoubtedly stifled, a separate, state-sanctioned theoretical system of physics was never actually established.

For ordinary teachers like myself, the pressure was terrifyingly real. I remember one specific study session where a young colleague turned to me and half-jokingly asked, "Mr. Lin, is the theory of relativity a bourgeois invention?" 

I froze for a moment, my mind racing, before carefully replying, "Scientific theories must always be tested in practice, and they must also be viewed in combination with their specific historical conditions." This kind of vague, noncommittal phrasing was a survival tactic that many of us had to master in those days.

During that dark period, I quietly disposed of several foreign-language books I had spent years collecting. It wasn't because I doubted their academic value; I simply couldn't risk the danger they might bring to my family. But there was one book I simply couldn't bring myself to throw away: my worn copy of *The Theory of Relativity*. I carefully wrapped it in layers of old newspapers and hid it deep in the back of my closet.

In the early 1970s, the suffocating atmosphere began to subtly shift. After 1971, the state slowly began to restore its emphasis on scientific research. The National Science Planning Conference was held in 1972, proposing a renewed push to advance science and technology. Although basic theoretical research was still heavily restricted, the breathing room was slowly expanding.

In 1978, the monumental National Science Conference was held, famously declaring that "science and technology are productive forces." The very next year, the Chinese scientific community openly held activities to commemorate the centenary of Einstein's birth. The conference publicly and unequivocally affirmed the foundational status of the theory of relativity in modern physics. This was not merely a rehabilitation of a single scientist's reputation; it was the long-overdue correction of an entire era's poisoned academic environment.

That afternoon, I went to my wardrobe, reached into the hidden interlining, and pulled out the book I had concealed for so many years. The pages were yellowed and brittle, the cover lightly dusted with mold. I opened it and smiled. Einstein was still the same Einstein, and the speed of light was still exactly the same speed of light.
