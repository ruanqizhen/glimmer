# Criticizing the Theory of Relativity

*1983*

In the early 1950s, I was teaching physics at a local middle school. But my obsession with the theory of relativity had started long before that.

The theory of relativity hit China around the turn of the twentieth century. After the famous 1919 solar eclipse observation verified general relativity, Albert Einstein became a global superstar, and Chinese intellectuals quickly devoured his theories through translated English and Japanese papers. By the time the May Fourth Movement rolled around, Einstein's theory had practically become a buzzword for "modern science." In the 1930s and 1940s, a brilliant wave of Chinese scholars—like Zhou Peiyuan—went overseas to study theoretical physics and brought their deep understanding of relativity back home. To students like us back then, Einstein wasn't just a scientist. He was the ultimate symbol of human reason and boundless creativity.

Back in school, I borrowed a small booklet explaining relativity and found myself completely hooked. The principle of the constancy of the speed of light, combined with the mind-bending shifts in how we understand space and time, made me realize for the very first time that science isn't just a bandage applied to common sense. It's a complete, violent reshaping of our picture of the universe.

In the early days of New China, our entire educational system was a carbon copy of the Soviet model. Our physics textbooks, our reference materials, and even the basic structure of our courses were heavily dictated by Soviet pedagogy. That wasn't necessarily a bad thing—the Soviet Union had undeniably profound expertise in the basic sciences. 

However, in the early 1950s, Soviet philosophers began aggressively criticizing the theory of relativity. Their core argument was that the philosophical interpretation of the theory harbored dangerous "idealist tendencies." Naturally, these debates bled into China. Newspapers and academic journals started reprinting these Soviet critiques, loudly demanding that all scientific theories must be strictly guided by dialectical materialism.

I remember sitting in the faculty lounge one afternoon in early 1953, reading one of these articles. My stomach dropped. I realized, with a creeping sense of dread, that the evaluation of Einstein was no longer going to be an academic debate. It was about to become a political purity test. As a middle school teacher, I had to start watching my mouth. Whenever I brought up Einstein in class, I made sure to emphatically declare that "the philosophical interpretation of this theory must be analyzed strictly through a materialist lens."

In reality, even within the Soviet Union, the actual math of relativity was never officially banned; the attacks were mostly confined to philosophical grandstanding. But in the paranoid, suffocating atmosphere of that era, ordinary people couldn't afford to care about nuance.

As we moved into the 1960s, the political climate tightened like a vise. Scientific discussions were increasingly hijacked and weaponized for ideological warfare. Einstein's personal politics were dragged into the spotlight and dissected. Radicals constantly brought up his famous 1939 letter to President Roosevelt—warning the US government about the military potential of atomic energy—along with his later push for a world government and global peace. They stripped away all the complex historical context and boiled his life down into convenient, toxic labels used to denounce him.

When the Cultural Revolution exploded, academic debates were dragged kicking and screaming into the arena of direct political struggle. Radical magazines ran fiery hit pieces criticizing relativity, deliberately confusing "relativity" with the philosophical concept of "relativism." Some local units even forced their workers to attend mandatory study sessions specifically designed to cram complex physics into rigid, brainless political frameworks.

Now, contrary to some of the wild rumors that circulated later on, the national government never issued an official decree declaring that "the speed of light is no longer constant." Instead, it was mostly local radicals weaponizing extreme views under the cover of the era's political chaos. While the broader scientific community was absolutely crippled by fear, a separate, state-sanctioned "socialist physics" was never actually established.

But for ordinary teachers like me, the terror was incredibly real. I vividly remember one specific study session where a young colleague turned to me and half-jokingly asked, "Mr. Lin, is the theory of relativity a bourgeois invention?" 

I froze. My mind raced as I desperately searched for a safe answer. "Scientific theories must always be tested in practice," I replied carefully, weighing every single syllable. "And they must be viewed within their specific historical conditions." It was vague, noncommittal garbage—the exact kind of survival tactic we all had to master back then.

During those dark years, I quietly threw away several foreign-language textbooks I had spent years collecting. It wasn't because I doubted the science; I just couldn't risk the danger they might bring to my family. But there was one book I couldn't bring myself to destroy: my worn, dog-eared copy of *The Theory of Relativity*. I wrapped it in thick layers of old newspaper and shoved it deep into the back corner of my closet.

By the early 1970s, the suffocating atmosphere finally began to thin. After 1971, the state slowly started caring about scientific research again. The National Science Planning Conference in 1972 proposed a renewed push to advance technology. Even though theoretical research was still heavily chained, we could finally breathe a little easier.

The dam finally broke in 1978 during the landmark National Science Conference, which famously declared that "science and technology are productive forces." The following year, the Chinese scientific community held massive, public celebrations for the centenary of Einstein's birth. The conference openly and unequivocally affirmed the theory of relativity as the absolute bedrock of modern physics. It wasn't just a rehabilitation of a dead scientist's reputation; it was a long-overdue apology for a poisoned academic era.

That afternoon, I went to my wardrobe, reached into the hidden back panel, and pulled out the book I had hidden away for so many years. The pages were yellow and brittle. The cover was lightly dusted with mold. I opened it and smiled. Einstein was still the exact same Einstein, and the speed of light was still the exact same speed of light.
