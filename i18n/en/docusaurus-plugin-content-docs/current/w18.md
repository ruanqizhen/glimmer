# Fishing

*1998* 

After I was discharged from the army in the late 1980s, I followed the trend and headed down to Guangdong to make a living for a few years. Having scraped together some hard-earned savings, I returned to my hometown in the early 1990s, where the villagers soon elected me as their village head.

Back then, getting the village-run plastic products factory up and running meant I had to host bureaucrats from the county out by the fishpond almost every single day. When people were around, my face was plastered with a perpetual smile. I offered them Chunghwa cigarettes one after another, kept their tea and water cups full with practiced ease, and looked nothing short of exceptionally attentive. But the moment the guests left and I was safely behind the closed doors of my own house, I couldn't help but curse out loud. Being a village head was far more complicated than serving in the military. If any local "immortal" felt like they hadn't been worshipped enough, there was absolutely no way to get the factory's paperwork officially stamped. My biggest headache was Section Chief Qian from the county industrial bureau, an incredibly difficult man who absolutely refused to budge. I constantly racked my brains trying to figure out how to handle him.

One afternoon in 1995, I fired up my chugging walking tractor, loaded it with a massive fishing net, and called a few villagers over to tear through the village's east fishpond for most of the day. We chased, drove, and netted, forcing every last fish into the neighboring pond. Before heading home, I firmly warned Zhou Qiang, the man in charge of the fishpond, "Tomorrow, Section Chief Qian from the county is bringing his people to fish. You make sure the fence by the pond is locked up tight. Whatever you do, do not let those fish swim back over."

The next morning, just after nine o'clock, three black Santana sedans rolled in, trailing yellow dust, and majestically pulled up to the east fishpond. I intentionally feigned illness that day to avoid accompanying them myself. Later, Zhou Qiang told me it was obvious that some of them were seasoned regulars. The moment they stepped out of the cars, they led the pack, lined up along the bank, propped up their parasols, and popped open their bait boxes. Baiting their hooks with earthworms and wine-soaked rice, they cast their fiberglass rods and quickly slipped into the zone.

But how could there possibly be any fish left in the pond? 

After the time it takes to eat a meal, the floats on the water hadn't twitched once. A few of the more impatient guys couldn't take it anymore. Looking around, they spotted the old fishpond right next door. Hesitating only briefly, they shouldered their rods and relocated. It wasn't long before the remaining seven were drawn by the shouts and splashing sounds coming from the next pond, and soon they all followed suit. A lively chorus of cheerful chatter and laughter erupted over the water.

Just as the anglers were fishing with wild abandon, an old man in his sixties suddenly materialized by the fishpond. Standing with his hands firmly on his hips, his weathered, calloused face flushed bright red, he roared, "Well, look at this bunch of robbers! I was wondering why my fishpond was losing money year after year. Turns out people are bold enough to rob me in broad daylight! On your feet, all of you!"

The crowd scrambled to their feet, exchanging dismayed glances. Standing among them, the slightly chubby Section Chief Qian managed a painfully awkward, apologetic smile. "Sir, we thought this was the village's public pond. We're very sorry. We'll gladly pay market price for the fish we caught."

But the old man wasn't easily placated. He slammed his walking stick into the dirt. "Pay only when you get caught? When my fish were alive and kicking in that water, they were worth top dollar. If you want to leave, you're leaving all your cars behind."

Section Chief Qian grew visibly anxious. "Sir, please calm down. We were invited here by Gao Dayong, the village head of Shuanghe Village."

The old man’s fury only flared higher, spit flying from his mouth. "The village head is a piece of shit! Let me tell you, I've lived this long and I'm not afraid of heaven or earth. Dealing with officials like you is my specialty!"

Seeing the time was right, Zhou Qiang immediately hopped on his motorcycle, sped to my house, and rushed me back to the pond. As soon as Section Chief Qian saw me, he looked as though he'd found a savior. He pulled me aside and desperately whispered a few words. 

I then pulled the old man aside and murmured an explanation. Unexpectedly, upon hearing it, the old man jumped back and shouted at the top of his lungs, "So you officials just cover for each other! I've memorized your license plate numbers, and I'm taking this all the way to the central government in Beijing!"

Section Chief Qian, frantically wiping cold sweat from his brow, quietly asked me about the old man's background. I put on a perfectly helpless expression, lowered my voice, and replied, "Section Chief Qian, to tell you the truth, this old guy is named Guo Benwang. He's the most notorious professional petitioner in our entire village. My predecessor was stubbornly sued right out of office by him."

The muscles in Section Chief Qian's face twitched violently.

I gritted my teeth and pressed on, "This old bastard is going too far. Section Chief, don't you worry. I'll risk my job as village head if I have to. I'll get two young guys to tie him up and haul him back. I absolutely will not let him ruin the futures of important leaders like yourselves."

Section Chief Qian waved his hands frantically. "Dayong, please, no violence! For this kind of grassroots masses, we have to do careful ideological work. We absolutely cannot intensify the conflict. I'm leaving this entirely in your capable hands. You must appease him. As for the difficulties with your village factory, the county will help get them resolved as soon as possible."

True to his word, a few days later, all the approval procedures for setting up the plastic products factory sailed through with a green light.

A few evenings later, I invited the old man out to the Juxian Teahouse in town. Respectfully pouring him a steaming hot cup of tea, I grinned and said, "Uncle Guo, I really owe you big time for this one!"
