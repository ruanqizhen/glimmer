# A Master Move

*1998*

The rain came down in sheets, smashing relentlessly against the office windowpanes and pounding heavily on everyone's nerves. The entire city was waterlogged, and the daily newspapers were full of tragic reports from the disaster zones: collapsed walls, ruined buildings, and streets turned into muddy soup. Inside the government bureau, everyone walked around with their heads down, nervously waiting to see which way the wind would blow regarding the impending institutional restructuring. Nobody knew when it would be their turn to be laid off. The air in the building was thick with a musty smell, cut with stale tobacco smoke and the sour tension of people desperately waiting for their luck to turn.

I had been chained to a desk in the bureau's supply and marketing department for over ten years. My days ticked by as predictably as a pendulum: punch in at exactly 7:30 in the morning, punch out at 5:30 in the afternoon. At home, my wife kept nagging me about how Wang's wife had just bought an imported "Panasonic" rice cooker, raving about how the rice it made was crystal clear and never stuck to the bottom of the pot. She buzzed in my ear every single day: "Shouzhi, you need to get one for our family too, so I don't always have to stand watch over the gas stove." 

I scraped and saved for half a year, and finally had six hundred yuan safely tucked into my pocket—six crisp, new hundred-yuan bills. I planned to head straight to the department store the moment work ended, bring the cooker home, and enjoy a delicious, fragrant bowl of rice that very night.

Who could have predicted how suffocatingly stuffy the office would be that afternoon? Seeking a breeze, I strolled into the conference room with a hand fan to watch the television. The news was broadcasting footage from the flood zones. An old woman with stark white hair was sitting beside a half-submerged earthen stove, desperately clutching a mud-caked aluminum pot to her chest. Her eyes were hollow with absolute despair. 

I suddenly thought of my own elderly mother, who had spent her entire life toiling in front of a similar earthen stove back in our home village. A sudden, fierce warmth flared up in my chest.

Right at that exact moment, the conference room door burst open with a loud *clang*. Liu from the labor union marched in, hugging a donation box wrapped tight in bright red paper. The slit carved into the top looked like a gaping mouth. young Wang trailed closely behind her, holding a "Seagull" camera at the ready, prepared to capture "advanced moral deeds" at a moment's notice.

I must have been possessed that day. My mind was entirely consumed by the image of that old woman and her broken pot, and all I could think was: *Without a pot, how can she possibly cook rice?* 

Gritting my teeth, I abruptly stood up. Under the wide-eyed, stunned gaze of everyone in the room, I pulled that roll of hard-earned cash straight out of my pocket and shoved the entire wad into the box.

Out in the corridor, several deputy directors had been huddled together, smoking and whispering. None of them had donated yet. The director himself hadn't even shown up, and absolutely no one dared to drop a dime before he did. Everyone was waiting for the director to set the tone and establish the unofficial ceiling.

"Jiao Shouzhi, six hundred yuan!" Liu screamed, her voice cracking with pure shock. 

The camera flashed with a sharp *click*, blinding me until I saw stars. Out in the hallway, the cigarettes in the hands of the deputy directors uniformly froze in mid-air. The way they all stared at me, you would think they were looking at an alien—or a lunatic actively seeking his own destruction.

The very next day, the large red honor roll was proudly posted in the lobby. My name pressed down on the top of the list like a giant Buddha, the words "Six Hundred Yuan" shining so brightly they made everyone in the building deeply uncomfortable. Beneath me was the director with three hundred yuan, followed by the deputy directors at two hundred, slowly decreasing downwards. 

In a 1990s government organ, this kind of visual "usurpation" was considered nothing short of political suicide. 

Someone muttered privately in the hallway, "What exactly is Jiao Shouzhi trying to pull? Does he want to publicly embarrass the director, or does he actually think his political consciousness is higher than the leadership's?"

Everyone waited eagerly for my impending downfall. The buddies I usually joked around with began looking at me with deep pity, treating me like a dead man walking who was about to be hung out to dry. In the pantry, the whispers rose and fell: "That guy is finished. He just had to steal the director's thunder."

Unexpectedly, half a month later, I was formally promoted to Section Chief of the supply and marketing department. 

Throughout the entire bureau, people's jaws hit the floor. First, a deathly silence swept through the office, and then, instantly, everyone changed their tune.

"Chief Jiao, brilliant! Truly brilliant!" The former section chief, who had been sideways transferred to the personnel department, came over specifically to light my cigarette. He lowered his voice, his eyes shining with admiration. "At the time, everyone thought you were being an idiot. Who knew you had so accurately grasped the changing wind from the higher-ups? That six hundred yuan was thrown with absolute precision! Did you know early on that the city was planning to set you up as a moral model?"

Meanwhile, in the pantry, someone else sneered. "Just look at Jiao Shouzhi. He always pretends to be so honest and simple, but he's incredibly sinister when it counts. How was that just a donation? That was a public trap perfectly set for the director! His mind is so deep, none of us could ever hope to learn from him."

Sitting behind my new desk, staring blankly at my official certificate of appointment, my heart was filled with a bitter, helpless smile. I couldn't possibly tell them that on that day, I really, truly just wanted to buy a rice cooker. 

The next day, I ran into young Liu, the colleague I had always been closest with. I tried to explain myself in a few quick sentences. He just narrowed his eyes, gave me a knowing smirk, and patted my shoulder. 

"Understood, buddy," he chuckled. "We all completely understand."
