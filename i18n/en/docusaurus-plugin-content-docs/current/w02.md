# Cleared to Zero

*1998*

For years, the loudest noise in the finance department of our machine tool factory wasn't the gossiping staff. It was the rhythmic, sharp *crackle-and-pop* of the antique, red sandalwood abacus flying through my hands.

When I was young, I caught a lucky break. I met a retired old-timer from the local state-run co-op and became his apprentice. He drilled me in the brutal, authentic fundamentals of the Hundred-Bead Abacus, teaching me complex, interlocking fingering techniques with poetic names like "Lion Rolling the Ball." Because of him, I dominated the city's abacus calculation competitions for years. Inside the finance department, the crisp *clack-clack* of my wooden beads set the tempo. The second I started tallying, everyone else's work rhythm unconsciously fell right in step with mine. Even later, when the factory handed out those little solar-powered Casio calculators, I refused to give them the time of day.

"If you punch those cheap plastic buttons too fast, you miss a stroke. There's no feedback," I'd lecture my apprentices, adjusting my blue cloth oversleeves and taking a sip from a chipped enamel mug. "The abacus has a soul. When your fingers flick the beads and you hear that hard, crisp crack, you know the numbers are solid."

The year-end financial close was my time to shine. Kraft paper vouchers piled up on our desks like small mountains. All around the room, calculators beeped frantically as stressed young clerks fat-fingered a single key and had to scrap an entire column of numbers. Meanwhile, I'd rapidly flip through the thick ledgers with my left hand, while three fingers on my right hand danced across the beads like lightning. Whether I was running complex multiplication or rapid division, every flick of my wrist was smooth, fluid, and deadly accurate. Amidst the sharp collision of wood, I could flawlessly reconcile millions of yuan in subsidiary ledgers without a single mistake. Even the factory director, when he came through the office, would hand me a premium Hongtashan cigarette and politely call me "Mr. Lin."

That was my kingdom. I was the undisputed grandmaster, sitting comfortably on the throne.

Then, the nightmare arrived. Early that year, the factory rolled out a "Modern Enterprise Management Pilot Program" and hauled in several massive, clunky Great Wall computers. Chen, a fresh-faced college grad, was installed as the new "Information Supervisor." Wearing thick glasses and carrying neither an abacus nor a pen, he buzzed around the office all day, clutching little black floppy disks.

"Mr. Lin, from now on, all our accounts are going into a database. We're running FoxBASE," Chen announced enthusiastically, leaning over my desk. "In the future, when you need to check an account, you just hit 'Enter.' The summary pulls up in one second flat."

I stared at him, completely lost. "What base? Where is the base? Pulls up in a second? Doesn't it have to be tallied line by line?"

To prove his point, Chen dragged me over to the glowing screen. He pointed at a glaring green cursor blinking in the dark, calling it UCDOS. He asked me to rattle off the warehouse entry details for a specific batch of steel from a month ago. I turned around to grab the heavy, dust-covered ledger. Just as I started flipping the pages, the massive dot-matrix printer next to the computer let out a violent, piercing screech. 

*Zhi-la, zhi-la.*

In less than two minutes, a continuous sheet of perforated paper spooled out onto the floor. Neatly printed across the page were the exact details, unit prices, and total amounts I was looking for. Even the material loss rate had been calculated and perfectly formatted.

I was absolutely dumbfounded.

Refusing to believe in this electronic witchcraft, I ripped the paper off the machine, dragged my red sandalwood abacus in front of me, and started recalculating every single line item by hand. My thumb and index finger flew up and down the rods, a thin sheen of sweat breaking out on my forehead. Half an hour later, I stared at the final numbers resting on the beads. My hand started to shake.

Not a single mistake.

The "Lion Rolling the Ball" technique I was so fiercely proud of—the muscle memory I had drilled into my hands for thirty years until they were thick with calluses—had been obliterated. Placed next to a "microcomputer" that didn't even need physical beads, my life's work suddenly felt like a slow, clunky, pathetic joke.

Less than six months later, the department chief pulled me aside. "Lin, the financial system is entirely computerized now. It's all about digital accounting," he mumbled, refusing to look at the abacus sitting on my desk. "You haven't memorized the keyboard, and you can't type. Management found a new spot for you. We're moving you down to the logistics warehouse. Your bookkeeping skills will still be useful tracking inventory. Hey, we're all just doing our part for the factory, right?"

I didn't throw a fit. I didn't beg for my job. That afternoon, I silently wrapped my red sandalwood abacus in a clean, red cloth. When I walked out of the finance department for the last time, the crisp, rhythmic crackle of the abacus was gone. It had been replaced entirely by the dull, monotonous *clack-clack-clack* of plastic keyboards—the sound of a soulless machine slowly chewing up the best years of my life.

After sitting on a cold bench in the dusty warehouse for two weeks, I decided I wasn't just going to quietly fade away. Computers might be fast, but the ancient wisdom carved into the abacus couldn't just be thrown in the trash. The math concealed deep, elegant rules about structure and logic. When my mentor handed this craft down to me, he asked for exactly one thing: *Don't let it die with our generation.* I decided to set up a stall on the street. Not to make a quick buck, but just to show people the art. The factory might not want it anymore, but I refused to believe that in a city this massive, absolutely no one would care.

I got old Liu from the carpentry shop to build me a giant, instructional abacus—over a meter long and painted high-vis yellow and black. I bought a cheap megaphone from the hardware store. After my warehouse shift ended, I hauled the giant frame to an empty spot near the Cultural Palace, hung it on a brick wall, flicked on the mic, and started my pitch.

"Step right up, take a look! The abacus doesn't just do decimals, it handles the hexadecimals of the old weighing scales! One tael is zero-point-zero-six-two-five pounds. The old chant goes: 'One retreats, six two five.' It's lightning fast!" I boomed over the crackling speaker. "Let me show you the Nine-Return rapid division..."

People streamed past me on the sidewalk. Most of them, clutching briefcases tightly against their ribs, hurried by without even glancing my way. Occasionally, someone would stop, stare at the giant wooden frame, shake their head in pity, and keep walking.

Finally, a pack of middle schoolers in baggy uniforms, lugging heavy backpacks and laughing loudly, wandered over. They actually looked curious. My spirits spiked. With a sharp, sweeping motion, I brought both hands down on the beads, flawlessly demonstrating how to multiply 12,345 by 11,111 in a matter of seconds.

One of the kids pulled a digital calculator out of his pocket. He punched the buttons, stared at the tiny screen, and his eyes went wide. "Whoa! He actually got it right!"

Another kid leaned in. "That's awesome, Grandpa. Is it hard to learn?"

But the kid with the calculator just sneered. "I mean, it's right, but who cares? Hey Grandpa, can that wooden thing calculate the square root of 37?"

I froze. Square roots? Of course I could do square roots! The abacus has dedicated subtraction methods just for that. 

I took a deep breath, raised my hands, and hovered my wrists in the air, getting ready to set the beads.

"Oh man, he can't do it. Let's go," the kid scoffed, waving his calculator in my face. "It's 6.08276! Come on, let's go play pool!"

The kids instantly lost interest, turning and sprinting down the street, laughing. My hands, still hovering over the wooden beads, slowly went rigid.

He was right. I *could* calculate it. But no matter how fast my fingers flew, no matter how elegant the math was... what did it matter to them? 

The autumn wind whipped down the street, dragging dry leaves across the concrete. Everyone was rushing off to be somewhere else. The world was moving too fast, and nobody needed wooden beads in a square frame to measure their lives anymore.

I slowly took the giant abacus down from the wall. I pressed my right index finger against the center beam and dragged it hard from right to left.

*Pop.*

Every single bead snapped back to its starting position. It's the very first thing you do before you start a new calculation. It's called "wiping the board and clearing to zero."

I shoved the abacus into my canvas bag. *Time to go home,* I told myself.

The new era was rushing in like a flood, and I had already been cleared to zero.
