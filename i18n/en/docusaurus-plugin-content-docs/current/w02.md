# Cleared to Zero

*1998*

For years, the loudest sound in the finance section of our machine tool factory wasn't the chatter of the staff, but the rhythmic "crackle and pop" of the antique red sandalwood abacus in my hands.

In my younger years, I was lucky enough to meet a retired elder from the supply and marketing cooperative. I apprenticed under him and learned the authentic, foundational skills of the "Hundred-Bead Abacus," including the intricate interlocking fingering technique known as "Lion Rolling the Embroidered Ball." I reigned as the perennial champion of the city's abacus calculation competition. Within the walls of the finance section, the moment the crisp clatter of beads rang out from my desk, everyone else's work rhythm unconsciously fell in step with mine. Even later, when the factory issued everyone one of those Casio calculators with the little solar panels, I refused to give them the time of day.

"If you punch those things too fast, it's easy to miss a key, and there's no tactile feedback," I would always tell my apprentices, adjusting my blue cloth oversleeves and clutching a chipped enamel tea mug. "The abacus has spirit. When your fingers flick the beads and you hear that crisp crack, the account feels solid."

The year-end financial close was the moment that truly showcased the value of "Mr. Lin." The kraft paper vouchers piled up on the desks like small mountains. Several calculators would beep frantically, and the young clerks often had to scrap everything and start completely over just because they fat-fingered a single key halfway through. Meanwhile, I would rapidly flip through the ledger with my left hand while the three fingers of my right hand flew over the beads like lightning. Whether it was "Breaking Head Multiplication" or "Nine-Return Division," every move was smooth, natural, and precise. Amidst the crisp collision of wooden beads, I could flawlessly sort out a set of subsidiary ledgers worth millions, without a single error. Even the factory director, upon seeing me, would politely offer me a Hongtashan cigarette and respectfully address me as "Mr. Lin."

That was the martial arts world I proudly inhabited, and I was the undisputed grandmaster, sitting securely on my throne.

But eventually, my nightmare arrived. At the beginning of the year, the factory launched a "Modern Enterprise Management Pilot Program" and hauled in several clunky "Great Wall" microcomputers. Xiao Chen, a freshly graduated college student, was appointed as the new information supervisor. Wearing thick-rimmed glasses and holding neither an abacus nor a ledger, he bustled around the section every day clutching a few square, black floppy disks.

"Mr. Lin, from now on our accounts will all be entered into a database. We're using FoxBASE," Xiao Chen gestured enthusiastically by my desk. "In the future, to check the accounts, you just hit 'Enter,' and all the summary data can be pulled up in a single second."

I was completely baffled. "What Bass? What database? Where is the base? Pulled up in one second? Doesn't it need to be tallied item by item?"

To prove his point, Xiao Chen pulled me over to the computer. He told me this was UCDOS, pointing to a glaring green cursor blinking on the screen. He asked me to recite the warehouse entry details for a specific batch of steel from a month prior. I turned around to fetch the thick, dust-covered ledgers. Just as I was flipping halfway through, I heard the wide carriage printer next to the computer let out a piercing screech. 

*Zhi-la, zhi-la.*

In less than two minutes, a continuous sheet of perforated printer paper spooled out. Neatly listed across the page were all the details, unit prices, and total amounts—even the material loss rate had been automatically calculated and perfectly formatted.

I was completely dumbfounded.

Refusing to believe in this electronic heresy, I ripped off the printout, pulled my red sandalwood abacus in front of me, and started recalculating every single item by hand. My thumb and index finger flew up and down, fine beads of sweat breaking out on my forehead. Half an hour later, staring at the final numbers resting on the abacus, my hand began to tremble uncontrollably.

Not a single error.

The "Lion Rolling the Embroidered Ball" technique I was so fiercely proud of, the fingering I had practiced for thirty years until my hands were thick with calluses—in the face of this "microcomputer" that didn't even require physical beads, my life's work had been reduced to a slow, clunky, and ultimately meaningless joke.

Less than half a year later, the section chief came to talk to me. "Old Lin, the financial system is entirely computerized now. It's all about 'accounting computerization' these days," he mumbled, his eyes darting away, too afraid to look at the abacus on my desk. "You can't even memorize the Wubi typing method, and you can't type smoothly on a keyboard. The factory has arranged a new role for you. We're transferring you to the logistics warehouse; your bookkeeping foundation will still be useful there. Whatever we do, it's all about contributing to the factory."

I didn't make a fuss, nor did I plead for mercy. That afternoon, I silently wrapped my red sandalwood abacus carefully in a clean red cloth. When I walked out of the finance section, the crisp sound of the abacus was gone, replaced entirely by the dull, monotonous *clack-clack-clack* of keyboards—like a soulless machine slowly chewing away the better half of my life.

After sitting on the cold bench in the warehouse for half a month, I decided I couldn't just fade away into uselessness. Computers might be fast, but the wisdom passed down from our ancestors couldn't be easily discarded. The addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of the abacus concealed deep rules, encompassing both the square and the circle. When my mentor passed these skills down to me, he had only one request: never let this craft lose its roots in our generation. I decided to set up a stall on the street. Not to make money, but simply to show people this unique skill. The factory might not need it anymore, but I refused to believe that in such a massive city, absolutely no one would recognize its value.

I asked Old Liu from the factory's carpentry team to build me a massive instructional abacus, over a meter long, painted in alternating yellow and black. I also went to the hardware store and bought a portable megaphone. After work, I found an empty space near the city's Cultural Palace, hung the giant abacus on the street wall, turned on the microphone, and started pitching.

"Everyone, stop for a moment and take a look! The abacus can calculate not only decimals, but also the hexadecimals of our old scales! One tael equals 0.0625 catties. The abacus chant goes 'One retreats six two five'—it's incredibly fast!" I shouted. "Let me demonstrate the 'Nine-Return Chant' for you..."

People flowed past on the street. Most, clutching briefcases tightly under their arms, hurried by without so much as a glance. Occasionally, a few onlookers would stop, stare at the abacus, sigh, shake their heads, and walk away.

Finally, a group of middle schoolers in uniforms, chattering loudly and carrying heavy backpacks, wandered over. Seeing my giant abacus, they actually looked interested. My spirits instantly lifted. With a swift "Make-up Multiple Multiplication" move, both my hands flew down simultaneously, flawlessly demonstrating 12,345 multiplied by 11,111.

One of the students pulled out a calculator. After punching the keys for a few seconds, he looked up in surprise. "Hey! It's actually right!"

Another student leaned in closer. "That's so amazing, Grandpa. Is this hard to learn?"

But the first student just curled his lip. "It's right, but what's the point? Grandpa, can your abacus calculate the square root of 37?"

I froze for a moment. Square root? Of course I could calculate it! In abacus calculation, there's the "Odd Number Subtraction Method" and the "Shao Guang Method."

I took a deep breath, suspending my wrists in mid-air, preparing to set up the numbers and position the beads.

"Oh man, he can't do it. Let's go, let's go," the student said, jabbing a thumb at his calculator. "6.08276! Come on, let's go play billiards!"

The children immediately chimed in agreement and ran off laughing. My hands, still suspended in mid-air, stiffened. 

Yes, I could calculate it. But no matter how exquisite the calculation, what meaning did it hold in their eyes?

The autumn wind blew past, sweeping a flurry of dry poplar leaves across the pavement. Everyone on the street was hurrying along to somewhere else, and no one needed the wooden beads in this square frame to measure their lives anymore.

I took down the abacus. Extending my right index finger along the middle beam, I steadily swept it from right to left.

*Pop.*

All the abacus beads instantly snapped back to their starting positions. This is the very first action one must perform before any calculation on an abacus. It's called "wiping the board and clearing to zero." 

I packed the abacus away into my cloth bag. *Let's go home,* I told myself. 

In this wildly rushing new era, I had already been cleared to zero.
