# Cleared to Zero

    *1998*

For many years, the loudest sound in the finance section of our machine tool factory wasn't people talking, but the "crackle and pop" of the old red sandalwood abacus in my hands.

In my early years, I was fortunate enough to meet a senior predecessor who had retired from the supply and marketing cooperative. I took him as my master and learned authentic basic skills of the "Hundred-Bead Abacus" and the interlocking fingering technique known as "Lion Rolling the Embroidered Ball." I was the perennial champion of the city's abacus calculation competition. In the realm of the finance section, as long as the crisp sound of beads rang out on my desk, the work rhythm of the others in the section would unconsciously follow me. Even later, when the factory issued everyone one of those Casio calculators with a small solar panel, I never looked at them straight on.

"If you press those things too fast, it's easy to miss a key, and there's no tactile feel," I would always say, wearing my blue cloth oversleeves and holding an enamel tea mug, teaching my several apprentices. "The abacus has spirit. When your fingers flick the beads and you hear that crisp crack, the account feels solid."

When closing the accounts at the end of the year, that was the moment that best reflected my value as Old Lin. The kraft paper vouchers on the desk piled up like a small mountain. Several calculators beeped wildly, and the young people often had to scrap everything and start over just because they pressed a wrong key in the middle. But I, rapidly flipping the ledger with my left hand, flew three fingers of my right hand like lightning. "Breaking Head Multiplication," "Nine-Return Division"—every move was smooth and natural. Amidst the crisp collision of wooden beads, a set of subsidiary ledgers worth millions was sorted out clearly by me, without a single error. Even the factory director, upon seeing me, had to politely offer a Hongtashan cigarette and call me "Master Lin."

That was the martial arts world I was proud of, and I was the supreme master sitting securely on the fishing platform in this world.

However, my nightmare finally arrived. At the beginning of the year, the factory started a "Modern Enterprise Management Pilot Program" and moved in a few clunky "Great Wall" microcomputers. Xiao Chen, a newly arrived college student, was appointed as the information supervisor. Wearing thick-rimmed glasses, holding neither an abacus nor a ledger, he shuttled around the section every day holding a few square black plastic disks.

"Master Lin, from now on our accounts will all be entered into a database. We're using FoxBASE," Xiao Chen gestured by my desk. "In the future, to check the accounts, you just hit 'Enter,' and all the summary data can be pulled up in one second."

I was completely baffled listening to this: "What Bass? What database? Where is the warehouse? Pulled up in one second, doesn't it need to be added up item by item?"

To prove his words, Xiao Chen pulled me in front of the computer. He told me this was UCDOS, with a glaring green cursor jumping on it. He asked me to report the warehouse entry details of a certain batch of steel from a month ago. I turned around to fetch that stack of thick, dust-covered ledgers. Just as I flipped halfway through, I heard the wide printer next to me let out a piercing screech of "zhi-la zhi-la."

In less than two minutes, a continuous sheet of perforated printer paper was spat out. Neatly listed on it were all the details, unit prices, total amounts, and even the material loss rate was automatically calculated and formatted.

I was completely dumbfounded.

Refusing to believe in this heresy, I ripped off that piece of paper, pulled my red sandalwood abacus in front of me, and started to recheck item by item. My thumb and index finger flew up and down, and fine beads of sweat seeped from my forehead. Half an hour later, looking at the final numbers resting on the abacus, my hand began to tremble uncontrollably.

Not a single error.

The "Lion Rolling the Embroidered Ball" I was so proud of, the fingering I had practiced for thirty years and developed thick calluses for, in front of this "microcomputer" that didn't even have a physical bead-flicking process, had become a slow, clunky, and meaningless joke.

In less than half a year, the section chief came to talk to me. "Old Lin, the financial system is completely on microcomputers now. It's all about 'accounting computerization' these days." His eyes dodged, afraid to look at the abacus on my desk. "You can't even memorize the Wubi typing method, and you can't type on the keyboard smoothly. The factory has arranged a new task for you, transferring you to the logistics warehouse. Your bookkeeping foundation still has a place to be used there. Whatever we do, it's all for contributing to the factory."

I didn't make a fuss, nor did I plead for mercy. That afternoon, I silently wrapped that red sandalwood abacus carefully in a clean red cloth. When I walked out of the finance section, there was no longer the crisp sound of the abacus inside, only the dull, monotonous "da-da-da" of keyboards, like an emotionless machine slowly chewing away more than half of my life.

After sitting on the cold bench in the warehouse for half a month, I felt I couldn't just become useless like this. Computers might be fast, but the wisdom passed down from our ancestors couldn't be broken. The addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division in the abacus hid rules and contained the square and the circle. When my master passed the skills to me back then, he had only one request: not to let this craft lose its roots in our generation's hands. I decided to set up a stall on the street, not to make money, but just to let people see this unique skill. The factory might not use it anymore, but I didn't believe that in such a huge city, no one could recognize its value.

I asked Old Liu from the factory's carpentry team to make me a large instructional abacus over a meter long, painted with yellow and black alternating paint. I also went to the hardware store to buy a portable megaphone. After work, I found an empty space near the city's Cultural Palace, hung the large abacus on the street wall, turned on the microphone, and started to holler.

"Everyone, stop your steps and take a look. The abacus can calculate not only decimals, but also the hexadecimals of our old scales! One tael equals 0.0625 catties. The abacus chant is called 'One retreats six two five', it's very fast!" "Let me demonstrate the 'Nine-Return Chant' for you..."

People came and went on the street. Most people with briefcases under their arms hurried past without even looking. Occasionally a few onlookers stopped, stared at the abacus, sighed, shook their heads, and walked away again.

Finally, a few middle school students wearing school uniforms and carrying backpacks chattered over and got interested when they saw my large abacus. My spirit was instantly lifted. With a move of "Make-up Multiple Multiplication", both hands went down together, demonstrating 12345 multiplied by 11111.

One of the students pulled out a calculator, and after a few seconds said in surprise: "Hey? It's actually correct!"

Another student also leaned over: "So amazing, Grandpa, is this hard to learn?"

As a result, the first student curled his lip: "It's correct, but what's the use? Grandpa, can your abacus calculate the square root of 37?"

I froze for a moment. Square root? Of course I can calculate it! In abacus calculation, there is the "Odd Number Subtraction Method" and also the "Shao Guang Method".

I took a deep breath, just suspending my wrists in mid-air, setting up the numbers and positioning.

"Oh man, he can't calculate it, let's go, let's go." That student pressed with his thumb, "6.08276! Let's go play billiards!"

The children immediately chimed in and ran far away laughing. My hands, suspended in mid-air, stiffened. Yes, I can calculate, but no matter how exquisite the calculation is, what meaning does it have in their eyes?

The autumn wind blew past, sweeping up a ground of poplar leaves. Everyone on the street was hurrying along, and no one needed to use the beads in this square wooden frame to measure life anymore.

I took down the abacus, extended my right index finger, and along the middle beam, from right to left, steadily swept across.

"Pop."

All the abacus beads returned to their places instantly. This is the first action that must be done before every calculation in abacus calculation, called "wiping the board and clearing to zero". I put the abacus into the cloth bag. Let's go home, I told myself. In this wildly running new era, I have already been cleared to zero.
