# Mending the Rest of Life

    *1996*

I took one last look at Ni'er, and softly said a sentence to her in my heart: "Ni'er, don't be afraid, Mom will come to accompany you soon."

Coming out of the columbarium next to the crematorium, the sky was already a bit overcast. I felt in my pocket, and only a few small-denomination bills, softened by sweat, remained. To treat Ni'er's illness, anything that could be sold had long been sold, and even that small room that could barely be called a "home" was finally taken back by the landlord.

Eight years old, she was only eight years old.

At that moment, I was very clear that my life would probably just end here.

When it was getting dark, I flagged down a red Xiali taxi by the roadside.

The heater in the car was turned up high, carrying an indescribable smell—a mix of cheap tobacco, engine oil, and a slight sourness of gasoline. The driver was a middle-aged man wearing an old leather jacket with cuffs shiny from grease.

"Where to?" He didn't look back, asking casually.

"Under the North Bridge."

His voice paused for a moment, and he glanced at me in the rearview mirror: "Big sister, that place is a muddy swamp. It's so late, what are you going there for?"

"To meet someone," I said.

He glanced at me again, asked no more, shifted gears, and started off.

The car drove all the way north, getting more desolate as it went. The streetlights disappeared, leaving only pitch-black wild land and the sound of wind blowing across the empty ground. When we arrived at the place, he stepped on the brake.

I took a deep breath and placed the few bills I was clutching hotly in my hand on the seat: "Keep the change, and no need to wait for me."

I reached out to push the door, but it didn't open. I pushed again, still wouldn't budge.

"Master, please open the door." I was a bit anxious.

He didn't move. With a click, he lit a Hongmei cigarette. The flame suddenly lit up, clearly illuminating his stubbly face. He took a drag on the cigarette, exhaled slowly, and his speech also slowed down:

"Big sister, who is down there? It's just a river. In this cold weather, are you planning to go down for a swim? Or are you bringing me bad luck?"

I froze instantly. Tears spun in my eyes but wouldn't fall no matter what, and my mouth could only say over and over again: "Open the door, I want to get out."

The more I spoke, the more certain he became. He refused to let me get out no matter what, cursed, saying he was really unlucky today, asked where my home was, and said he would drive me back for free.

At that moment, I could no longer hold on and cried out loud. I told him that I had no home, I had nothing left, and I shouldn't be alive. I begged him to let me out of the car.

After hearing this, he was silent for a while, then suddenly sneered: "Want to die? Fine, I won't stop you anymore. But we need to settle the bill first."

"This round trip is over ten kilometers, and I definitely can't take any more passengers tonight. Plus, all this bad luck you brought has made my heart uncomfortable. The compensation for mental distress is two hundred yuan. Not too much, right?"

After saying this, he turned the car around.

"The moment I open my eyes, this car means paying the company's quota money. Until you pay off what you owe, don't even think about going anywhere. Even the King of Hell doesn't accept people with debts."

Just like that, I was "kidnapped" back to his home.

It was a small single-story house, only about ten square meters. A pile of dark honeycomb briquettes was piled at the door, and the room had an engine oil smell that never dissipated year-round. The light bulb was dim, and the wall paint was peeling off.

He let me make do for a night on that folding bed in the corner, lit a cigarette himself, and asked me in a muffled voice: "What did you do before?"

"Tailor," I kept my head down, looking at the black dirt in my fingernails, "I used to run a shop. Later... to treat my child's illness, I sold everything."

He didn't respond.

After a while, he bent down, dragged a bulging snakeskin bag from under the bed, and dumped it on the ground with a clatter. It was full of old clothes; some were missing buttons, some had torn edges, and there was a red velvet scarf with a hole burned by a cigarette butt.

"As long as you can do the work," he said. "Mend these, alter the styles, and maybe we can sell them for some money. Whenever you earn enough of that two hundred yuan to pay me back, you can go wherever you want."

In the blink of an eye, the weather turned cold, and it snowed heavily, turning the whole city white.

He got some fabrics from somewhere, along with a "Butterfly" brand sewing machine with peeling paint. When the pedal was pressed down, it went "da-da-da"; the sound wasn't loud but very steady, as if it was meant to calm a person's heart.

During the day, he went out to drive the taxi, and I worked alone under the lamp. Ripping, washing, sewing, piecing together. That velvet scarf with a hole was altered by me into a small handbag; old woolen hats were embroidered with wild mountain chrysanthemums; scraps of cloth were pieced together to make headbands—those things had just started to become popular in the city at that time.

His red Xiali became my sales counter. The first transaction was a beret I crocheted, decorated with fake pearls, which sold for twenty yuan.

When he came back at night, he slapped that bill, still carrying body heat, onto the sewing machine. His rough hand accidentally touched my fingertips, and he immediately pulled it back, only saying muffledly: "The craftsmanship is okay, didn't embarrass our car."

In that instant, I saw a flash in his eyes, hotter than the warm air in the car.

Days passed just like this, like the thread under the sewing machine, stitch by stitch, slowly moving forward.

On New Year's Eve, firecrackers went off non-stop outside, and the smell of gunpowder smoke drilled in through the door cracks. He brought the car back early, carrying a bag of frozen dumplings and a small thing wrapped in old newspaper.

"From a street vendor at the entrance of the department store, looked quite bright," he said, handing the thing to me, his hands constantly rubbing together, "just... bought it in passing."

It was a red plastic hairpin, dazzlingly bright.

"Hair always blocks the eyes," he said and went to turn on the TV. It was playing the opening song of the Spring Festival Gala, lively and bustling, covering up the sound of the wind and snow outside the window.

I pinned the hairpin by my ear and looked at myself again and again in that small round mirror with a crack. The woman in the mirror had deeper wrinkles at the corners of her eyes, but her gaze was no longer dead.

Tears fell drop by drop onto the red velvet, blurring out a small dark patch.

I said softly in my heart: "Ni'er, Mom is sorry. Mom can't go to accompany you just yet."
