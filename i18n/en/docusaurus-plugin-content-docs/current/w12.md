# Mending the Rest of a Life

*1996*

I took one last look at Ni'er, and silently whispered a promise in my heart: *Ni'er, don't be afraid. Mom will come to keep you company soon.*

Walking out of the columbarium next to the crematorium, the sky had already turned an overcast, heavy gray. I dug into my pockets. All that remained were a few crumpled, small-denomination bills, softened by my own sweat. To pay for Ni'er's medical treatments, I had long since sold anything of value we owned. Even the tiny, cramped room we had barely been able to call "home" was finally repossessed by the landlord.

Eight years old. She was only eight years old.

At that moment, I was absolutely certain that my life was meant to end right there.

As the sky grew dark, I flagged down a red Xiali taxi by the side of the road.

The heater in the car was blasted high, blowing out an indescribable stench—a mix of cheap tobacco, stale engine oil, and the faint, sour tang of gasoline. The driver was a middle-aged man wearing a battered leather jacket, the cuffs worn slick and shiny from years of grease.

"Where to?" he asked casually, not bothering to look back.

"Under the North Bridge."

His voice caught for a second, and his eyes flicked up to look at me in the rearview mirror. "Big sister, that place is nothing but a muddy swamp. It's so late. What are you going out there for?"

"To meet someone," I said flatly.

He gave me another long look in the mirror, but didn't ask anything else. He shifted gears, and the car lurched forward.

We drove steadily north, the surroundings growing increasingly desolate as we went. The streetlights eventually vanished, leaving only a pitch-black wasteland and the hollow howling of the wind sweeping across the empty ground. When we finally arrived, he stepped heavily on the brake.

I took a deep breath, placed the few crumpled bills I had been clutching onto the seat, and said, "Keep the change. You don't need to wait for me."

I reached out to push the door open, but it didn't budge. I pushed harder. Still nothing. 

"Master, please open the door." I was starting to panic.

He didn't move. With a sharp *click*, he lit a Hongmei cigarette. The sudden flare of the lighter clearly illuminated his rough, stubbled face in the dark cabin. He took a long drag, exhaled slowly, and his voice dropped to a deliberate crawl.

"Big sister, who exactly is down there? It's just a freezing river. In this weather, are you planning to go for a swim? Or are you just trying to drag your bad luck onto my car?"

I froze instantly. Hot tears swam in my eyes, but I stubbornly refused to let them fall. My mouth could only repeat, over and over, "Open the door. I want to get out."

But the more I pleaded, the more certain he became. He absolutely refused to let me out. He started cursing, complaining that he was incredibly unlucky today, and demanded to know where my home was, insisting he would drive me back for free.

At that moment, my last thread of endurance snapped. I broke down and cried out loud. I screamed at him that I had no home, that I had absolutely nothing left, and that I didn't deserve to be alive. I begged him to just let me out of the car.

He sat silently for a long time, listening to me sob. Then, suddenly, he let out a cold sneer. 

"You want to die? Fine. I won't stop you. But we have a bill to settle first."

"This round trip is over ten kilometers, and I definitely can't pick up any more passengers tonight out here. Plus, all this miserable luck you've brought has made my heart incredibly uncomfortable. Compensation for my mental distress is two hundred yuan. That's not asking too much, right?"

Before I could even process what he was saying, he threw the car into reverse and spun it around.

"The moment I open my eyes every morning, this car means I owe the company their daily quota money. Until you pay off what you owe me, don't even think about going anywhere. Even the King of Hell doesn't accept people who die with debts."

And just like that, I was "kidnapped" and taken back to his home.

It was a tiny, single-story house, barely ten square meters in total. A pile of dark honeycomb coal briquettes was stacked awkwardly by the door, and the room was permanently steeped in the smell of stale engine oil. The single light bulb hanging from the ceiling was dim, and the paint on the walls was peeling off in large flakes.

He pointed to a rickety folding bed in the corner and told me to make do for the night. Lighting another cigarette, he asked in a muffled voice, "What did you do before?"

"I was a tailor," I answered, keeping my head bowed, staring at the black dirt trapped beneath my fingernails. "I used to run a small shop. Later... to pay for my child's treatments, I sold everything."

He didn't respond.

After a while, he bent down, grabbed a bulging snakeskin bag from under his bed, and dumped it onto the floor with a heavy thud. It was overflowing with old, discarded clothes. Some were missing buttons, some had frayed edges, and there was a bright red velvet scarf with a hole burned straight through it by a cigarette butt.

"As long as you can do the work," he said gruffly. "Mend these. Alter the styles. Maybe we can sell them for some money. Whenever you earn enough to pay back that two hundred yuan, you can go wherever the hell you want."

In the blink of an eye, the weather turned bitter cold, and heavy snow blanketed the entire city in white.

He managed to scrounge up some fabric from somewhere, along with an old, peeling "Butterfly" brand sewing machine. When you pressed down on the pedal, it went *da-da-da*. The sound wasn't loud, but it was incredibly steady and rhythmic—like a heartbeat designed to calm a person's soul.

During the day, he went out to drive the taxi, and I sat alone under the dim lamp. Ripping, washing, sewing, and piecing things together. I altered that ruined velvet scarf into an elegant little handbag. I embroidered bright, wild mountain chrysanthemums onto plain, old woolen hats. I gathered scraps of leftover cloth and pieced them together to make colorful headbands. At the time, those kinds of accessories had just started becoming fashionable in the city.

His red Xiali taxi became my mobile sales counter. My very first sale was a crocheted beret, decorated with fake pearls. It sold for twenty yuan.

When he came back that night, he slapped the bill—still warm from his pocket—onto the table next to the sewing machine. His rough, calloused hand accidentally brushed against my fingertips, and he yanked it back immediately, muttering, "Your craftsmanship is alright. Didn't embarrass my car."

In that brief instant, I caught a flash in his eyes. It was hotter than the blast of the heater in his cab.

The days passed exactly like this, moving forward steadily, stitch by stitch, just like the thread feeding through the sewing machine.

On New Year's Eve, firecrackers exploded non-stop outside the tiny house, the sharp smell of gunpowder smoke drifting in through the cracks in the door. He brought the car back early that night, carrying a bag of frozen dumplings and a small object wrapped tightly in an old newspaper.

"Got it from a street vendor outside the department store. Looked pretty bright," he said, holding the small package out to me. He kept nervously rubbing his hands together. "Just... bought it in passing."

It was a bright red plastic hairpin, dazzling in the dim light of the room.

"Your hair is always falling in your eyes," he muttered, turning away quickly to flick on the television. The loud, bustling opening song of the Spring Festival Gala filled the room, completely drowning out the howling wind and snow outside the window.

I pinned the bright red clip behind my ear and stared at myself, over and over, in the small, cracked round mirror on the wall. The woman looking back at me had deeper wrinkles gathering at the corners of her eyes, but her gaze was no longer dead.

Tears slipped down my cheeks, falling drop by drop onto the red velvet I was sewing, blooming into small, dark patches.

*Ni'er,* I whispered softly in my heart. *Mom is so sorry. Mom can't go to keep you company just yet.*
