# Birth Permit

*2000*

Bringing a new life into the world is rarely just a decision between two people. In our era, it felt more like an engineering project requiring endless layers of confirmation and step-by-step bureaucratic signatures, all deeply embedded within the national policy of family planning. Having a child was never a purely private family matter; rather, it was just one link within a massive institutional framework. Looking back on that experience, the path we walked was long, tortuous, and every single step was paved with forms, red stamps, and endless waiting. Let me start with getting married.

According to the Marriage Law, men who had reached the age of 22 and women who were at least 20 could legally register for marriage. However, the national policy strongly advocated for "late marriage": men over 25 and women over 23. This wasn't merely a suggestion; it was directly tied to very practical benefits, such as extra vacation days and workplace welfare.

One weekday afternoon, my fiancée and I took time off work and went to the civil affairs bureau in our district to apply for our marriage certificate. The registration process wasn't inherently complicated, but it certainly wasn't easy: household registration books (*hukou*), ID cards, official certificates from our employers, and formal photographs were all absolutely indispensable. We queued up, filled out endless forms, and paid fees for one item after another. After a full half-day had passed, we finally held that little red marriage certificate in our hands.

But we quickly realized that the marriage certificate was only the starting line. It meant our marital relationship had officially entered the radar of grassroots management. From that point on, the neighborhood committee and our employers would begin tracking our marriage and childbearing status. As for actually having a child? That was the next major hurdle.

Shortly after our wedding, the sub-district office notified us that we were required to attend mandatory family planning training. It was standard procedure. The curriculum revolved around policy propaganda, basic knowledge of prenatal care, contraceptive methods, and the like. The classroom lighting was dim, the teacher at the podium spoke in a flat, solemn drone, and everyone sitting below had their own distracted thoughts swirling in their heads.

The lecture heavily emphasized the benefits of late marriage and late childbearing, stressed the importance of genetic disease screening, and painstakingly explained common contraceptive methods. At the end of the course, every couple was handed a bag of contraceptives and a stack of propaganda materials. The underlying logic was actually glaringly clear: before you are allowed to *have* children, you must first learn how *not* to have children. From a public health perspective, this step genuinely improved many people's health awareness. But on a personal level, the overwhelming sense of being disciplined and managed left many feeling profoundly depressed.

Married couples without children were immediately added to the local family planning archives. Family planning officials from the neighborhood committee or our employers would regularly check in to assess our situation, meticulously updating our residential addresses and workplace details. In some areas, women were even required to undergo routine physical examinations to confirm they weren't pregnant or had successfully adopted birth control measures.

It was a state of continuous "confirmation." The human body seemed reduced to an object that needed to be verified at a moment's notice. My wife went to the sub-district health station for these examinations several times. While the process couldn't necessarily be called rough, it was always deeply uncomfortable. And if you didn't cooperate fully, it would inevitably sabotage your workplace performance appraisals, annual awards, and welfare benefits. For us, it meant our private lives were constantly being probed by public authority.

The real challenge, however, was applying for the official childbearing permit, commonly known as the "Birth Permit" (*Zhun Sheng Zheng*).

This process usually had to start in the district where the woman's *hukou* was registered. We ran back and forth between our employers, the neighborhood committee, and the sub-district office, endlessly filling out forms, signing documents, and collecting official stamps. We had to submit our marriage certificate, household registration books, ID cards, and formal "marriage and childbearing certificates" issued by both of our employers or neighborhood committees. The grassroots officials had to meticulously verify whether this was our first marriage and first birth, whether we had reached the approved "late childbearing age," and whether we had any prior policy violation records.

The execution of these policies differed drastically between urban and rural areas. In cities, it was strictly one child per couple. In rural areas, if certain conditions were met, a couple might apply to have a second child, but the bureaucratic procedure was even more agonizing. The specific approval levels varied by region; some required only sub-district approval, while others had to be reported all the way up to township, district, or county departments for the official record. There was no uniform timeline; everything depended entirely on the local bureaucratic pace.

During that period, we filled out so many forms that I couldn't even remember half their names. Every single page required a stark red stamp for confirmation. The more stamps accumulated, the heavier the paper seemed to feel in our hands. Finally, after endless rounds of verification, we received a certificate printed on rough, yellowed paper. Its format was crude and simple, but it bore a bright, glaring red official stamp. Only at that exact moment did we officially earn the qualification to "start trying."

Before issuing the birth permit, many jurisdictions also required couples to sign a formal family planning agreement. We signed one, too. Beneath the crest of the national emblem, we solemnly promised to have a child strictly according to policy and to implement permanent birth control measures immediately after delivery. This agreement wasn't just symbolic. Many places collected a financial deposit; the amount wasn't astronomical, but it was certainly enough to ensure people remembered the constraint. The money would only be refunded after birth control measures were officially implemented and verified. It was an institutionalized reminder: promises made to the state are never just verbal. The insertion of an IUD (intrauterine device) was the most common choice at the time, quickly becoming the "standard answer" for countless families.

Eventually, we stood at the window of the sub-district family planning office and received our pass in the truest sense—the Birth Permit, officially titled the Childbearing Service Certificate. It was emblazoned with the national emblem, usually valid for one or two years, and indicated explicit state permission to have exactly one child. Without this permit, it would be nearly impossible to secure a *hukou* for the child after birth, let alone enroll them in kindergarten or public school. It wasn't just a piece of permission; it was a literal ticket to the child's future.

It wasn't until the moment that piece of paper was safely in our hands that our tense nerves finally began to relax. We could finally go to the hospital, create a medical file, apply for a maternal health manual, and officially begin the journey of prenatal checkups.
