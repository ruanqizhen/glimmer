# Birth Permit

   *2000*

The birth of a new life is not just the decision of two people. It is more like a project that requires layers of confirmation and step-by-step signatures, embedded within the basic state policy of family planning. Childbearing is never a purely private family matter, but rather a link within the institutional framework. Looking back at that experience, what I walked was a long and tortuous path, with every step accompanied by forms, stamps, and waiting. Let me start from getting married.

According to the Marriage Law, men who have reached the age of 22 and women who have reached the age of 20 can register for marriage. However, the policy advocated late marriage: men over 25 and women over 23. This was not just an advocacy; it was also linked to practical benefits such as holidays and welfare.

On a weekday afternoon, my fiancée and I took leave from work and applied for our marriage certificate at the civil affairs bureau in our jurisdiction. Registration wasn't complicated, but it wasn't easy either: household registration book (hukou), ID card, employer's certificate, and photos were all indispensable; queuing, filling out forms, paying fees, one item after another. After half a day passed, we finally received that red marriage certificate.

We quickly realized that the marriage certificate was just the starting point. It meant our marital relationship had officially entered the field of vision of grassroots management—the neighborhood committee and our employers would start recording our marriage and childbearing status. As for having a child, that was the next hurdle.

Shortly after our wedding, the sub-district office notified us to attend family planning training. It was a routine procedure. The content revolved around policy propaganda, knowledge of healthy birth and sound care, contraceptive methods, and so on. The classroom lighting was dim, the teacher on the podium spoke in a solemn tone, and the people below had different thoughts in mind.

The lecture emphasized the benefits of late marriage and late childbearing, the importance of genetic disease screening, and also explained common contraceptive methods. At the end of the course, each person was given a bag of contraceptives and propaganda materials. The logic here was actually very clear: before you are allowed to have children, you must first learn how not to have children. From a public health perspective, this step indeed improved many people's health awareness; but from a personal feeling perspective, that sense of being disciplined made people feel quite depressed.

Married couples without children would be included in the family planning archives. Family planning officials from the neighborhood committee or employer would regularly understand the situation and update information such as residential addresses and workplaces. Some places would also require the woman to undergo routine examinations to confirm that she was not pregnant or had already adopted birth control measures.

That was a continuous "confirmation." The body seemed to become an object that needed to be verified at any time. My wife went to the sub-district health station for examinations several times. The process couldn't be called rough, but it always made people uncomfortable. If you didn't cooperate well, it would inevitably affect your appraisal for excellence and awards, as well as welfare benefits. For us, this meant our private lives were constantly touched by public power.

The real hurdle was applying for the childbearing permit, commonly known as the "Birth Permit" (Zhun Sheng Zheng).

The process usually started from the location of the woman's household registration. We ran back and forth among our employers, the neighborhood committee, and the sub-district office, filling out forms, signing, and stamping. We needed to submit our marriage certificate, household registration book, ID cards, and marriage and childbearing certificates issued by our employers or neighborhood committees. The grassroots would verify whether it was the first marriage and first birth, whether the late childbearing age was reached, and whether there were any violation records.

Policy execution differed between urban and rural areas: in cities, it was generally one child per couple; in rural areas, if conditions were met, they could apply to have a second child, but the procedure was more cumbersome. The specific approval levels varied by region; some only needed sub-district approval, while others had to be reported to township or district/county departments for the record. There was no uniform time limit standard; everything depended on the local pace.

During that time, we filled out so many forms that we couldn't remember their names. Every page required a red stamp for confirmation. The more stamps there were, the heavier the paper felt. Finally, after repeated verification, we received a certificate made of rough paper. The paper was yellowed, the format was simple, but it bore a bright red official stamp. Only at that moment did we gain the qualification to "start trying."

Before issuing the birth permit, many places would also require the signing of a family planning agreement. We signed it too. Under the national emblem design, we solemnly promised to have a child according to the policy and to implement birth control measures after delivery. The agreement was not symbolic. Many places would collect a deposit; the amount was not huge, but it was enough to make people remember this constraint. Only after birth control measures were implemented could it be refunded. That was an institutionalized reminder: promises are not just verbal. Insertion of an IUD (intrauterine device) was the most common choice at the time, becoming the "standard answer" for many families.

Eventually, we received that pass in the true sense at the window of the sub-district family planning office—the Birth Permit, also called the Childbearing Service Certificate. It was printed with the national emblem, usually valid for one or two years, indicating permission to have one child. Without the birth permit, there would be trouble getting household registration (hukou) for the child after birth, as well as entering kindergarten and school. It was not just a piece of permission, but more like a ticket to the future.

It wasn't until that moment that our tense nerves slightly relaxed. We could finally go to the hospital to create a file, apply for a maternity health manual, and then officially enter the prenatal checkup process.
