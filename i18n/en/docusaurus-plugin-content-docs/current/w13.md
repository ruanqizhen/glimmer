# The Loyal Dog

*1994*

I sat comfortably in the position of Personnel Section Chief of the City Commercial Bureau for exactly five years. Those five years were the most glorious of my entire life—and also the most profoundly muddled.

During that time, the red-and-blue pencil in my hand acted as the absolute "conductor's baton" for the entire local commercial system. Did someone want to transfer to a cushy sales job at the department store? Did the government canteen need a new purchasing agent? It all came down to a single word from me. If I nodded, it was done. The threshold of my office door was practically trampled flat by people seeking favors. Every single night, my calendar was packed with lavish dinner parties that dragged on into the early hours of the morning, and the empty Maotai liquor bottles piled up on my apartment balcony like a brick wall.

I slowly grew accustomed to the intoxicating feeling of being surrounded and fussed over. I got used to that cautious, sickeningly sweet, ingratiating tone people used whenever they called out "Section Chief Zhao." Once a person tastes the absolute sweetness of unchecked power, it is incredibly difficult not to let it completely go to your head.

Su Xiaomei was a broadcaster who had been temporarily seconded to the bureau from the county art troupe. Every day at exactly 12:30 noon, the government loudspeakers would crackle to life, and her clear, standard Mandarin would flow through the building like a cool, refreshing breeze, effortlessly suppressing the thick Xiangxi, Eastern Henan, and Northern Anhui accents that dominated our offices. She wasn't classically beautiful, but she was young, and that was more than enough. She always wore a brand of vanishing cream called "Friendship," and that faint, sweet fragrance was enough to make any man's heart soften. 

The county art troupe was on the verge of being disbanded, and she was desperate to stay in the city. To do that, she needed three things: an official staff quota, a permanent city household registration (*hukou*), and a formal conversion of her status from rural to non-rural. All three were absolutely indispensable for her survival.

The first time she knocked on my office door, she was clutching a broadcasting script to her chest. "Section Chief Zhao, could you possibly help me look over this paragraph? Is the phrasing too stiff? I'm worried the leaders won't be happy when they hear it." 

The door was left slightly ajar. It was during the lunch break, and the corridor was so dead quiet you could hear a fly buzzing against the window pane. She lowered her head, her cheeks flushing a delicate pink. I knew exactly why she had come, and she knew that I knew. Neither of us needed to speak plainly. Many things in this world are separated by nothing more than half a step—you step forward slightly, and I naturally go with the flow. I deeply enjoyed the feeling of being looked up to and desperately depended upon. When she looked up at me, her eyes were incredibly soft, carrying an unmistakable hint of ingratiation.

I deliberately sat on the issue of her official staff quota. In reality, it wouldn't have been difficult to push through, but I knew that if I handled it too quickly, she might not have a reason to stay by my side anymore. At the time, I arrogantly considered this kind of psychological manipulation to be a brilliant tactical maneuver. Thinking back on it now, it was nothing but sheer, arrogant stupidity.

Wang Zhiyuan was a newly assigned college graduate. College graduates were considered a scarce, valuable resource in our department, but upon entering a government organ, even they had to start at the bottom, drafting endless administrative materials. He wore thick, black-rimmed glasses, rarely spoke, and had an annoying habit of using incredibly dense, long-winded sentences in his official documents. He was constantly tossing out phrases like "under the premise of..." and "further strengthening the vital importance of...". I absolutely couldn't stand it. The one thing a government organ fears above all else is drafting a document that "the leadership cannot understand." 

I scolded him relentlessly, often throwing his meticulously written materials back across my desk in front of the entire section. "Who the hell can understand this nonsensical crap of yours?" I'd roar. "Rewrite it!"

Every single time, he would quietly bend down, gather the scattered pages one by one from the floor, and murmur in a voice as low as a mosquito's, "I'm very sorry, Section Chief. I will rewrite it immediately."

This was his one redeeming quality: he was as obedient as a loyal dog. Because of this, I gradually began to treat him as a trusted, reliable subordinate, even privately assigning him to handle certain sensitive matters that were too inconvenient for me to be seen handling directly.

One quiet afternoon, I was locked in the office with Little Su, preparing to "discuss her work in depth." Suddenly, the door was pushed open, and Wang Zhiyuan, clutching a manila folder, froze dead in the doorway.

Little Su and I were usually very careful about secrecy, and Wang Zhiyuan had never stumbled in on us before. Being caught completely off guard, a burst of violent irritation flared in my chest. I cursed at him casually, "Don't you have any actual work to do? Running around aimlessly during working hours! You still haven't submitted your monthly summary. If you don't hand it in by tonight, you can forget about seeing your bonus this month!"

Sitting on the sofa, Su Xiaomei let out a soft, mocking chuckle. Wang Zhiyuan slowly looked up at us. His Adam's apple bobbed heavily in his throat, and he only said a single sentence: "I'm sorry. I will go write it right now." 

He turned around and walked out. I didn't give the incident a second thought. In my arrogant eyes, he was just a dog that relied entirely on me to feed him.

That year, the bureau was allocated a strict internal quota for "rural to non-rural" status conversions. Su Xiaomei was keeping an incredibly close eye on it. One night, lying in a hotel bed, she turned to me and asked, "Jianguo, it will definitely be me this time, right?"

I smoked my cigarette in silence for a long time before finally answering. "This time... Director Li's nephew has already been pre-determined for the spot. I promise, I'll get you the next one."

She didn't cry. She just quietly sat up, put her clothes back on, and, with her back turned to me, softly said, "You say 'next time' every single time."

When she left the room, the clicking of her high heels echoing down the corridor was very slow, but she never once looked back.

Sometime later, I started noticing that Wang Zhiyuan was frequently running over to the broadcasting station. I stopped him one day and asked, "Little Wang, are you lacking things to do lately? Why are you always running over there?"

He flashed me his usual smile and said he was just helping to repair some of the faulty broadcasting lines. The smile looked exactly as honest and simple-minded as it always had.

I secretly began to guess: *Could this kid actually have designs on Su Xiaomei? Is this a good thing or a bad thing for me?* For a moment, I couldn't quite sort out the implications. But by the time I finally realized something was horribly wrong, it was far too late to fix it.

Two months later, officials from the Municipal Discipline Inspection Commission marched directly into my office. The leader's tone was ice-cold and professional. "Comrade Zhao Jianguo, we have received a detailed, real-name report. Please come with us and cooperate with the investigation."

The stack of reporting materials they had compiled was shockingly thick. Right on top was a detailed, handwritten letter by Su Xiaomei. The times, the locations, even the specific physical postures we used—every single sordid detail was meticulously documented. The handwriting clearly belonged to her, but the dense, flowing sentences, the flawless logical structure, and the perfect punctuation were unmistakably Wang Zhiyuan's signature "academic" writing style. Attached to the letter were more than a dozen photographs. They were taken from tricky, hidden angles, all secretly shot from a distance, with exact dates stamped clearly on the edges.

I suddenly understood everything. Those long afternoons he spent "repairing broadcasting lines," that total, submissive silence when he bowed his head to pick up the materials I threw at him—it had all been a meticulously laid trap.

After half a year of agonizing investigations, I felt as though an entire layer of my skin had been peeled off. Although they couldn't find any conclusive evidence of economic corruption, the charge of "severe corruption of lifestyle" was absolutely ironclad. The final penalty was devastating: placed on severe probation within the Party, stripped of all administrative posts, and permanently exiled to the bureau's rear warehouse to guard old materials, spending my remaining days dealing with garbage, rats, and the smell of mildew.

During the period I was under investigation, Little Su was quietly fired by the bureau and sent back to the county art troupe in disgrace. Wang Zhiyuan, on the other hand, was officially promoted to Deputy Section Chief.

Sometime later, I was out in the bureau's rear courtyard, miserably cleaning up a pile of sundries. Wang Zhiyuan happened to walk past. Seeing me, he strode right up, his face breaking into a wide, beaming smile. He grabbed my dirty hand and shook it vigorously. 

"Zhao! Long time no see! I wanted to come visit you a few days ago, but work has just been so incredibly busy. How have you been doing lately?"

That smile was still perfectly honest, perfectly simple-minded. I couldn't detect the slightest thrill of revenge in his eyes, nor was there any gloating. And there certainly wasn't a single trace of pity.
