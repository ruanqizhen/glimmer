# Uncle Zhou

    *1997*

In this family, I was "Uncle Zhou" for a full seven years. Although on the household registration book, I was Minmin's father, in her heart, I was just "Uncle Zhou."

When Minmin followed Xiulan and married into my home, she was only six years old. The little girl was thin, didn't like to talk, and always had a bit of wariness when looking at people. The first time we met, she hid behind her mother and timidly called me "Uncle Zhou."

That call lasted for seven years, and I never forced her to change her form of address. Some things cannot be rushed; saplings need to grow slowly, and the human heart is the same.

In order to make this reconstituted family have less awkwardness and more warmth, I put almost all my spare time into it. Minmin liked drawing, an extremely money-burning hobby. A box of imported watercolor paints was equivalent to half a month's worth of my cigarette money; a set of paintbrushes could make me stare at my salary slip and worry for half a day.

Every Tuesday and Friday evening, I would ride that "Forever" (Yongjiu) brand bicycle—where everything rattled except the bell—to take her to the City Cultural Palace. Winter was the hardest to endure; the north wind poured in from the street corner, blowing so hard it made people's ears hurt. I wore my military greatcoat backward and wrapped her in the front, like wrapping a ball of cotton. She shrank her neck along the way, while I pedaled behind her, sweating profusely.

She went inside to draw plaster busts, and I waited outside. Sometimes the gatekeeper uncle was kindhearted and let me into the reception room to warm up by the stove for a while; most of the time, I just squatted under the streetlight reading borrowed martial arts novels, or took out cigarette pack paper and calculated stroke by stroke on the back how much money was left this month. For two and a half hours, she learned to draw inside, and I learned how to manage life outside.

Later she won the second prize in the junior group and excitedly held the certificate up in front of me. I was as happy as if I had been admitted to a university myself. That night, I specially bought half a catty of pig head meat and a bottle of soda. Minmin drank the soda and smiled; I looked at her smiling and felt that the money was really well spent.

Although we were poor, I always felt that life was moving in a better direction. But what happened a few days ago completely shattered my hope.

Mountain bikes started to become popular in Minmin's junior high school. That kind of bike with derailleurs, front fork suspension, and handlebars tilted up like ox horns looked more impressive to the children than a car.

During dinner that evening, she suddenly said: "Uncle Zhou, I want to buy a Giant (Jie'ante) bike, a purple one."

My heart skipped a beat. An authentic Giant bike cost over eight hundred yuan. And I had just paid the heating bill a couple of days ago; my salary hadn't been issued yet, and I couldn't even scrape together two hundred yuan in my pocket. I put down my chopsticks, trying to make my tone sound a bit more relaxed:

"Minmin, eight hundred yuan is indeed a bit expensive. Dad went to the flea market to look; there is a refurbished ladies' bike, over two hundred yuan, also purple. I'll buy it back and repaint it for you, it'll be just like new, okay?"

Minmin raised her head and slammed her chopsticks on the table: "I knew it! What else can you do besides saving money? Wang Jia's dad is also a stepfather, how come he bought one for her?"

Stepfather! These two words pierced down like an awl. The words that no one had said in seven years were finally spoken. She slammed the door and went back to her room.

Xiulan sat by the dining table secretly wiping tears. I patted my wife's hand and said nothing.

A few days later, the peace was completely broken. Xiulan's ex-husband came back. That man had disappeared for exactly ten years. I heard he had made a fortune dealing in electronic components in the south over these years, opened a company, became a boss, and earned a lot of money. He asked someone to pass a message, wanting to see his daughter.

Xiulan trembled with anger. "What was he doing earlier? Now he remembers he still has a daughter?"

I smoked all night. When it was almost dawn, I said to Xiulan: "Let him see her. You can't stop things like blood ties. The more we stop him, the more Minmin will resent us in the future."

The day of the meeting was set for Friday. Xiulan and I specially took a leave of absence. She put on her woolen coat from the bottom of the trunk, and I wore that Mao suit I usually couldn't bear to wear. We planned to pick up Minmin at school first and then go to the restaurant together. At least to be a bit decent. Even if it was just the last decency of a poor father.

The setting sun bathed the school gate in golden yellow. The dismissal bell rang, and students surged out like a tide. Suddenly, Xiulan grabbed my arm, her hands icy cold.

Following her gaze, I saw Minmin standing in the center of the crowd, pushing a brand-new purple Giant bike in her hands. The English logo on the bike shone blindingly in the setting sun, and she was surrounded by classmates. She smiled so happily, a happiness I hadn't seen in many years.

A boy asked enviously: "Minmin, who bought you this bike?"

Minmin raised her chin, her voice crisp and loud: "My dad bought it! Brought it back from Guangzhou! My dad is a big boss, this is nothing!"

The surroundings immediately erupted in gasps of admiration. The boy asked again: "Then what about the one who often drops you off and picks you up?"

Minmin waved her hand, as if talking about an unimportant trivial matter. "Oh, don't mention him. That stingy ghost, who cares about him."

I stood outside the crowd, suddenly feeling the surroundings were exceptionally quiet. My ears were clearly full of the sounds of students talking, but I couldn't hear anything.

Xiulan was so angry she was about to rush over. I grabbed her: "Don't go."

I heard my own voice, terribly hoarse. "The child is happy. Let her be."

After saying that, I turned around and pushed that old "Forever" bike. The chain made a clattering sound. It was the same as before, only the bike was still that bike, but the person seemed to have suddenly aged a lot.

Along the way, I thought of many things. I thought of the streetlight at the entrance of the Cultural Palace, thought of the heat inside the military greatcoat in winter, thought of the way she threw herself into my arms holding the certificate. Those things I thought would always stay were not as solid as I had imagined. Later I finally understood one thing: upbringing and blood ties are never a fair competition. Blood ties are a gift you are born with, while upbringing is more like a long job. It has no certificates, no medals, and may not necessarily yield a return. You just give day after day, and then wait. Sometimes you can wait for a "Dad," sometimes you wait for nothing.
