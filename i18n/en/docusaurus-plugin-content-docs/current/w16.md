# Uncle Zhou

*1997*

In our house, I was known as "Uncle Zhou" for seven long years. Even though my name was written on the official household registry as Minmin's father, in her heart, I was only ever "Uncle Zhou."

When Minmin followed her mother, Xiulan, and married into my home, she was only six years old. The little girl was terribly thin, rarely spoke, and always watched people with a guarded, wary look in her eyes. The very first time we met, she hid entirely behind her mother's legs and timidly called me "Uncle Zhou."

That single form of address lasted for seven years. I never once forced her to change it. Some things simply cannot be rushed; saplings need time to grow slowly, and the human heart is exactly the same.

In order to smooth over the awkwardness of our blended family and try to build some genuine warmth, I poured almost all of my spare time and energy into her. Minmin loved to draw—an exceptionally money-burning hobby for a family like ours. A single box of imported watercolor paints cost the equivalent of half a month's cigarette money for me; a decent set of paintbrushes was enough to make me stare at my meager salary slip and agonize for half the day.

Every Tuesday and Friday evening, I'd ride my battered "Forever" brand bicycle—a bike where absolutely every part rattled loudly except the bell—to take her to the City Cultural Palace. Winter was always the hardest season to endure. The freezing north wind would pour through the street corners, blowing so fiercely it made your ears physically ache. I would wear my heavy military coat backward, wrapping her entirely inside the front of it until she looked like a giant ball of cotton. She would shrink her neck down to stay warm the whole way, while I pedaled frantically behind her, sweating profusely despite the freezing cold.

Once we arrived, she would go inside a warm room to sketch plaster busts, and I would wait outside. Occasionally, the old uncle guarding the gate would take pity on me and let me into the reception room to warm my hands by his coal stove. Most of the time, though, I just squatted outside under the dim streetlight, reading borrowed martial arts novels, or smoothing out empty cigarette packs to meticulously calculate on the back how much money we had left for the month. For two and a half hours, she learned how to draw on the inside, and I learned how to manage our survival on the outside.

Later on, she won second prize in the junior division of a city-wide art competition. When she came running out and excitedly held the certificate up in front of my face, I was as overjoyed as if I had just been admitted to a prestigious university myself. That night, I splurged and bought half a pound of braised pork and a cold bottle of soda. Minmin drank the sweet soda and beamed; I watched her smile and felt, down to my bones, that the money had been incredibly well spent.

Although we were very poor, I honestly felt that our lives were steadily moving in a better direction. But what happened a few days ago completely shattered my hope.

Mountain bikes had recently become the absolute latest craze at Minmin's junior high school. Those sleek bikes with multi-speed gears, front fork suspension, and handlebars tilted sharply upward like ox horns looked more impressive to those kids than a luxury car.

During dinner one evening, she suddenly blurted out, "Uncle Zhou, I want to buy a Giant (*Jie'ante*) bike. A purple one."

My heart instantly skipped a beat. An authentic Giant mountain bike cost over eight hundred yuan. I had just paid the massive winter heating bill a few days ago; my next paycheck hadn't hit yet, and I couldn't have scraped together two hundred yuan if I emptied every single pocket I owned. I slowly put down my chopsticks, desperately trying to keep my tone light and relaxed.

"Minmin, eight hundred yuan is a bit too expensive for us right now. But Dad actually went to the flea market today to take a look. There's a refurbished ladies' bike there for just over two hundred yuan, and it's also purple! I'll buy it, bring it home, and repaint it for you myself. It'll look exactly like brand new. How about that?"

Minmin abruptly raised her head and slammed her chopsticks violently onto the table. 

"I knew it!" she screamed. "What else can you do besides pinch pennies? Wang Jia's dad is also a stepfather, so how come he bought one for her?"

*Stepfather!* Those two words pierced straight into my chest like an ice pick. The word that no one had dared to speak aloud in seven years had finally been weaponized. She stormed off, slamming her bedroom door violently behind her.

Xiulan sat frozen by the dining table, silently wiping away tears. I just patted my wife's hand and didn't say a single word.

A few days later, our fragile peace was completely destroyed. Xiulan's ex-husband suddenly returned to the city. 

The man had vanished without a trace for exactly ten years. Rumor had it that he had made an absolute fortune dealing in electronic components down south, opened a massive company, become a wealthy boss, and was practically swimming in cash. He sent a message through a mutual acquaintance, demanding to see his daughter.

Xiulan trembled with sheer, furious anger. "What the hell has he been doing for the last ten years? He suddenly remembers he has a daughter now?"

I chain-smoked the entire night. When the sky finally began to lighten toward dawn, I turned to Xiulan and said, "Let him see her. You can't sever something as deep as a blood tie. The harder we try to stop him, the more Minmin will ultimately resent us in the future."

The meeting was scheduled for Friday afternoon. Xiulan and I specifically took a half-day off from work. She dug her nicest woolen coat out from the very bottom of our trunk, and I put on the formal Mao suit that I usually couldn't bear to wear. We planned to pick Minmin up at the school gates first, and then the three of us would go to the restaurant together to meet him. At the very least, we wanted to look decent. Even if it was just the final, desperate decency of a poor father.

The setting sun bathed the crowded school gates in a rich golden yellow. The dismissal bell rang loudly, and the students surged out like a chaotic tide. Suddenly, Xiulan gasped and grabbed my arm, her hands turning icy cold.

Following her shocked gaze, I saw Minmin. She was standing proudly in the dead center of a massive crowd of students, her hands gripping the handlebars of a brand-new, metallic purple Giant mountain bike. The silver English logo on the frame gleamed blindingly in the setting sun. She was completely surrounded by her awestruck classmates, and she was smiling so brightly—a pure, unadulterated happiness I hadn't seen on her face in years.

A boy next to her asked enviously, "Minmin, who bought you this bike?"

Minmin raised her chin high, her voice crisp and ringing out loudly over the crowd. "My dad bought it! He brought it all the way back from Guangzhou! My dad is a massive boss now, so this is nothing to him!"

The surrounding students immediately erupted in loud gasps of admiration. The boy asked again, "Then what about the guy who usually drops you off and picks you up on that broken bike?"

Minmin waved her hand dismissively, as if swatting away a mildly annoying fly. "Oh, don't mention him. That stingy ghost? Who cares about him."

I stood frozen outside the edge of the crowd. Suddenly, the entire world felt exceptionally, terrifyingly quiet. I knew my ears were full of the chaotic sounds of hundreds of students talking and shouting, but I couldn't hear a single thing.

Xiulan was so furious she was shaking, preparing to rush into the crowd. I grabbed her arm tightly. "Don't go."

I heard my own voice. It sounded terribly, brokenly hoarse. "The child is happy right now. Just let her be."

After saying that, I turned around and began pushing my battered old "Forever" bicycle away from the school. The rusty chain clattered loudly against the guard. It was the exact same sound it had always made. The bicycle was still the exact same bicycle. But the man pushing it felt as though he had suddenly aged twenty years.

Walking home, my mind raced through a thousand different memories. I thought about the dim streetlight outside the Cultural Palace. I thought about the suffocating heat inside that military coat in the dead of winter. I thought about the pure joy on her face when she threw herself into my arms holding that art certificate. I had foolishly believed those moments would stay with us forever, but they weren't nearly as solid as I had imagined.

Later on, I finally understood one bitter truth: upbringing and blood ties are never a fair competition. Blood ties are an absolute gift you are born with. Upbringing, on the other hand, is more like a grueling, lifelong job. It offers no official certificates, it awards no medals, and it carries absolutely no guarantee of a return on your investment. You just give, day after exhausting day, and then you wait. 

Sometimes, if you are very lucky, you wait long enough to hear the word "Dad." And sometimes, you wait for absolutely nothing at all.
