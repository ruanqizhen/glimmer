# Knees

*1984*

When I was fifteen years old, our village was suddenly submerged in a restless, violent shade of red.

It happened at noon in the dead of midsummer. The sun was so fierce it threatened to bake and crack the bark of the ancient locust tree at the edge of the village. My primary school teacher, Mr. Zhang, was dragged violently out of his dark, thatched cottage. 

Mr. Zhang was the only person in our entire village who had ever studied in the city. He was an incredibly polite man; whenever he saw anyone, he would always be the first to nod in greeting. He usually spoke in a soft, gentle voice, and no matter how exhausted he was from working in the fields, he never uttered a single word of complaint. But on that scorching day, he was suddenly branded a "class enemy."

"Kneel down!"

Wang Er, the man who drove the large cart for the production team, bellowed the order. His breath reeked heavily of cheap tobacco and harsh liquor. Along the way, he had ransacked Mr. Zhang's home and smashed several of his fine porcelain bowls against the stone steps. The sound of the shattering porcelain was crisp and jarring; the pale, bluish-white fragments scattered wildly across the ground, gleaming coldly in the harsh sunlight.

"Confess your crimes to the Great Leader! You stinking ninth category!"

Mr. Zhang didn't say a single word. His thin body swayed unsteadily, and then, like a dead tree finally broken by the wind, he sank to his knees directly onto that jagged pile of broken porcelain.

I was standing not far from him. In that terrifying silence, I clearly heard the sickening sound of jagged porcelain piercing into human flesh—a low, muffled tearing—followed immediately by the horrific grinding of bone pressing against hard stone. A thin, bright stream of blood began to flow down the stone steps, soaking a dark, ugly patch into the dry, cracked earth. Heavy beads of sweat dripped from Mr. Zhang's forehead, mixing with the metallic tang of blood, fermenting rapidly in the suffocating heat wave.

Hiding amidst the crowd, my own knees felt weak, trembling in waves. I desperately wanted to step back, but I didn't dare move an inch. I stole terrified glances at the adults surrounding me. Some had buried their heads as low as possible; others stared ahead with hollow, dead eyes. It was a coldness so absolute it threw my heart into a blind panic.

Just then, my classmate Li Huzi suddenly rushed forward from the crowd. Huzi was a timid boy, usually the one always being bullied by the older kids, but that day he seemed possessed by a completely different spirit. He threw himself at Mr. Zhang, slapping the teacher's face viciously, left and right. The sharp cracks of his palms against flesh were piercing. He shouted louder and more aggressively than anyone else, as if desperately trying to announce to the entire village that the harder he hit, the safer he would be.

At that exact moment, I had only one coherent thought left in my mind: *I absolutely cannot kneel there.*

From that day forward, I became the most active, zealous person in the entire village. 

Whenever a new political directive came down from above, I scrambled over everyone else to reach the big loudspeaker at the village entrance so I could read the documents first. Whenever the production team held "criticism meetings," I stayed up all night drafting fiery speeches. I knew absolutely nothing about those "big shots" far away in the capital city, but I still utilized the most vicious, venomous words I could possibly think of to curse them.

Standing up on the dirt platform and leading the readings, my voice was always the loudest. I wouldn't stop screaming, even when my voice broke and my throat bled; I had to make absolutely sure that everyone heard the depth of my "political consciousness." I believed that only if I appeared angrier and more unyielding than everyone else would the village consider me "one of their own." And only then would I never have to worry about when it would be my turn for my knees to be punished.

During those dark years, I existed like a mindless puppet driven entirely by fear. Every single day when I woke up, my first instinct was to carefully align my political statements. Before stepping out my door, I always checked the direction of the political wind. Before uttering a single syllable, I weighed the deadly weight of my own words.

Much later, the broken porcelain was finally swept away, and the bloodstains on the stone steps were scrubbed clean by years of rain. But I always felt that the bloody mark had never truly disappeared; it had simply retreated inside my own body. 

Even to this day, whenever I hear a sudden, loud noise, I subconsciously bend my back.
