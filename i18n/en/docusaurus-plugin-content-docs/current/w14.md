# Knees

    *1984*

When I was fifteen years old, the village was suddenly submerged by a restless red color.

That was a noon in midsummer. The sun was so fierce it could bake and crack the bark of the old locust tree. My primary school teacher, Mr. Zhang, was dragged out of his dark thatched cottage. Mr. Zhang was the only person in the village who had studied in the city; he was very polite and would nod first whenever he saw anyone. Usually, he spoke softly and gently, and no matter how tired he was from working, he never uttered a sound. But that day, he became a "class enemy."

"Kneel down!"

Wang Er, who drove the big cart in the production team, yelled with a mouth full of the smell of smoke and liquor. Along the way, he smashed a few fine porcelain bowls, which had been rummaged out of Mr. Zhang's home, onto the stone steps. The sound of the porcelain bowls shattering was crisp and abrupt; the pale bluish-white fragments scattered all over the ground, gleaming coldly in the sunlight.

"Confess your crimes to the Great Leader! You stinking ninth category!"

Mr. Zhang didn't say a word. His body swayed, and like a dead tree broken by the wind, he kneeled down onto that pile of broken porcelain pieces.

I stood not far from him. At that moment, I clearly heard the sound of porcelain pieces piercing into flesh and skin—low and muffled—followed immediately by the friction sound of bones pressing against hard objects. A thin stream of blood flowed down the stone steps, soaking out a dark patch on the dry, cracked earth. The beads of sweat on his forehead dripped down, mixing with the smell of blood, fermenting in the heat wave.

Mixed in the crowd, my knees felt weak in waves. I wanted to step back, but didn't dare to move. I stole glances at the adults around me; some buried their heads very low, some had hollow eyes—it was so cold it made one's heart panic.

Just then, my classmate Li Huzi rushed out. Huzi was usually always bullied by others, but that day he seemed like a different person. He pounced on Mr. Zhang, slapping his face left and right; the sound of the slaps was piercing to the ears. He shouted harder than anyone else, as if announcing to the whole village: the harder he hit, the safer he would be.

At that moment, I only had one thought left—I absolutely could not kneel there.

From then on, I became the most active person in the village. As soon as there was a new spirit from above, I scrambled to rush to the big loudspeaker at the village entrance to read the documents; when the team held criticism meetings, I wrote speeches all night long. I didn't know those "big shots" far away in the capital city at all, but I still used the most vicious words and sentences I could think of to curse them.

Standing on the dirt platform leading the reading, my voice was always the loudest. I wouldn't stop even when I shouted until my voice broke; I had to let people hear my "political consciousness." Only if I was angrier and firmer than others would people think I was "one of their own," and only then wouldn't I have to worry about when it would be my turn for my knees to be punished.

During those years, I was like a puppet driven by fear. Every day when I woke up, I first aligned my statements; before going out, I first checked the direction of the wind; before speaking, I first weighed the weight of my words.

Later, the broken porcelain pieces were swept away, and the bloodstains on the stone steps were washed clean by the rain. But I always felt that the bloody mark had not disappeared; it stayed inside my body. Up to this day, as long as I hear a slightly loud noise, I will subconsciously bend my back.
