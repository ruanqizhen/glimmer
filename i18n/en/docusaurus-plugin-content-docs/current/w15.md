# Night Bus at Pingshi

   *1996*

When it was nearing the end of the year, several fellow villagers and I settled our wages as plasterers in Huadu, Guangzhou. We stuffed our bedrolls and tools into snakeskin bags and squeezed onto a long-distance passenger bus heading for Hengyang, Hunan.

It was a "Yellow River" brand bus, square-headed and square-brained, its paint having long faded to gray. Most of the shock-absorbing steel plates were probably broken long ago. On the damaged sections of National Highway 107, the entire cabin jolted violently, and the windows clattered loudly, feeling like it would fall apart at any moment. The seats had long been filled, and the aisles were packed with people sitting on their luggage. The air was mixed with the bitter burnt smell of Hongmei cigarettes, the sour smell of sweat, and the rancid odor coming from a few pairs of shoeless feet.

When the bus reached the vicinity of Pingshi in Shaoguan, the sky was completely dark. The mountains of Nanling pressed down layer upon layer; only the headlights split open a short stretch of grayish-white road ahead. Suddenly, with a "clatter," the bus stopped abruptly. The doors opened, and the cold wind, carrying the moisture of the mountains, poured in fiercely, bringing a person up with it.

The dim little light bulb on the roof flickered with the engine's idling speed. By that bit of light, I saw that she was in her early twenties, not tall, with a thin figure. She wore a beige belted trench coat, a style often worn by young women in the city; the belt was tied loosely, and inside was a white shirt. Her lower body wore straight-leg pants ironed perfectly straight, and on her feet was a pair of dusty, shallow-cut leather shoes.

She had obviously walked a long way. Her hair was blown messy by the night wind, and traces of careful makeup could still be seen on her face: thin eyebrows and light lipstick, only her lips were already chapped.

Among this bus full of dusty Mao suits, old camouflage uniforms, and military greatcoats covered in cement dust, she looked particularly out of place.

"Where to?" the driver asked impatiently.

She put her hand into the inner pocket of her trench coat, felt around for a long time, and finally took out a crumpled banknote, saying softly: "To the town ahead."

The driver tore off a small stub of a ticket and said no more.

There were no empty seats in the cabin. She could only stand beside the engine cover at the front of the bus. That was the most uncomfortable place in the whole vehicle. The sheet metal was scalding hot, the vibration of the diesel engine drilled straight into the bones through the soles of the shoes, and the noise was so loud that one had to shout to speak.

She held a canvas bag that had been washed white, her two hands gripping it tightly, as if afraid it would be snatched away.

"Hey, look," my childhood friend Heipi poked me with his elbow, lowered his voice and smiled, "City girl."

He had half a Hongmei cigarette dangling from his mouth, not caring as the ash fell onto his pants. Next to him, Erlengzi, sitting on a snakeskin bag, raised his head, followed his gaze, and also grinned.

Having worked on the construction site for a year, there was nothing but dust and cursing, and it was hard to even see the shadow of a woman once. All desires and pent-up frustrations were pressed down in the day-after-day brick-moving and cement-mixing; once a different scent appeared, it was like the gas in a sealed jar had suddenly found an outlet.

Heipi kicked me and said: "You call out to her."

I originally didn't intend to move, but several people around me were looking at me, as if waiting to watch a show. I didn't want to seem like a wet blanket, nor did I want to be called gutless. With a rush of blood to my head, I stretched my leg into the aisle and yelled at her: "Sister, no seat, right? Standing is so tiring. Come squeeze in here, bro's leg is warm, you can use it as a stool."

As soon as the words fell, a burst of roaring laughter immediately rang out around me. Heipi whistled: "Come on over, don't be shy."

Erlengzi patted the snakeskin bag and said: "I'll make some room for you."

She didn't move, didn't even turn her head. She just held the canvas bag tightly, stood in place, her gaze crossing the door window, staring at the boundless dark night outside. The cold wind poured in through the crack of the door, blowing the hair on her forehead. Her side profile looked so cold and aloof in the flickering light.

Our laughter gradually thinned out. This kind of silent rejection was more embarrassing than cursing. The smile on Heipi's face tightened, and he pushed me hard: "Go on."

I stumbled to the front of her. She looked up, and that young and tired face revealed unconcealable delicacy. An inexplicable restlessness suddenly rose in my heart. I reached out and pinched her cheek lightly.

"Ah!" She suddenly shrank towards the door, as if burned by fire, her voice thin and trembling: "Don't touch me..."

In that instant, I saw her eyes clearly. Fear, humiliation, and a kind of desperately suppressed despair. That look was so much like my timid and introverted younger sister far away in our hometown. My heart suddenly sank heavily, like stepping on empty air from a height. That bit of tipsiness and excitement brought by the jeering just now vanished without a trace instantly.

Heipi stood up and shouted: "Pretending to be aloof? Dressing like this, aren't you just coming out to sell?"

The roaring laughter in the cabin grew louder. But I lowered my head, retreated back to my seat, and pretended to tidy up the woven bag by my feet, avoiding everyone's gaze like a deserter. Just when I thought she would also yield and slump down, letting the malice of this whole cabin continue to bully her, she violently shook her messy long hair, like a small beast driven to the edge of a cliff, and used all her strength to let out a shrill and miserable roar: "Stop the bus! I want to get off!" The voice was hoarse, but as sharp as a knife.

The driver glanced in the rearview mirror and cursed: "We haven't reached the station yet!"

"I want to get off!" she shouted again.

The bus finally stopped by the winding mountain road. As soon as the door opened, she jumped off. The headlights illuminated a small patch of wasteland, next to which was an abandoned car repair shed, its iron roof rusted black. Further away was a bottomless valley. She didn't look back. The beige trench coat flashed in the night and was quickly swallowed by the darkness.

The door closed. The passenger bus restarted and continued heading north.

Heipi leaned against the back of the seat and soon started snoring. Erlengzi also shrank his neck and fell asleep. It was as if nothing had happened.

Only I couldn't sleep. Outside the window was the boundless dark night, occasionally brushing past the shadows of a few trees. I suddenly remembered when she saw me off from our hometown, my younger sister standing at the village entrance was also this thin and small. I had also been cursed as a country bumpkin, had my wages owed by contractors, and been pushed away by city people with that kind of invisible contempt. Yet just now, I had become the kind of person I hated the most.

I closed my eyes, but I still saw the way she looked at the front of the bus.
