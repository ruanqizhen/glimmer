# The Night Bus at Pingshi

*1996*

As the end of the year closed in, a few of my fellow villagers and I finally collected our wages as plasterers at a construction site down in Huadu, Guangzhou. We stuffed our dirty bedrolls and heavy tools into cheap woven sacks and squeezed onto a long-distance bus heading north for Hengyang, Hunan.

It was an old Yellow River bus, square-headed and boxy, its original paint completely faded to a dull, depressing gray. Most of the leaf springs in the suspension had probably snapped years ago. As we rumbled over the badly damaged sections of National Highway 107, the entire cabin jolted violently, and the loose windows clattered so loudly it felt like the bus might vibrate itself to pieces at any given moment. Every seat had been filled long ago, and the narrow aisles were packed shoulder-to-shoulder with people sitting miserably on their luggage. The stifling air was thick with a nauseating cocktail: the bitter, burnt tang of cheap Hongmei cigarettes, the sour stench of unwashed sweat, and the rancid odor wafting up from several pairs of shoeless feet.

By the time the bus reached the vicinity of Pingshi in Shaoguan, the sky was completely pitch-black. The massive, towering peaks of the Nanling Mountains pressed down around us, layer upon layer; only the bus's weak headlights managed to slice open a short, grayish-white stretch of the treacherous road ahead. 

Suddenly, with a violent *clatter*, the bus jerked to a halt. The pneumatic doors hissed open, and the freezing mountain wind poured fiercely into the cabin, carrying the heavy moisture of the wilderness—and bringing a single passenger up with it.

The dim, flickering little light bulb on the cabin roof pulsed weakly in time with the engine's idling speed. By that meager bit of light, I saw her. 

She looked to be in her early twenties, not particularly tall, with a thin, fragile frame. She was wearing a beige, belted trench coat—a stylish cut often favored by young, fashionable women in the major cities. The belt was tied loosely, revealing a crisp white shirt underneath. She wore straight-leg trousers ironed perfectly crisp, and a pair of shallow-cut leather shoes that were now heavily caked in dust.

She had obviously been walking for a very long time. Her hair had been whipped into a chaotic mess by the night wind, but the traces of careful, deliberate makeup were still visible on her face: delicately drawn eyebrows and a light shade of lipstick, though her lips were already badly chapped from the cold.

Standing in that filthy cabin full of dusty Mao suits, faded camouflage uniforms, and heavy military coats caked in cement dust, she looked jarringly, tragically out of place.

"Where to?" the driver barked impatiently.

She slipped a hand into the inner pocket of her trench coat, felt around nervously for a long time, and finally produced a single, crumpled banknote. "To the town ahead," she said softly.

The driver snatched the money, tore off a small ticket stub, and said nothing more.

There wasn't a single empty seat left anywhere in the cabin. She had no choice but to stand right beside the massive engine cover at the very front of the bus. It was universally known as the most miserable spot in the entire vehicle. The exposed sheet metal radiated scalding heat, the violent vibration of the diesel engine drilled straight up through the soles of your shoes into your bones, and the mechanical roar was so deafening you had to scream just to be heard.

She stood awkwardly, clutching a washed-out canvas bag tightly against her chest with both hands, looking terrified that someone might snatch it away.

"Hey, look," my childhood friend Heipi muttered, poking my ribs with his elbow. He lowered his voice and smirked. "City girl."

He had half a lit Hongmei cigarette dangling loosely from his lips, not caring in the slightest as the hot ash fell directly onto his pants. Next to him, Erlengzi, who was squatting on a lumpy woven sack, raised his head, followed Heipi's gaze, and broke into a leering grin.

After spending an entire year trapped on a grueling construction site, our lives had been reduced to nothing but choking dust and endless cursing. It was incredibly rare to even see a woman. All the natural desires and pent-up, suffocating frustrations had been violently repressed beneath the day-after-day grind of moving bricks and mixing cement. The sudden appearance of a different, softer scent in that confined space was like finding a sudden outlet for a highly pressurized jar of gas.

Heipi kicked my shin lightly. "You call out to her."

I had absolutely no intention of moving, but the guys around me were already staring, eagerly waiting for a show. I didn't want to seem like a wet blanket, and I definitely didn't want to be mocked as gutless by my own crew. With a sudden, stupid rush of blood to my head, I stretched my leg out into the cramped aisle and yelled over the engine, "Hey, sister! No seat, right? Standing is exhausting. Come squeeze in over here! Bro's leg is nice and warm, you can use it as a stool!"

The moment the crude words left my mouth, a burst of roaring, raucous laughter erupted from the men around me. 

Heipi let out a piercing wolf-whistle. "Come on over! Don't be shy!"

Erlengzi violently patted his dusty woven sack. "I'll make some room for you right here!"

She didn't move. She didn't even turn her head to look at us. She just clutched her canvas bag tighter to her chest, planted her feet, and kept her gaze fixed firmly out the door window, staring desperately into the boundless, impenetrable night. The freezing wind poured in through the cracks in the door, violently blowing the loose hair across her forehead. In the flickering, sickly yellow light, her profile looked incredibly cold and entirely aloof.

Our cruel laughter gradually began to thin out. That kind of absolute, silent rejection was somehow far more embarrassing than if she had simply turned and cursed at us. The leering smile on Heipi's face tightened into an ugly scowl. He shoved my shoulder hard. "Go on."

I stood up awkwardly and stumbled down the swaying aisle until I was standing right in front of her. She finally looked up at me. Beneath the exhaustion, that young face revealed an unconcealable, fragile delicacy. An inexplicable, restless heat suddenly flared in my chest. 

Without thinking, I reached out and pinched her cheek.

"Ah!" She shrieked, violently shrinking back against the metal door as if she had just been burned by a hot iron. Her voice was thin and trembling violently. "Don't touch me..."

In that single instant, I finally saw her eyes clearly.

There was sheer terror, deep humiliation, and a kind of desperately suppressed, hollow despair. That exact look was eerily identical to the expression I used to see on the face of my own timid younger sister, hundreds of miles away back in our home village. 

My heart suddenly sank like a stone, the sensation exactly like stepping blindly onto empty air from a great height. That fleeting, ugly rush of excitement brought on by the jeering men vanished without a trace, replaced instantly by profound shame.

But Heipi had already stood up. "Pretending to be aloof?" he shouted angrily over the engine. "Dressing up like that in the middle of nowhere? Aren't you just coming out here to sell it?"

The roaring laughter in the cabin exploded again, louder and uglier than before. But I just lowered my head in shame, retreated silently back to my seat, and pretended to fuss with the woven bag by my feet. I avoided everyone's gaze, feeling exactly like a pathetic deserter.

Just when I assumed she would finally yield, slump down, and allow the crushing malice of the entire cabin to endlessly bully her, she suddenly whipped her messy long hair back. Like a small, desperate beast driven to the absolute edge of a cliff, she gathered every ounce of strength she possessed and let out a shrill, miserable roar. 

"Stop the bus! I want to get off!" 

Her voice was ragged and hoarse, but it cut through the noise as sharp as a knife.

The driver glanced annoyed into his rearview mirror and shouted back, "We haven't reached the station yet!"

"I want to get off!" she screamed again.

Reluctantly, the bus hissed and jerked to a halt on the shoulder of the winding mountain road. The moment the pneumatic doors popped open, she jumped blindly into the dark. The headlights briefly illuminated a small patch of barren wasteland beside an abandoned, rusted-out car repair shed. Just beyond that lay a pitch-black, bottomless valley. 

She didn't look back even once. Her beige trench coat flashed briefly in the headlights and was instantly swallowed whole by the overwhelming darkness of the Nanling Mountains.

The door hissed shut. The passenger bus grumbled back to life and continued rumbling north.

Heipi leaned his head against the back of his seat and was soon snoring loudly. Erlengzi shrank his neck down into his collar and quickly fell asleep. It was as if absolutely nothing of consequence had just happened.

I was the only one who couldn't sleep. I just stared out the window into the boundless dark night, watching the fleeting shadows of gnarled trees brush past the glass. I suddenly remembered the day my younger sister had seen me off from our village; standing by the dirt road, she had looked just as thin, just as small, and just as fragile as that girl. 

Since leaving home, I had been mercilessly cursed as a filthy country bumpkin. I had had my meager wages stolen by corrupt contractors. I had been repeatedly shoved aside by wealthy city people, suffocated by their invisible, suffocating contempt. I knew exactly what it felt like to be looked down upon.

And yet, just now, I had willingly become the exact kind of monster I hated the most.

I squeezed my eyes tightly shut, but it was useless. All I could see was the way she had looked at me from the front of the bus.
