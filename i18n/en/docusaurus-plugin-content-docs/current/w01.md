# The Unsolvable Problem

     *2002*

Xiao Fang was tall and slender. When she walked, her ponytail swayed gently, occasionally brushing against the collar of her white shirt. That shirt always emitted a faint scent of soap, as if it had just been dried in the sun. The moment she looked back was like a breeze blowing into the lifeless classroom, becoming a name that countless boys secretly wrote down in the corners of their textbooks and hastily crossed out.

When teenage boys and girls gather together, the topic can never avoid "who likes whom." We folks in the Northeast have straight tempers; if we have something on our minds, we don't hide it. But I rarely joined in these chats because my mind was entirely occupied by one thought—going to Shanghai.

After my freshman year of high school ended, my father got a rare opportunity to go on a business trip to Shanghai. Our family wasn't wealthy. After pondering repeatedly for days, he finally gritted his teeth and bought me a train ticket to take me to see the world. That trip completely overturned my perception of life. I gazed at the brightly lit buildings along the Huangpu River, never able to count them all; on Nanjing Road, people in suits and leather shoes hurried back and forth, and I could never catch up with time. The flashing neon lights, glass curtain walls, crowded crowds, and rivers of traffic all dazzled me, a boy from a poor, remote village.

After returning from Shanghai, everything in the town seemed narrow. The streets were the same, but my heart felt as if something was blocking it. I clearly knew that I had to leave here, go to Shanghai, and go to even further places. And the only single-plank bridge leading to the distance was the college entrance examination (Gaokao). I had to get into a top university in Shanghai; there was no way back and no room for compromise.

Students with good grades are always more popular at school. Although I wasn't ranked first in the class, I had the best popularity. Because no matter who came to ask a question, I would patiently explain it to them until they understood. Even if they just wanted to copy homework, I wouldn't expose them and would just hand over my notebook directly.

It was exactly during these repeated moments of helping classmates that I began to feel the gap between people. For me, solving problems was as natural as eating and drinking, but for others, it was an insurmountable hurdle. Xiao Fang was stuck right at that hurdle.

When seating was rearranged in our senior year, she was moved directly behind me. We, who hadn't talked much before, began to have interactions. Her existence suddenly became real and intimate: the faint fragrance when her hair brushed past, the light sound of her foot accidentally bumping the chair leg while writing, and the times she often held a test paper, blushing slightly, and lightly tapping my back: "I don't know how to do this problem again. Can you help me take a look?"

I would turn around and try to break down those complicated mathematical formulas in various ways, step by step, paving the stairs she could climb.

She was one of the most hardworking students in the class, just lacking mathematical talent. I remember one time she got an analytic geometry problem wrong. I explained it to her three times, and she still didn't understand. Her eyes turned red with anxiety, and she bit her lip and said, "Am I especially stupid?" I said, "No, it's because I haven't completely grasped this problem, so I can't explain it clearly. Let's try another approach." She wiped the corners of her eyes, took a deep breath: "Okay, you explain it again, I will definitely understand." I don't know how many times I explained it that day, but until the evening self-study ended, when she finally got the answer right, she almost jumped up, excited like an elementary school student who had been praised.

Once, I noticed her zoning out. The cap of her pen rested lightly against her lips. Sunlight slid down the strands of hair at her temple and gently sprinkled on her cheek. Her delicate skin, gently stroked by the slanting sun, took on a layer of shallow blush. Her long eyelashes were also plated in gold, casting a small fan-shaped shadow on her eyelids, trembling slightly with her breathing. In her eyes was a pool of autumn water, reflecting the rolling clouds in the horizon. How I wished I could stay forever in this tranquil afternoon, just looking at her like this, but eventually, I still had to gently remind her. She immediately came back to her senses, a blush flying across her cheeks, and lowered her head to continue solving problems.

Sometimes, she would secretly put a fruit candy on the corner of my desk. When I picked it up and put it in my mouth, she would lean on the desk pretending to read, with both ears turning bright red.

Some things quietly changed flavor in the midst of calculating numbers and deducing formulas.

The first to lose control was my desk mate, who was also once my best buddy. That day when only the two of us were left in the classroom, he suddenly stopped his pen and complained with extreme grievance: "I can't stand it anymore. You two both treat me as transparent, acting all lovey-dovey here every day. You know very well that I pursued her before. You have never considered my feelings!"

The next day, he asked the head teacher to change his seat and moved to the corner at the very back of the classroom. Out of sight, out of mind.

A single stone stirred up a thousand ripples, and rumors quickly spread. The head teacher's look toward her also changed: Xiao Fang was once favored because of her outstanding looks, but now she had become a bad example who didn't attend to her proper duties and would drag down excellent students. Not long after, the head teacher found a random excuse and moved her far away too.

The physical distance increased, but the psychological distance did not. As long as it was between classes or free time, she would still appear in front of my desk right on time. We always talked about math, physics, and chemistry, but to outsiders, we were always sticking together and had long been defaulted as a couple.

After some time, another buddy cornered me in the restroom. His eyes flashed with an agitated ruthlessness: "Did you think I really treated you as a friend? I just wanted to use the opportunity to get close to her... If you ever dare to bully Xiao Fang, I won't let you off..."

What was even more embarrassing was one sunny noon on the playground. I was pulled by several girls to play badminton. Just as we were playing vigorously, Xiao Fang suddenly rushed over huffing and puffing, and shouted at me in front of everyone: "You still haven't taught me those two problems, why did you come out to play!"

The whole field fell silent. The girls looked at each other, then found an excuse and dispersed in a hubbub.

During the afternoon study session, my new desk mate whispered with the girls in the back row for a long time, then they turned their heads together and asked me seriously: "Can we still talk to you in the future? We won't be 'hunted down' by Xiao Fang, right?"

I could only reply with a helpless, bitter smile: "There's nothing between us, I'm just helping her tutor." The two little girls immediately returned to their usual giggling and said: "That's great. What is going on with this problem? Tell us quickly."

The clearer her feelings became, the more cowardly I felt inside. After all, what I felt from every family around me was not the beauty of love, but the sadness of poor couples, getting red in the face arguing over a few dozen yuan under the dim light. I was afraid that I would also fall into such a situation. With my family's conditions, I simply didn't deserve her. If I couldn't walk out of this small town, all the sweetness in front of my eyes would turn into a mess of chicken feathers in the future.

I struggled for a long time, and finally, Shanghai won. I made up my mind not to let any fetters block my way of escape.

From that day on, I deliberately started avoiding her. When she came to ask questions again, I was still patient, but I would deliberately stay across the desk, keeping my eyes fixed only on the test paper, absolutely refusing to make eye contact with her. When the fruit candy secretly appeared on the corner of my desk again, I didn't put it in my mouth, but instead stuffed it into my schoolbag right in front of her.

I don't know when she sensed my alienation. She would still come to ask questions, but the frequency was obviously less. Sometimes, despite holding a test paper and walking up to my desk, she would hesitate and retreat. Or she would only whisper, "If it doesn't delay you..." when I walked past her desk.

Amid hesitation and torment, our senior year flashed by. I received the admission letter to the magic city (Shanghai), while Xiao Fang went to a local junior college. Based on her foundation, this was already the limit, and I had tried my best.

In that small town where transportation basically relied on walking and communication basically relied on shouting, parting seemed extraordinarily absolute. I thought about going to her house to say goodbye, but stopped halfway. Since I couldn't promise anything, what was the point of talking? Say "I'm leaving"? Or "I wish you happiness"?

Forget it. She didn't come looking for me, and I didn't disturb her again.

After graduating from college, I went abroad for further studies, walking further and further away, and gradually losing contact with the circles of the past. It wasn't until many years later, when the Internet became widespread, that I sporadically heard some news about her. She stayed in the town, got married, had children, and lived a stable life. What a perfect ending this is: I finally saw the vastness of the world, and she found her own destination in the small town. We both lived the lives we yearned for.

However, I still picked up the glass bottle on the corner of the desk, inside which was hidden a fruit candy that I couldn't bear to eat.
