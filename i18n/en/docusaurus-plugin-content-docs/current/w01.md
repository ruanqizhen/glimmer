# The Unsolvable Problem

*2002*

Xiao Fang was tall and slender. When she walked, her ponytail swayed gently, occasionally brushing against the collar of her white shirt. That shirt always carried the faint, crisp scent of soap, as if it had just been dried in the sun. The moment she turned her head was like a fresh breeze blowing into our otherwise lifeless classroom. Soon, hers became the name countless boys secretly scribbled in the corners of their textbooks, only to hastily cross it out.

When teenage boys and girls gather, the conversation inevitably drifts to "who likes whom." We folks in the Northeast have straightforward temperaments; if we have something on our minds, we don't hide it. But I rarely joined these chats. My mind was entirely consumed by a single thought—getting to Shanghai.

At the end of my freshman year of high school, my father got a rare opportunity to travel to Shanghai for business. Our family wasn't wealthy. After agonizing over it for days, he finally gritted his teeth and bought me a train ticket so I could see the world. That trip completely upended my understanding of life. I stared at the brightly lit skyscrapers along the Huangpu River, never able to count them all. On Nanjing Road, people in sleek suits and polished leather shoes hurried past, and I felt I could never catch up to their pace. The flashing neon lights, towering glass facades, dense crowds, and endless rivers of traffic absolutely dazzled me—a boy from a poor, remote village.

After returning from Shanghai, everything in my hometown felt suffocatingly narrow. The streets were exactly the same, but my heart felt as though something heavy was pressing down on it. I knew with absolute certainty that I had to leave. I had to get to Shanghai, and eventually, even further. The only bridge leading to that distant world was the Gaokao (the college entrance examination). I had to get into a top university in Shanghai; there was no turning back and no room for compromise.

Students with top grades are always popular in school. Although I wasn't ranked first in the class, I was easily the most well-liked. No matter who came to me with a question, I would patiently explain it until they understood. Even if they just wanted to copy my homework, I never exposed them; I would simply hand over my notebook.

It was during these countless moments of tutoring classmates that I first began to sense the inherent differences between people. For me, solving math problems was as natural as breathing, but for others, it was an insurmountable hurdle. Xiao Fang was stuck right at that hurdle.

When seating was rearranged in our senior year, she was moved directly behind me. We hadn't spoken much before, but now we began to interact daily. Her presence suddenly became real and intimate: the faint fragrance when her hair brushed past, the soft sound of her foot accidentally bumping my chair leg as she wrote, and the moments she would hold up a test paper, blushing slightly, and lightly tap my back. "I don't know how to do this problem again. Can you help me take a look?"

I would turn around and try breaking down those complicated mathematical formulas in every way I could think of, step by step, building a staircase she could climb.

She was one of the hardest-working students in our class, simply lacking a natural talent for math. I remember one time she got an analytic geometry problem wrong. I explained it to her three times, and she still didn't understand. Her eyes turned red with frustration. Biting her lip, she asked, "Am I just especially stupid?" 

"No," I replied. "It's just that I haven't completely grasped this problem myself, so I'm not explaining it clearly. Let's try another approach." 

She wiped the corners of her eyes and took a deep breath. "Okay, explain it again. I'll definitely understand this time." 

I don't know how many times I explained it that day, but when evening study hall ended and she finally got the answer right, she practically jumped out of her seat, as excited as a praised elementary schooler.

Once, I caught her zoning out. The cap of her pen rested lightly against her lips. Sunlight slid down the strands of hair at her temple and gently dusted her cheek. Her delicate skin, brushed by the slanting rays, took on a shallow, rosy hue. Her long eyelashes were tipped in gold, casting small, fan-shaped shadows on her eyelids that trembled slightly with her breathing. In her eyes was a pool of autumn water, reflecting the rolling clouds on the horizon. How I wished I could stay forever in that tranquil afternoon, just watching her like that. But eventually, I had to gently remind her to focus. She instantly snapped back to reality, a blush flying across her cheeks, and lowered her head to continue solving problems.

Sometimes, she would secretly leave a fruit candy on the corner of my desk. When I picked it up and popped it into my mouth, she would lean over her desk, pretending to read, both ears burning bright red.

Some things quietly changed flavor amidst the endless calculations and deduced formulas.

The first person to lose it was my desk mate, who had once been my closest buddy. One day, when only the two of us were left in the classroom, he suddenly slammed his pen down and complained with intense grievance, "I can't stand it anymore. You two treat me like I'm invisible, acting all lovey-dovey every single day. You know damn well I pursued her before. You never considered my feelings!"

The very next day, he asked our head teacher to move his seat, relocating to the furthest corner at the back of the classroom. Out of sight, out of mind.

A single stone stirred up a thousand ripples, and rumors quickly spread. The head teacher's attitude toward her shifted as well. Xiao Fang had once been favored for her outstanding looks, but now she was viewed as a bad example—someone neglecting her studies and dragging down top students. Not long after, the teacher found a random excuse and moved her far away, too.

The physical distance increased, but the psychological distance did not. Whether between classes or during free time, she would still appear at my desk right on time. We only ever discussed math, physics, and chemistry, but to outsiders, we were always glued together and had long been branded a couple.

After some time, another buddy cornered me in the restroom. His eyes flashed with an agitated ruthlessness. "Did you think I actually treated you as a friend? I just wanted to use you to get close to her... If you ever dare bully Xiao Fang, I won't let you off."

Even more embarrassing was a sunny afternoon out on the playground. Several girls had dragged me into a game of badminton. Just as we were playing vigorously, Xiao Fang suddenly rushed over, huffing and puffing, and shouted at me in front of everyone, "You still haven't taught me those two problems! Why did you come out to play?!"

The entire field fell dead silent. The girls exchanged glances, quickly made up excuses, and dispersed in a flurry of whispers.

During the afternoon study session, my new desk mate spent a long time whispering with the girls in the back row. Then, they turned to me and asked in all seriousness, "Can we still talk to you in the future? We won't get 'hunted down' by Xiao Fang, will we?"

I could only offer a helpless, bitter smile. "There's nothing between us. I'm just helping her tutor." The girls immediately returned to their usual giggling. "That's a relief. So, how do you do this problem? Tell us quickly."

The clearer her feelings became, the more cowardly I felt inside. Growing up, the examples of love I saw in the families around me weren't beautiful; they were the sad realities of poor couples, screaming until they were red in the face over a few dozen yuan under dim lighting. I was terrified of falling into that exact trap. Given my family's financial situation, I simply didn't deserve her. If I couldn't escape this small town, all the sweetness right in front of me would inevitably turn into a chaotic mess of petty struggles.

I wrestled with the thought for a long time, and finally, Shanghai won. I made up my mind not to let any emotional fetters block my only path of escape.

From that day on, I deliberately started avoiding her. When she came to ask questions, I was still patient, but I intentionally stayed across the desk, keeping my eyes glued to the test paper, absolutely refusing to make eye contact. When the secret fruit candy appeared on the corner of my desk again, I didn't eat it. Instead, I stuffed it into my schoolbag right in front of her.

I don't know exactly when she sensed my sudden coldness. She still came to ask questions, but far less frequently. Sometimes, despite walking all the way to my desk with a test paper in hand, she would hesitate and retreat. Or, she would only whisper, "If it won't delay you..." as I walked past her desk.

Amidst this hesitation and torment, our senior year flashed by. I received an admission letter from a university in the magic city of Shanghai, while Xiao Fang enrolled in a local junior college. Based on her academic foundation, that was the absolute best she could do, and I had truly tried my best to help her.

In that small town, where transportation basically relied on walking and communication relied on shouting, parting felt extraordinarily absolute. I thought about going to her house to say goodbye but stopped halfway. Since I couldn't promise her anything, what was the point? Should I just say "I'm leaving"? Or "I wish you happiness"?

Forget it. She didn't come looking for me, and I didn't disturb her again.

After graduating from college, I went abroad for further studies, walking further and further away, gradually losing all contact with the people from my past. It wasn't until years later, when the internet became widespread, that I sporadically heard news of her. She had stayed in our hometown, gotten married, had children, and lived a stable, peaceful life. 

What a perfect ending this was: I finally saw the vastness of the world, and she found her own harbor in that small town. We both ended up living the lives we had yearned for.

And yet, I still reached out and picked up the glass bottle sitting on the corner of my desk. Inside, hidden away, was a fruit candy I had never been able to bear to eat.
