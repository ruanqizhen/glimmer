# The Unsolvable Problem

*2002*

Xiao Fang was tall and slender. When she walked, her ponytail swayed, lightly brushing the collar of a white shirt that always smelled faintly of crisp soap and sunshine. Whenever she turned her head, it felt like a fresh breeze cutting through the stale, suffocating air of our classroom. Before long, hers was the name dozens of boys were secretly scribbling into the margins of their textbooks, only to hastily scratch it out again.

Get a bunch of teenagers together, and the conversation inevitably drifts to who has a crush on whom. We Northeasterners are straightforward people; if something is on our minds, we say it. But I rarely joined in on that kind of talk. My brain was completely consumed by a single, desperate thought: *getting to Shanghai.*

At the end of my freshman year of high school, my dad scored a rare business trip to Shanghai. We had no money to spare. But after agonizing over it for days, he finally bit the bullet and bought me a train ticket so I could see the world. That trip completely shattered my understanding of reality. I stood on the banks of the Huangpu River, staring up at brightly lit skyscrapers, unable to even count them all. On Nanjing Road, men in sleek suits and polished leather shoes blurred past me; I knew I could never match their pace. The neon lights, the towering glass facades, the crushing crowds, and the endless rivers of traffic—it all completely dazzled a kid from a dirt-poor, remote village like me.

When I got back, my hometown suddenly felt suffocatingly small. The streets hadn't changed, but a heavy weight had settled onto my chest. I knew with absolute, terrifying certainty that I had to get out. I had to make it back to Shanghai, and from there, go even further. The only bridge out of that town was the *Gaokao*—the national college entrance exam. I had to test into a top university in Shanghai. There was no plan B. There was no room for failure.

The smart kids are always popular in school. I wasn't ranked first in the class, but I was easily the most well-liked. No matter who came to me for help, I'd patiently walk them through the problem until it clicked. Even if they just wanted to blindly copy my homework, I never turned them in. I'd just hand over my notebook.

It was during these endless tutoring sessions that I first started noticing the inherent, unbridgeable gaps between people. For me, solving math problems was as natural as breathing. For others, it was an insurmountable brick wall. Xiao Fang was stuck right at the bottom of that wall.

When seating was rearranged in our senior year, she ended up right behind me. We hadn't spoken much before, but suddenly, she was an undeniable presence in my daily life. I noticed the faint scent of her shampoo when she leaned forward. I felt the soft bump of her shoe against my chair leg when she stretched. And I got used to the moments when she would hold up a test paper, blushing slightly, and lightly tap my back. "I don't know how to do this one either. Could you help me?"

I'd turn around and break those complicated formulas down in every way I could think of, desperately trying to build a staircase she could actually climb.

She was easily one of the hardest-working students in the room; she just didn't have a natural talent for numbers. I remember she completely botched an analytic geometry problem once. I explained it to her three times, and she still stared blankly at the page. Her eyes welled up with frustration. Biting her lip, she asked quietly, "Am I just stupid?" 

"No," I told her. "I just don't have a total grip on this problem myself, so I'm not explaining it right. Let's try looking at it backward." 

She wiped the corners of her eyes and took a deep, shaky breath. "Okay. Walk me through it again. I'll get it this time." 

I don't know how many times I broke it down that afternoon. But when evening study hall finally ended and she nailed the answer, she practically jumped out of her chair, beaming like a kid who just won a prize.

Once, I caught her zoning out. The cap of her pen rested lightly against her lip. Sunlight spilled down the strands of hair framing her face, dusting her cheek in a warm, rosy glow. Her long eyelashes were tipped in gold, casting tiny, trembling shadows across her eyelids as she breathed. Her eyes looked like pools of clear autumn water, reflecting the rolling clouds out the window. Part of me wished I could freeze time right there, trapped forever in that quiet afternoon, just watching her. But eventually, I had to tap her desk and tell her to focus. She snapped out of it, a furious blush painting her cheeks, and buried her head back into her workbook.

Sometimes, a single fruit candy would magically appear on the corner of my desk. I'd pop it into my mouth, and she'd lean heavily over her own desk, pretending to read, her ears burning bright red.

Somewhere amidst the endless calculations and scribbled formulas, things shifted.

My desk mate—a guy who used to be my closest friend—was the first one to snap. One afternoon, when we were the only two left in the room, he slammed his pen down and glared at me. "I can't take this anymore. You two act like I'm invisible, flirting all day long. You know damn well I asked her out. You never even considered how I felt!"

The very next morning, he asked the homeroom teacher to move his seat to the furthest corner at the back of the room. Out of sight, out of mind.

That single stone kicked up a tidal wave of rumors. The teacher's attitude toward Xiao Fang turned ice-cold. She used to be the golden girl because of her looks, but now she was painted as a distraction—a bad influence dragging down a top student. Not long after, the teacher found a flimsy excuse and moved her seat to the other side of the room.

The physical distance grew, but the psychological distance didn't. Whether between classes or during study hall, she'd still show up at my desk, right on time. We only ever talked about math, physics, and chemistry. But to everyone else, we were attached at the hip. The whole school had already branded us a couple.

A few weeks later, another buddy cornered me in the bathroom. He shoved me against the wall, his eyes flashing with a raw, agitated anger. "Did you think we were actually friends? I just hung around you to get close to her. If you ever hurt Xiao Fang, I swear to God, I'll ruin you."

The breaking point was a sunny afternoon out on the playground. A group of girls had dragged me into a casual game of badminton. We were laughing, volleying the birdie back and forth, when Xiao Fang suddenly stormed across the field. Panting heavily, she shouted at me in front of everyone, "You still haven't taught me those two problems! Why are you out here playing?!"

The entire field went dead silent. The girls exchanged wide-eyed looks, mumbled awkward excuses, and scattered.

During afternoon study hall, my new desk mate spent twenty minutes whispering with the girls in the back row. Finally, they leaned forward and asked me, dead serious, "Are we still allowed to talk to you? Xiao Fang isn't going to hunt us down, is she?"

I just gave them a tired, bitter smile. "There's nothing going on between us. I'm just tutoring her." The girls immediately giggled in relief. "Oh, thank God. Okay, so how do you solve this equation? Show us."

But the clearer her feelings became, the more terrified I got. Growing up, the marriages I saw in my neighborhood weren't beautiful. They were brutal, exhausting realities—poor couples screaming themselves red in the face over a few dozen yuan under a flickering, dim lightbulb. I was terrified of falling into that exact trap. Given my family's bank account, I simply didn't deserve her. If I couldn't claw my way out of this small town, all the sweetness standing right in front of me would rot into a messy, bitter struggle for survival.

I agonized over it for weeks. In the end, Shanghai won. I made a cold, hard choice: I would not let any emotional baggage anchor me to this town.

From that day on, I built a wall. When she came over with questions, I still helped, but I stayed firmly on my side of the desk. I glued my eyes to the paper. I refused to look at her. When a secret fruit candy appeared on the corner of my desk again, I didn't unwrap it. I picked it up and shoved it into my backpack, right in front of her.

I don't know exactly when the message sank in. She still came to ask questions, but the visits slowed to a crawl. Sometimes, I'd see her walk halfway to my desk with a test paper in hand, only to stop, hesitate, and turn back. Other times, she'd just whisper, "If you're not too busy..." as I walked past her chair.

Amidst that quiet, suffocating torment, our senior year vanished. I got an acceptance letter from a university in Shanghai. Xiao Fang ended up enrolling in a local junior college. Based on her grades, it was the absolute best she could do. I really had tried my best to help her.

In a town where nobody owned cars and people communicated by shouting across the street, parting ways felt devastatingly final. I thought about walking over to her house to say a proper goodbye, but I stopped myself halfway there. I couldn't promise her a damn thing, so what was the point? What was I supposed to say? "I'm leaving"? "Have a nice life"?

Forget it. She didn't come looking for me, and I didn't bother her again.

After college, I went abroad. I moved further and further away, quietly cutting ties with the ghosts of my past. It wasn't until years later, when social media blew up, that I caught fragments of news about her. She had stayed in our hometown. She got married, had kids, and settled into a quiet, peaceful life. 

It was the perfect ending. I finally got to see the vast, glittering world, and she found a safe harbor in the town she knew. We both got exactly what we wanted.

And yet, sitting alone in my office, I reached out and picked up a small glass jar on the corner of my desk. Sitting quietly at the bottom was a single, ancient fruit candy—the one I never had the courage to eat.
