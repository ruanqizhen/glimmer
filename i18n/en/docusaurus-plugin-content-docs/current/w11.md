# Half a Sweet Potato

*1994*

Linzi used to love telling people about our "sweet potato friendship." He'd vividly paint the whole picture, and right when he reached the emotional climax of the story, he'd slam his hand flat on the table and declare, "If it weren't for Da Zhuang, I would have died back then!"

It all happened during the brutal winter of 1960. The freezing wind drilled relentlessly through the cracks in our adobe walls, slicing across our faces like a razor. By then, the bark on every tree in the village had already been stripped bare, and even the deepest wild grass roots had been dug out of the frozen dirt and boiled. Later on, when people began truly starving, their stomachs actually stopped growling. Instead, their legs would swell up and turn shiny; if you pressed a finger into the flesh, it left a deep pit that wouldn't bounce back for a long time.

Linzi was the most seriously ill among us. Lying on the heated brick bed (*kang*), he burned with a high fever and muttered deliriously, his lips cracked and peeling like dry riverbeds. His mother had passed away long ago, and his father hadn't been able to hold on either. That left only me to keep him company. 

Late that night, groggy and fading, he weakly grabbed the corner of my clothes and begged for water. But there was only half a gourd scoop of muddy, melted snow left in the vat.

Desperate, I ran to the back mountain. That stretch of land had already been turned over countless times; it looked like a torn, ragged mat. I didn't know why I thought I should turn it over yet again. I suppose when a person reaches an absolute dead end, they always want to place one final gamble. As the sky grew dark, my freezing fingers brushed against a hard lump buried beneath a rock-solid clod of dirt.

It was a sweet potato.

It was only about the size of a fist, shriveled and wrinkled like an old man's heel. But at that exact moment, it meant life.

I tucked it tightly against my chest, trying to thaw it with my own body heat. I didn't dare light a fire, terrified that the smell of smoke would attract desperate neighbors. Slipping back into the dark room, I smashed it open with a rock. I took the larger half and shoved it directly into Linzi's mouth. 

"Chew," I ordered him. "The King of Hell dozed off and dropped this. If we eat it, we both get to live."

He opened his deeply sunken eyes, froze in disbelief for a second, and then wolfed it down, desperately swallowing it all—peel, frozen mud, and flesh. Tears streamed down his hollow cheeks, and he mumbled indistinctly, "Da Zhuang... you gave me this life. I swear, from now on, if I have dry food to eat, I will absolutely never let you drink thin gruel."

At that time, I truly felt as though I had torn off half my own life and handed it to him.

Later, life slowly began to improve. We went to work in the fields together, we got married around the same time, and we raised our children together. Whenever there was a crisis or celebration in one family, the other was guaranteed to be there. It was the kind of friendship that required no words—like an old root buried deep in the soil, usually unseen, but always firmly holding on.

In the late 1980s, the world turned upside down. Linzi had an active mind and real courage, and he became the very first person in our village to plunge into the terrifying sea of private business. He traveled south to Guangdong to deal in wholesale clothing, and later transitioned into manufacturing leather goods. Letters arrived one by one, bragging that business was booming. As time passed, the letters became less frequent. Then, the rumors reached us: he had struck it rich. People said he was driving a Mercedes-Benz and carrying one of those massive, brick-like mobile phones (*Da Ge Da*). Some claimed he owned an entire company in Guangzhou; others swore he had built massive factory compounds in Shenzhen.

That summer, I realized I hadn't seen him in far too long. I debated it for days, but eventually, I bought a ticket on the slow, green-skinned train all the way to Guangzhou. It was stiflingly hot in the crowded train car, the air thick with the smell of cheap instant noodles mixed with stale sweat. I clung tightly to a canvas bag containing a few pounds of homegrown, sun-dried peanuts—I couldn't exactly show up empty-handed.

I finally met him in a towering office building. The marble floors were polished so brightly you could see your own reflection, and the air conditioning was blasting so cold it made me sneeze repeatedly. A young secretary brought me a glass of brown liquor, telling me it was called "whiskey." I couldn't taste anything special about it; it just burned my throat.

Linzi was wearing a sharp, expensive suit, his tie knotted meticulously. He didn't have much hair left, but what remained was slicked back perfectly. He clapped me on the shoulder and smiled broadly. "My friend! How many years has it been?" 

As he spoke, his mobile phone rang loudly. He glanced at it and hung up. It rang again a minute later, and he hung up again.

We talked about the past and caught up on the folks back in the village. He laughed loudly and often, but he sounded as though he were recounting a distant, fictional story. He even brought up that old sweet potato, waving his hand dismissively as he spoke. "Man, it was really bitter back then."

Before I left, he slid a thick kraft paper envelope across his massive desk.

"Da Zhuang, here's two thousand yuan. Go back and fix up your house."

I can't fully describe the bitter taste that rose in the back of my throat. I suddenly thought about that half a sweet potato. What I gave him that night wasn't currency. It was life. It was two terrified, starving teenagers huddling together in the freezing wind. We had absolutely nothing left in the world except each other. 

Now, he had everything in the world, and I had simply become an old obligation—one he could conveniently fulfill with two thousand yuan.

I slowly placed the envelope back on the polished table.

"Linzi, I didn't come all this way to ask for money."

He froze for a second, his face immediately darkening. "Da Zhuang, what is that supposed to mean? You think it's too little? Look, I don't treat you like an outsider. Giving you this much is already very generous of me."

His voice had risen sharply, and the secretary poked her head through the doorway to see what was wrong. I suddenly felt freezing cold. It wasn't the chill of the air conditioning; it was an indescribable, bone-deep chill.

"That half a sweet potato," I said slowly, looking him in the eye. "I never calculated its price."

The air in the luxurious office suddenly went dead quiet. He opened his mouth to reply, but ultimately, no words came out.

I turned around and walked out of the building. As the heavy glass doors slid shut behind me, it felt exactly like a wall dropping down to separate two entirely different worlds.
