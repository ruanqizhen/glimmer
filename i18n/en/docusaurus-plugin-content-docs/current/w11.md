# Half a Sweet Potato

  *1994*

Linzi often told people about our "sweet potato friendship." He described it vividly, and when he reached an emotional point, he would even slap the table: "If it weren't for Da Zhuang, I would have been gone back then!"

That was the winter of 1960. The wind drilled in through the cracks of the adobe house, cutting faces like a knife. The bark on the trees in the village had long been stripped bare, and even wild grass roots had been dug up thoroughly. Later on, when people starved, their stomachs stopped growling, but their legs swelled up and turned shiny; pressing a finger on them left a pit that wouldn't bounce back for a long time.

Linzi was the most seriously ill. Lying on the heated brick bed (kang), he had a high fever and spoke deliriously, his lips cracked like dry fields. His mother was long gone, and his father didn't hold on and left too, leaving only me to keep him company. That night, groggy, he grabbed the corner of my clothes and softly asked for water. But there was only half a gourd scoop of muddy snow water in the vat.

I ran to the back mountain. That piece of land had already been turned over countless times, looking like a torn mat. I didn't know why I was still going to turn it over; probably when a person reaches a dead end, they always want to gamble one more time. As it was getting dark, under a frozen, hard clod of dirt, I touched a hard lump.

It was a sweet potato.

As big as a fist, shriveled, and wrinkled like an old man's heel. But at that moment, it was life.

I tucked it in my bosom, warming it with my body heat. I didn't dare light a fire, afraid the smell of smoke would attract people. Returning to the room, I smashed it open with a stone. I stuffed the larger half into Linzi's mouth: "Chew. The King of Hell dozed off and dropped this; if we eat it, we both can live."

He opened his deeply sunken eyes, froze for a moment, and then wolfed it down, swallowing it peel, mud, and all. Tears streamed down his cheeks, and he mumbled indistinctly: "Da Zhuang... you gave me this life. From now on, if I have dry food, I will absolutely not let you drink thin gruel."

At that time, I truly felt that I had given half my life to him.

Later, life slowly got better. We went to work in the fields together, married and had children together. Whenever there was something going on in one's family, the other would definitely be there. That kind of friendship needed no words, like an old root buried in the soil; usually unseen, but always there.

In the late 1980s, the world changed. Linzi had an active mind and courage, becoming the first person in the village to plunge into the sea of business. He went south to Guangdong to deal in clothes, and later made leather goods. Letters came back one by one, saying business was good. Even later, the letters became fewer. Then I heard he had struck it rich, riding in a Mercedes-Benz and using a brick-like mobile phone (Da Ge Da). Some said he had a company in Guangzhou, others said he had factory buildings in Shenzhen.

That summer, I hadn't seen him for too long. I thought about it for a long time, and still got on the slow green-skinned train to Guangzhou. It was stiflingly hot in the train car, with the smell of instant noodles mixed with sweat. I held a canvas bag containing a few catties of homegrown sun-dried peanuts—it wouldn't be good to show up empty-handed.

I met him in an office building. The marble floor was bright enough to reflect a person's image, and the air conditioning was so cold it made me sneeze constantly. A secretary brought a glass of brown liquor, saying it was whiskey. I couldn't taste anything special, just felt it was spicy.

Linzi wore a suit, his tie tied meticulously. He didn't have much hair left, but it was combed neatly. He patted my shoulder and said: "Old brother, how many years has it been." While speaking, his phone rang twice, and he hung up both times.

We talked about the past, talked about the people in the village. He laughed very loudly, as if recalling a distant story. He also mentioned that sweet potato, speaking of it lightly: "It was really bitter back then."

Before I left, he handed me a kraft paper envelope.

"Da Zhuang, here's two thousand. Go back and repair the house."

But I couldn't describe the flavor in my heart. I suddenly thought of that half a sweet potato. That wasn't money; it was life, it was two teenagers huddling together for warmth in the wind. We had nothing back then, only each other. Now, he had everything, but I had become an obligation he fulfilled with two thousand yuan.

I put the envelope back on the table.

"Linzi, I didn't come to ask for money."

He froze for a moment, his face darkening: "Da Zhuang, what do you mean? Think it's too little? I don't treat you as an outsider; this is already very generous of me."

His voice rose a bit, and the secretary poked her head in from outside the door to look. I suddenly felt cold. Not the cold of the air conditioning, but a kind of indescribable chill.

"That half sweet potato," I said slowly, "I never calculated its price."

The air suddenly went quiet. He opened his mouth but ultimately didn't speak.

I turned and walked out of that building. The glass doors closed behind me, as if separating two worlds.
