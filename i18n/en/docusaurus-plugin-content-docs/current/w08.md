# The Blue Stamp

*1996*

When I graduated from middle school, the country was right in the middle of deepening its economic reforms and opening up. The rural contract system had stabilized, countryside markets were buzzing with activity once again, and privately owned businesses were becoming an increasingly common sight. Peasant families like ours no longer had to rely entirely on farming just to scrape together a living.

It only felt natural that I took over my father's butchery trade. Buying and slaughtering pigs was considered a solid, respectable business in our township. During those years, the local government heavily promoted the slogan "getting rich through hard work," and they even awarded me the official title of "Model of Getting Rich." A photo of me was even published in the small township newspaper—a moment of real glory for our family. But doing business successfully is all about reputation. I never forced prices down when buying pigs from the farmers, nor did I ever shortchange my customers on the weight of their meat, let alone resort to dirty tricks like injecting water to inflate the weight. The local folks trusted me implicitly.

Who could have possibly predicted that I would eventually lose everything over a simple ink stamp?

It was exactly a week before the National Day holiday. Pork always sold incredibly well before a major festival, so I woke up well before dawn and slaughtered a few exceptionally fat pigs. According to the strict regulations of the time, all live pigs had to pass an official quarantine inspection before their meat could hit the market. The town's epidemic prevention station would dispatch an inspector to the site, and any pig carcasses that passed inspection had to be stamped with a blue quarantine seal before they were legally allowed to be sold.

The inspector dispatched that morning was Officer Li from the epidemic prevention station. He cast a few cursory glances over the meat, symbolically prodded the internal organs, and then pulled out his official stamp, pressing it against the pig carcasses a few times. The blue ink pad he used was quite dry, and the resulting imprint was faint and blurry—you could barely make out the outer border.

I bent over, offering him an apologetic smile. "Officer Li, could you please stamp it just a bit more clearly? The mark is too faint. It'll be really hard to explain to the market administrators if they check."

With a cigarette dangling loosely from his lips, he waved his hand impatiently. "Passed is passed. I'm standing right here in person, how could it be fake? Stop dawdling, I'm in a hurry."

I figured to myself, *He's a public official, and he's already collected the official quarantine fee. Nothing should go wrong.* So, I didn't insist any further. I loaded the freshly stamped meat onto my agricultural tricycle and hurried off to the morning market.

Market inspections were noticeably stricter then than they had been in previous years; private slaughtering and unauthorized selling were met with heavy fines. As soon as my stall was set up, business was booming. My regular customers chatted happily while I weighed out their meat, and everything felt exactly like a normal, profitable day.

But something went horribly wrong that very afternoon.

After Old Zhang's son went home and ate some of the meat they bought from me, he began vomiting violently and suffering from severe diarrhea. The family panicked and immediately reported the incident to the local police station. Before long, law enforcement officers descended upon the market to conduct a rigorous inspection. They instantly zeroed in on my pork with the blurry, nearly unreadable stamp. Because the blue seal was unclear, they determined on the spot that my quarantine procedures were highly questionable, accusing me of selling privately and illegally slaughtered meat.

Panicking, I hurried to find Officer Li so he could testify on my behalf. Instead, he completely turned hostile. "The stamps we use at our station are always perfectly clear! Where is the passing stamp on this meat? I never quarantined this pig!"

His denial was crisp, absolute, and utterly shameless. I stood there completely stunned, unable to force a single word of rebuttal from my throat.

My agricultural tricycle was confiscated on the spot, and all my meat was sealed away as evidence. Ultimately, I was officially charged with "selling meat products without valid quarantine," and handed a devastating fine of five thousand yuan.

A whole pig only sold for seven or eight hundred yuan at the time. A five-thousand-yuan fine felt like the sky itself collapsing on my head. 

Much later, I heard through the grapevine that Old Zhang's child simply had a bout of acute gastroenteritis, which very likely had absolutely nothing to do with my meat in the first place.
