# The Blue Stamp

*1996*

By the time I graduated middle school, the country was diving headfirst into economic reforms. The rural contracting system had settled in, the country markets were booming again, and private businesses were popping up everywhere. Peasant families like ours didn't have to rely strictly on pulling crops out of the dirt just to survive anymore.

It felt completely natural for me to take over my father's butchery business. Buying and slaughtering pigs was considered solid, respectable work in our township. During those years, the local government was pushing the slogan "getting rich through hard work," and they even gave me the official title of "Model of Getting Rich." They printed my picture in the local township newspaper—a huge point of pride for my family. But doing business successfully is all about your reputation. I never lowballed the farmers when buying their pigs, I never shortchanged my customers on the scale, and I absolutely never resorted to dirty tricks like injecting water into the meat to inflate the weight. The locals trusted me implicitly.

Who could have possibly predicted that a single ink stamp would wipe me out?

It was exactly a week before the National Day holiday. Pork always sold like crazy right before a festival, so I woke up well before dawn and slaughtered a few exceptionally fat pigs. Under the strict regulations back then, all live pigs had to pass an official quarantine inspection before their meat could hit the market. The town's epidemic prevention station would send an inspector out to the site. Any pig carcasses that passed inspection had to be stamped with a blue quarantine seal before they were legally allowed to be sold.

The inspector that morning was Officer Li. He cast a few lazy glances over the meat, symbolically poked at the internal organs, and then pulled out his official stamp, pressing it against the pig carcasses a few times. The blue ink pad he used was completely dried out, and the stamp he left was faint and blurry. You could barely even make out the outer border.

I bent over and gave him an apologetic smile. "Officer Li, could you please hit that one more time? It's really faint. If the market admins check, it's going to be really hard to explain."

With a cigarette dangling loosely from his lips, he waved me off impatiently. "Passed is passed. I'm standing right here in person, how could it be fake? Stop nagging me, I'm in a hurry."

I figured he was a government official, and he had already collected the official quarantine fee, so nothing should go wrong. I didn't push it. I loaded the freshly stamped meat onto my agricultural tricycle and rushed off to the morning market.

Market inspections were noticeably stricter that year; illegal slaughtering and selling uninspected meat carried heavy fines. But as soon as I set up my stall, business was booming. My regular customers chatted happily with me while I weighed out their cuts, and it felt exactly like a normal, highly profitable day.

Then, that afternoon, everything fell apart.

Zhang's son ate some of the pork they bought from my stall. An hour later, the kid was vomiting violently and suffering from severe diarrhea. The family panicked and immediately called the cops. Before long, law enforcement officers descended on the market to run a full inspection. They instantly zeroed in on my pork with the blurry, nearly unreadable stamp. Because the blue seal was impossible to read, they declared on the spot that my quarantine paperwork was fake. They accused me of selling privately and illegally slaughtered meat.

Panicking, I sprinted across town to find Officer Li so he could clear my name. Instead, he completely turned on me. "The stamps we use at our station are always perfectly clear! Where is the passing stamp on this meat? I never quarantined this pig!"

His denial was crisp, absolute, and completely shameless. I stood there stunned, unable to force a single word out of my throat.

They confiscated my tricycle on the spot and sealed away all my meat as evidence. Ultimately, I was officially charged with "selling meat products without valid quarantine," and handed a devastating fine of five thousand yuan.

A whole pig only sold for seven or eight hundred yuan back then. A five-thousand-yuan fine felt like the sky itself collapsing on my head. 

A long time later, I heard through the grapevine that Zhang's kid just had a bad bout of stomach flu. It probably had absolutely nothing to do with my meat.
