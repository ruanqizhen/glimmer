# The Experience of Being Detained in Shenzhen

   *1997*

The air in Shenzhen in May was like a pot of boiling paste, so thick it was almost suffocating, and even the wind was hot. That day, my girlfriend Ah Lan came back from a business trip out of town, and I promised to pick her up at Luohu Railway Station at exactly three o'clock. My workplace was only a little over a ten-minute walk from the station. I had already calculated the time, writing my work report while occasionally glancing at the wall clock. As soon as it hit two-thirty, I stood up with a "whoosh" and rushed out, my mind full of how Ah Lan would look walking out of the station dragging her luggage. In my panic, I left my ID card and temporary residence permit in my desk drawer.

Just as I ran to the intersection of the Dragon Hotel, several joint defense patrolmen (Lianfang Duiyuan) suddenly blocked my way from the street corner. Their uniforms were washed white and wrinkled, with black rubber batons tucked in their waists. Their eyes were as alert as hunters staring at prey. "Stop! ID check!"

My heart skipped a beat, and I quickly put on an apologetic smile and said, "Brothers, I work at the unit just ahead. I came out to pick up my girlfriend and left my IDs on my desk. There's a public phone over there; can I make a call and have a colleague bring them over?"

The leader, a burly man with a heavy local accent, didn't listen at all. He raised his hand, pointed at the ground, and shouted, "Cut the crap! Squat down!"

I squatted down instinctively, only to realize that there was already a row of people squatting by the roadside: migrant workers covered in cement, street vendors carrying large sacks, and two people wearing shirts and dress pants who looked like office workers. Everyone kept their heads down, not daring to breathe heavily, as sweat dripped onto the scalding concrete ground.

Before I could speak again, a dilapidated gray minivan roared over, and the door was pulled open with a clatter. We were pushed and shoved inside. The cabin was as stiflingly hot as a sauna, mixed with the smells of foot odor, sour sweat, and diesel. My decent shirt and stable job all became a joke at this moment.

That night, we were taken to a detention center in Luohu District. The guards first ordered us to take off our leather shoes and pull out our belts and shoelaces. All personal belongings were confiscated. An old hand sitting next to me, who was on his "second trip to the palace," whispered, "If you have money, hide it quickly. They will strip-search us in a moment. Money cannot be confiscated; it will be useful later."

I hurriedly touched my pockets; there were only two ten-yuan bills. While no one was looking, I stuffed them into my underwear. Fortunately, during the strip-search, the guards only checked outer garments and trouser pockets.

Next was fingerprinting. A burly bald man brought a can of dark stuff, like shoe polish, smeared it all over our palms, and roared, "Rub hard! Rub until they get hot!" After pressing down ten fingerprints, accompanied by the blinding flash of a camera, I felt that I had completely become a "blind drifter" (mangliu) in the files.

I was kicked into "Cell No. 6." The concrete floor was damp and cold. In the corner was an uncovered toilet pit, and the stench was so overpowering that standing in there made you want to vomit. At night, with my back against the floor, it felt like lying on ice cubes. I could only sit against the wall and doze off. In the early hours of the morning, the giggling of young women suddenly came from outside the cell. Through the small window on the iron door, I saw that a few fashionably dressed "ladies" who had been brought in with us yesterday were being led away. Before leaving, they were still flirting with the guards.

The next day when food was served, the rice was in oil-stained baskets. The white rice was as hard as sand, and the dish was watery salted radish. Several people in the cell had pink eye; their eyes were red and swollen. They could only use their own urine to wash their eyes for relief—that was the most common folk remedy at the time.

I found out that paying 600 yuan would get a person released. But I only had 20 yuan on me, which was far from enough. They wanted money but wouldn't let me make a phone call. In despair, I heard that a group of people who had paid the money would be released in the afternoon. I desperately grabbed a young man from Henan who was about to go out, and stuffed a piece of paper with my workplace phone number and 10 yuan into his hand: "Brother, go out and make a phone call for me. Tell my girlfriend to come and redeem me. I will repay you for the rest of my life!"

In the evening, a guard came in and coldly announced, "The rest of you, prepare to be transferred to the Zhangmutou detention center."

I completely panicked. If I left Shenzhen, how could Ah Lan find me? On the escort vehicle, more than fifty people were paired up and handcuffed together with cold handcuffs. The siren blared all the way, and the tall buildings outside the window gradually turned into pitch-black mountain roads. I could only pray in my heart over and over again that the brother from Henan had already made the phone call for me.

I endured another two days and two nights in Zhangmutou. The concrete cell was even more crowded, mosquitoes were like bombers, the food was worse, and the guards would beat people with rubber batons at the slightest provocation. Just when I was about to become numb, on the afternoon of the third day, my name was suddenly called outside the iron door.

I was so excited that my legs went weak and I almost couldn't stand up. Two cellmates pulled me and said eagerly, "After you get out, don't forget to make a phone call for us." I nodded desperately.

Walking out of the last iron door, the blinding sunlight made me unable to open my eyes. When my vision adjusted, I saw Ah Lan standing outside the door. She had lost a circle of weight, her eye sockets were dark, and her hair was a mess.

Later she told me that during the few days I was missing, she almost went crazy. She went to my workplace first, but no one knew I had been arrested; they all thought I was on vacation. She called the police, ran to all the hospitals in the city, and even went to the morgue for unclaimed bodies, lifting the white sheets one by one to check. It wasn't until the evening of the third day that a colleague from my workplace passed on a message to her, saying they received a strange call with a heavy Henan accent confirming I was in a detention center. Only then did she follow the clue, bring the money, and rush to Dongguan.

At that time, I was barefoot, my eyes were red and swollen, and my clothes were torn and covered in black grime and sweat stains. Ah Lan's face was originally covered with tear streaks from crying, but when she saw me, she suddenly smiled.
