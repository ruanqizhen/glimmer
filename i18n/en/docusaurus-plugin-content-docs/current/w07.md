# The Experience of Being Detained in Shenzhen

*1997*

The air in Shenzhen that May was like a pot of boiling paste—so thick it was almost suffocating, with even the wind blowing hot against the skin. That day, my girlfriend, Ah Lan, was returning from a business trip out of town, and I had promised to pick her up at the Luohu Railway Station at exactly three o'clock. My office was only a ten-minute walk from the station. I had carefully calculated the time, typing out my work report while occasionally throwing glances at the wall clock. 

The moment it hit two-thirty, I stood up with a *whoosh* and sprinted out the door, my mind already filled with the image of Ah Lan walking out of the station, dragging her luggage. In my panic and excitement, I completely forgot my ID card and temporary residence permit, leaving them locked inside my desk drawer.

Just as I ran up to the intersection by the Dragon Hotel, several joint defense patrolmen (*Lianfang Duiyuan*) suddenly stepped out from the street corner and blocked my path. Their uniforms were washed white and wrinkled, with heavy black rubber batons tucked aggressively into their belts. Their eyes were as sharp and alert as hunters stalking prey. 

"Stop! ID check!"

My heart skipped a beat. I quickly pasted on an apologetic smile and said, "Officers, I work right at the office just ahead. I just ran out to pick up my girlfriend and accidentally left my IDs on my desk. There's a public phone right over there; can I make a quick call and have a colleague run them over?"

The leader, a burly man with a thick local accent, wasn't having any of it. He raised his hand, pointed sharply at the ground, and barked, "Cut the crap! Squat down!"

I squatted down instinctively, only then realizing there was already an entire row of people squatting miserably by the side of the road. There were migrant workers covered in cement dust, street vendors clutching large woven sacks, and even two people wearing crisp shirts and dress pants who looked exactly like office workers. Everyone kept their heads bowed low, not daring to breathe too loudly as their sweat dripped onto the scalding concrete.

Before I could try to explain again, a dilapidated gray minivan roared up, and its side door was yanked open with a loud clatter. We were forcefully pushed and shoved inside. The cabin was as stiflingly hot as a sauna, thick with the nauseating stench of foot odor, sour sweat, and diesel fumes. My decent dress shirt and my stable job were suddenly reduced to a complete joke.

That night, we were hauled into a detention center in the Luohu District. The guards immediately ordered us to take off our leather shoes and pull out our belts and shoelaces. All personal belongings were summarily confiscated. An old hand sitting next to me, who was clearly on his "second trip to the palace," leaned in and whispered, "If you have cash, hide it right now. They're going to strip-search us in a minute. Money isn't supposed to be confiscated, and it will save your life later."

I frantically patted my pockets; I only had two ten-yuan bills on me. While the guards were distracted, I hastily shoved the money down into my underwear. Fortunately, during the strip-search, the guards only checked our outer garments and trouser pockets.

Next came fingerprinting. A massive, bald guard brought over a tin of dark ink that looked exactly like shoe polish, smeared it thick across our palms, and roared, "Rub hard! Rub until they get hot!" After pressing down all ten fingerprints, accompanied by the blinding flash of a mugshot camera, I realized I had officially become a "blind drifter" (*mangliu*) in the state archives.

I was kicked into "Cell No. 6." The concrete floor was bone-chillingly damp and cold. In the corner sat an uncovered toilet pit, emitting a stench so overpowering it made you want to vomit just standing there. At night, resting my back against the floor felt like lying on a slab of ice, so I was forced to sit propped against the wall, drifting in and out of a fitful doze. In the early hours of the morning, the sudden sound of young women giggling echoed from the corridor. Peering through the small barred window on the heavy iron door, I saw a few fashionably dressed "ladies" who had been brought in with us the day before being led away. Even as they left, they were still casually flirting with the guards.

The next day, when the food arrived, the rice was served in filthy, oil-stained baskets. The white rice was as hard as gravel, and the "dish" was nothing but watery, salted radish. Several men in the cell had pink eye; their eyes were swollen and bright red. With no medical care, they were forced to use their own urine to wash their eyes for relief—it was the most common folk remedy available in there.

I soon learned that paying a 600-yuan fine would get a person released immediately. But I only had 20 yuan hidden on me, nowhere near enough. They wanted money, but they absolutely refused to let anyone make a phone call to get it. In sheer despair, I heard that a group of detainees who had paid up were being released that afternoon. Desperate, I grabbed a young man from Henan who was preparing to leave and shoved a scrap of paper with my workplace phone number, along with my crumpled 10-yuan bill, into his hand. "Friend, please, go out and make a phone call for me! Tell my girlfriend to come redeem me. I will owe you my life!"

That evening, a guard strode in and coldly announced, "The rest of you, pack up. Prepare to be transferred to the Zhangmutou detention center."

Pure panic washed over me. If I was taken out of Shenzhen, how would Ah Lan ever find me? On the escort vehicle, more than fifty of us were paired up and chained together with freezing steel handcuffs. The siren wailed relentlessly the entire way, and the glittering tall buildings outside the window gradually gave way to pitch-black, winding mountain roads. I could only pray, over and over, that the guy from Henan had made that phone call.

I endured another two days and two nights in Zhangmutou. The concrete cell was even more tightly packed, the mosquitoes swarmed like tiny bombers, the food was significantly worse, and the guards wouldn't hesitate to beat people with their rubber batons at the slightest provocation. Just as my mind was beginning to go completely numb, on the afternoon of the third day, my name was suddenly barked out from the other side of the iron door.

I was so overwhelmed with relief that my legs gave out, and I nearly collapsed. Two of my cellmates hauled me up, their eyes burning with desperation. "When you get out," one pleaded, "don't forget to make a phone call for us." I nodded frantically.

Stepping out from behind the final iron gate, the blazing sunlight was so blinding I had to squeeze my eyes shut. When my vision finally adjusted, I saw Ah Lan standing there. She had lost a shocking amount of weight in just a few days. The circles under her eyes were bruised and dark, and her hair was a chaotic mess.

She later told me that during the days I was missing, she had nearly gone insane. She went to my workplace first, but no one there knew I had been arrested; they all assumed I had taken sudden leave. She filed a police report, sprinted to every single hospital in the city, and even went to the municipal morgue to check the unclaimed bodies, lifting the white sheets one by one with trembling hands. It wasn't until the evening of the third day that a colleague from my office passed a message to her—they had received a strange call from a man with a heavy Henan accent, confirming I was locked up in a detention center. Only then was she able to track down the location, gather the cash, and rush to Dongguan.

Standing there in the sunlight, I was barefoot, my eyes were violently red and swollen, and my clothes were torn to shreds, caked in black grime and dried sweat. Ah Lan's face was stained with the tracks of endless tears, but the moment she saw me, she suddenly broke into a beautiful smile.
