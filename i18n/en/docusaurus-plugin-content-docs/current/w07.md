# The Experience of Being Detained in Shenzhen

*1997*

Shenzhen in May was like stepping into a boiling pot of glue. The air was so thick you could barely breathe, and even the breeze felt like a hairdryer against your skin. That afternoon, my girlfriend, Ah Lan, was coming back from a business trip. I promised to meet her at the Luohu Railway Station at exactly three o'clock. My office was only a ten-minute walk from the station. I had carefully calculated the time, typing out my work report while throwing nervous glances at the wall clock. 

The second it hit two-thirty, I jumped out of my chair and sprinted out the door, my head already full of the image of Ah Lan walking out of the station dragging her suitcase. In my rush, I completely forgot my ID card and temporary residence permit. They were still locked inside my desk drawer.

Just as I ran up to the intersection by the Dragon Hotel, several joint defense patrolmen (*Lianfang Duiyuan*) stepped out from an alleyway and blocked my path. Their uniforms were washed white and wrinkled, and heavy black rubber batons were tucked aggressively into their belts. Their eyes were sharp, scanning the crowd like hunters stalking prey. 

"Stop! ID check!"

My heart skipped a beat. I quickly pasted on a polite, apologetic smile. "Officers, I work right at the office just down the street. I just ran out to pick up my girlfriend and accidentally left my IDs on my desk. There's a payphone right over there—can I just make a quick call and have a colleague run them over?"

The leader, a burly guy with a thick local accent, wasn't having any of it. He raised his hand, pointed sharply at the concrete, and barked, "Cut the crap! Squat down!"

I squatted down instinctively. Only then did I realize there was already an entire row of people squatting miserably by the side of the road. There were migrant workers covered in cement dust, street vendors clutching cheap woven sacks, and even two guys wearing crisp shirts and dress pants who looked exactly like me. Everyone kept their heads bowed low, not daring to breathe too loudly as sweat dripped off their noses onto the scalding concrete.

Before I could try to explain myself again, a beat-up gray minivan roared up to the curb. The side door was yanked open with a loud clatter, and we were violently shoved inside. The cabin was as stifling as a sauna, thick with the nauseating stench of foot odor, sour sweat, and cheap diesel. My nice dress shirt and my stable desk job were suddenly reduced to a total joke.

That night, they hauled us into a detention center in the Luohu District. The guards ordered us to kick off our leather shoes and rip out our belts and shoelaces. They confiscated absolutely everything. An older guy sitting next to me, who had clearly been through the system before, leaned in and whispered, "If you have cash, hide it right now. They're going to strip-search us in a minute. Money isn't supposed to be confiscated, and it's the only thing that's going to save your life later."

I frantically patted down my pockets. I only had two crumpled ten-yuan bills. When the guards looked away for a split second, I shoved the cash down my underwear. Thank God, during the strip-search, they only patted down our outer clothes and pants.

Next came fingerprinting. A massive, bald guard brought over a tin of dark ink that looked exactly like shoe polish, smeared it thick across our palms, and roared, "Rub hard! Rub until they get hot!" After pressing down all ten fingers, accompanied by the blinding flash of a mugshot camera, I realized I had officially become a "blind drifter" (*mangliu*) in the state archives.

They kicked me into "Cell No. 6." The concrete floor was bone-chillingly cold and slick with dampness. In the corner sat an open toilet pit, putting out a stench so overpowering it made you want to vomit just standing near it. At night, resting my back against the floor felt like lying on a slab of ice, so I was forced to sit propped against the wall, drifting in and out of a miserable, fitful sleep. In the early hours of the morning, I heard young women giggling out in the corridor. Peering through the small barred window on the heavy iron door, I saw a few fashionably dressed "ladies" who had been hauled in with us being led away. Even as they walked out, they were still casually flirting with the guards.

The next day, when the food finally arrived, the rice was dumped into filthy, oil-stained plastic baskets. The white rice was as hard as gravel, and the "dish" was just watery, salted radish. Several men in the cell had pink eye; their eyes were swollen shut and bright red. With no doctors around, they were forced to use their own urine to wash their eyes for relief—it was the only medicine anyone in there had.

I quickly learned the rules: a 600-yuan fine would buy your immediate release. But I only had 20 yuan hidden in my underwear. It wasn't even close. They wanted money, but they absolutely refused to let anyone make a phone call to get it. In sheer despair, I heard that a group of detainees who had paid their fines were being released that afternoon. Desperate, I grabbed a young guy from Henan who was packing up to leave. I shoved a scrap of paper with my office phone number and my crumpled 10-yuan bill into his hand. "Friend, please, go out and make a phone call for me! Tell my girlfriend to come bail me out. I owe you my life!"

That evening, a guard strolled in and barked, "The rest of you, on your feet. You're being transferred to the Zhangmutou detention center."

Pure panic hit me. If they dragged me out of Shenzhen, how the hell would Ah Lan ever find me? On the prison transport, more than fifty of us were paired up and chained together with freezing steel handcuffs. The sirens wailed relentlessly the entire way. The glittering skyscrapers outside the window slowly faded into pitch-black, winding mountain roads. I sat there shivering, praying over and over that the kid from Henan had made that phone call.

I survived another two days and two nights in Zhangmutou. The concrete cell was packed even tighter, the mosquitoes swarmed like tiny bombers, the food was practically inedible, and the guards wouldn't hesitate to crack someone over the head with a baton at the slightest provocation. Just as my brain was starting to completely shut down, on the afternoon of the third day, my name was suddenly barked through the iron door.

I was so overwhelmed with relief my legs literally gave out. I nearly collapsed. Two of my cellmates hauled me back up, their eyes burning with pure desperation. "When you get out," one pleaded, grabbing my shirt, "don't forget to make a phone call for us." I nodded frantically.

Stepping out from behind the final iron gate, the blazing sunlight was so blinding I had to squeeze my eyes shut. When my vision finally cleared, I saw Ah Lan standing in the dirt lot. She had lost a shocking amount of weight in just a few days. The circles under her eyes were dark and bruised, and her hair was a chaotic mess.

She told me later that she nearly went insane while I was missing. She went to my office first, but nobody there knew I'd been arrested; they all assumed I'd just blown off work. She filed a missing persons report with the police, sprinted to every single hospital emergency room in the city, and even went down to the municipal morgue to check the unclaimed bodies, lifting the white sheets one by one with shaking hands. It wasn't until the evening of the third day that a coworker passed a message to her—they had received a weird phone call from a guy with a heavy Henan accent, confirming I was locked up in a detention center. Only then was she able to track down the location, pull the cash together, and rush out to Dongguan.

I was standing there in the blazing sun, barefoot and shaking. My eyes were violently red and swollen, and my dress clothes were torn to shreds, caked in black grime and dried sweat. Ah Lan's face was tracked with endless tears, but the second she saw me, she broke into the most beautiful smile I had ever seen.
